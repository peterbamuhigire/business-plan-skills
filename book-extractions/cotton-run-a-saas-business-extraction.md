# Book Extraction: Ben Cotton — How to Run a SaaS Business

**Source:** Cotton, Ben. *How to Run a SaaS Business: Lessons Learned from a Trio of Billion Dollar Companies* (2019). Author was on the marketing/strategy teams at HubSpot, Indeed.com, and Automation Anywhere through their billion-dollar trajectories.

**Why this matters:** This book distils nine operating rules from three blitzscaled SaaS companies. For business plans that aim beyond a lifestyle SaaS (i.e. plans targeting $10M–$100M ARR), it codifies the unit economics, organisational frameworks, and growth disciplines required to translate "we have ARR" into "we have a runnable, fundable, scalable SaaS company." Cotton's contribution complements Walling (bootstrap) and Mersch (CFO finance) by sitting in the middle: the marketing/operating frameworks (Rule of 40, Rule of 3 and 10, LTV:CAC, NPS, MSPOT, S-curves, three horizons) that all enterprise SaaS plans should adopt.

---

## 1. Rule of 40 — the master health check

**Annual revenue growth rate % + profit margin % ≥ 40%.**

Examples:
- 40% growth + 0% profit = 40 ✓
- 20% growth + 20% profit = 40 ✓
- 60% growth + (-20%) profit = 40 ✓

**Why it works:** investors don't reward growth at any cost, and they don't reward profit at the cost of growth. The Rule of 40 simultaneously expresses both. Public-market SaaS (Salesforce, Workday, ServiceNow, HubSpot) live or die by this metric. Private SaaS valuations now use it explicitly.

**Plan implication:** Section 10 (Financial Projections) must include a Rule of 40 line for each projected year. A plan projecting 25% growth and -25% margin (= 0) signals investor red flag regardless of headline ARR.

## 2. Rule of 3 and 10 — everything breaks when you triple

Cotton's "everything-breaks" cadence: at headcount milestones of **3, 10, 30, 100, 300, 1,000** the operating model fundamentally breaks. Required adaptations:
- New processes
- New people (sometimes replacement of founding generation)
- New playbooks
- New management layers

**Plan implication:** Section 09 (Management Team) and Section 13 (Implementation Timeline) must show org-design evolution tied to ARR / headcount milestones, not as one snapshot. The Section 09 org chart needs three flavours: today, at next milestone, at +1 milestone.

## 3. LTV:CAC ≥ 3:1 (the unit-economics gate)

**LTV** = monthly subscription value × average customer lifetime (months) × gross margin %
**CAC** = (S&M + product-maintenance attributable to acquisition) ÷ new customers acquired
**Ratio target:** ≥ 3:1 (higher is better; below means the business is destroying capital).

Cotton emphasises three retention levers that improve LTV (the denominator of the ratio when you flip it):
1. Accurate prospect qualification during sales
2. Excellent account management
3. Excellent support and customer success

**Plan implication:** Section 10 must show LTV, CAC, LTV:CAC, and CAC payback period. Section 07 must show how the qualification, AM, and CS organisations enforce LTV.

## 4. Annual churn < 7%

Cotton's most repeated rule. Monthly vs annual churn arithmetic is critical:
- 5% **annual** churn = 0.42% monthly
- 5% **monthly** churn = 46% **annual** churn. (Start January with 100 customers; finish December with 54.)

**Plan implication:** business plans must state the period of churn measurement explicitly, distinguish gross vs net revenue churn (NRR), and target:
- **SMB SaaS**: gross monthly churn 1–3%, gross annual churn 7–12% acceptable
- **Mid-market SaaS**: gross annual churn 5–10%
- **Enterprise SaaS**: gross annual churn 3–7%, with NRR ≥ 110%

"Retention trumps acquisition." A leaky bucket cannot be filled.

## 5. Net Promoter Score — the leading retention indicator

NPS = % promoters (9–10) − % detractors (0–6). Scale -100 to +100. Cotton's framing: NPS is the early-warning system for churn. A customer scoring 0–6 will churn within 12 months.

**Plan implication:** Section 08 (Operations) should include an NPS measurement cadence (quarterly), benchmark against competitors, and a closing-the-loop process where detractors trigger CS intervention.

## 6. Discounting process — never react, always sell value

HubSpot's five-gate discount process (no discount discussion until the first four are checked):
1. Prospect agrees challenge needs solving NOW.
2. Prospect agrees the company solves this challenge (not a competitor).
3. Prospect shares desired start date and procurement process.
4. Key decision-maker is in the sales process.
5. **Only now** can discount be discussed.

Authorisation matrix: rep can give 5%, manager 10%, director 10%+. Use **gives-to-gets** (Saleshacker): every concession traded for a multi-year contract, prepayment, expanded scope, case-study consent, or referral introduction.

## 7. Strategic investment frameworks

Cotton uses two complementary frameworks for portfolio bets:
- **S-Curves** — every product follows growth → maturity → decline. Invest in your next S-curve before the current one tops out.
- **McKinsey Three Horizons** — Horizon 1 (core, current profits), Horizon 2 (emerging, near-term substantial profits), Horizon 3 (R&D, pilots, far-future bets).

**Plan implication:** Section 03 (Products/Services) and Section 13 (Timeline) must show the current S-curve position of each product line and the planned H2/H3 bets — not a single product roadmap.

## 8. Freemium as CAC-reduction strategy

HubSpot reduced CAC dramatically by launching free CRM, free Marketing Hub, free Sales Hub tiers. The dynamics:
- Free users self-serve (no sales touch) — slashes CAC.
- Free users upgrade "touchlessly" → low-touch upsell.
- Those who do need sales need fewer touches than cold leads.
- Free is a brand-and-distribution lever, not a charity.

**Caution:** freemium only works if (a) the free tier delivers genuine value, (b) the paid tier has obvious upgrade triggers, (c) infrastructure cost of free users is low, (d) the company has the brand reach to attract millions of free users.

**Plan implication:** if Section 07 proposes freemium, it must justify all four conditions and show free-to-paid conversion projections.

## 9. Hire for stage fit, not pedigree

Cotton's most counter-intuitive rule. A VP from Salesforce at a 100-person startup will usually fail because: (a) they need huge support staff they no longer have, (b) they manage rather than do, (c) they optimise for political wins that 100-person companies don't have.

**Plan implication:** Section 09 must justify each senior hire by referring to the **stage the company is at when the hire begins**, not the stage the hire came from.

## 10. Avoid overeating — the MSPOT discipline

HubSpot's MSPOT document:
- **M**ission — rarely changes
- **S**trategy — annually changes
- **P**rojects — 4–5 big initiatives this year
- **O**missions — explicitly funded NOT to do
- **T**racking — what numbers show we're on track

Brian Halligan: "Companies are more likely to die of indigestion than starvation." The Omissions section is the most important — it's how the organisation limits the CEO's appetite.

**Plan implication:** the engine's `meta-quarterly-gameplan` skill should adopt MSPOT verbatim as the quarterly artefact. Every plan should produce an MSPOT for Year 1 and refresh it annually.

## 11. SaaS Business Model — the LTV:CAC core

Cotton's framing: SaaS is **predictable recurring revenue** + **low marginal cost of additional units** + **customer-at-the-heart** (because subscription forces continual earning of loyalty).

Marc Benioff's rule from *Behind the Cloud*: in early SaaS, measure on revenue not profitability. Pour fuel on the fire if LTV:CAC is healthy and market is untapped. But this only applies to genuinely venture-class markets — most African SaaS should target Rule-of-40 discipline from year 2 onward.

## 12. Brand Building — the under-measured lever

Cotton (a marketer) makes the case that B2B SaaS has under-invested in brand because it's hard to measure. Yet "meaningful brands" outperform the stock market by 206%. Brand creates pricing power, sales velocity, and recruiting advantage.

**Plan implication:** Section 07 marketing budget should include explicit brand-building line (10–25% of marketing spend), separate from performance / acquisition.

## 13. Marketing Arbitrage and the Sales-and-Marketing Engine

- Identify a channel where customer acquisition cost is **temporarily** below the LTV-justified ceiling.
- Pour budget into that arbitrage until it closes.
- Have at least 3 acquisition channels running so channel concentration doesn't kill you.
- Never build a sales team with a single rep — pair reps with a peer so you can isolate "is it the rep or is it the model?"

## 14. Churn is the Quiet SaaS Killer

Cotton dedicates an entire essay to this. Beyond gross churn, plans must track:
- **Net Revenue Retention (NRR)** — best-in-class >120%, healthy >110%, acceptable >100%
- **Customer Health Score** — composite (product usage, NPS, support tickets, payment history, executive sponsor health)
- **Logo churn vs revenue churn** — losing 10% of small customers ≠ losing 10% of revenue
- **Churn cohorts by acquisition source** — discount-acquired customers churn 2-3× faster

## 15. Hardening Rules for the Business-Plan Engine

- Section 10 must include: Rule of 40, LTV, CAC, LTV:CAC, CAC payback, gross churn, net retention, NPS as benchmarked KPIs.
- Section 07 must justify discounting through a defined process (HubSpot 5-gate or equivalent).
- Section 09 must show org evolution tied to Rule of 3 and 10 milestones.
- Section 13 must show S-curve / Three-Horizons portfolio thinking.
- MSPOT (or equivalent Mission/Strategy/Projects/Omissions/Tracking artefact) becomes the annual operating cadence.
- Freemium claims require the four-condition justification (genuine free value, upgrade triggers, low infra cost, brand reach).
- Brand budget line item separate from performance marketing.
- Every senior hire justified by "fit for stage," not pedigree.

## 16. Uganda / East Africa / Africa Application Notes

- Rule of 40 must be benchmarked locally — African SaaS gross margins are slightly lower (FX, infra cost, payments friction). Plan for 70–80% gross margin instead of the global SaaS 80–90% until scale is achieved.
- Annual churn benchmarks are different in African markets due to payment-failure churn (involuntary churn from card declines, network failure). Set involuntary-churn target separately (<2% annual) and invest in payment-retry / dunning automation early.
- NPS culture is weaker — closing-the-loop on NPS is a real competitive differentiator because most local incumbents don't do it.
- Brand-building is disproportionately powerful in African B2B SaaS because category awareness is low; the first credible local-language brand in a sector often takes 40%+ category share.
- MSPOT discipline is a high-leverage import for African founders who tend to under-invest in explicit Omissions (the "no" list).
- Stage-fit hiring is hard in markets with shallow senior-talent pools — plans often need to include investment in **building** senior operators (executive coaching, secondments, MBA fellowships) rather than hiring them in finished form.
