## Operating thesis

Write for a particular reader who has a reason to care and a decision to make. “Human” comes from attention, judgement, accurate detail, and a recognisable point of view—not from slang, invented anecdotes, random quirks, or deliberately broken grammar.

## The five-pass method

1. **Purpose and reader.** Name the reader, situation, knowledge level, emotional temperature, and next action. State the single job of the piece.
2. **Genre and spine.** Choose the form that fits the job. Make headings describe decisions or content, not merely topics. Put the governing point where this reader needs it.
3. **Meaning and evidence.** Separate fact, interpretation, recommendation, assumption, and open question. Put proof beside the claim. Remove any claim that cannot be supported.
4. **Sentence and paragraph craft.** Prefer concrete nouns, strong verbs, exact modifiers, natural collocations, and active voice where the actor matters. Give each paragraph one centre of gravity. Vary sentence length for meaning, not decoration.
5. **Proof and reader test.** Check grammar, spelling, names, dates, figures, links, headings, and consistency. Read aloud once and skim only the headings once. Repair every stumble, false promise, and unexplained term.

Do not merge the substantive revision pass with the mechanical proof pass. A polished sentence may still belong in the wrong paragraph, and a well-argued document may still contain a damaging typo.

## Language decisions

- Use British English by default, while retaining accepted local names, institutional terms, currencies, and expressions.
- Prefer the ordinary word when it carries the meaning: *use* over *utilise*, *start* over *commence*, *help* over *facilitate*, *about* over *with regard to*.
- Use an advanced or technical word only after checking its meaning, connotation, collocation, and register. A word learned from a list is not automatically a word suited to a sentence.
- Check the error families that repeatedly damage otherwise strong prose: subject–verb agreement, articles, tense and aspect, pronoun reference, relative clauses, prepositions, parallelism, misplaced modifiers, punctuation, spelling, homophones, fragments, and run-on sentences.
- Do not turn grammar into a hunt for isolated rules. First ask what the sentence means, who acts, what time is intended, and what relationship the words must express.
- Avoid vague intensifiers and prestige adjectives unless evidence earns them: *very, really, significant, substantial, world-class, leading, innovative, seamless, robust*. Replace them with a fact, a limit, a comparison, or a qualified judgement.
- Avoid stock connectors when the logic is already clear. Use *however*, *therefore*, and *for example* when they show a real relationship, not as paragraph decoration.
- Keep lists grammatically parallel. Do not mix nouns, infinitives, and full clauses without a reason.

## Paragraph and argument craft

A strong paragraph normally has a point, support, and a consequence or link. It does not have a mandatory sentence count. Change paragraph length when the thought changes; do not create a wall of uniform blocks.

For claims that matter, use this private test: **claim → evidence → warrant → countercase → implication**. The final prose need not display those labels, but the writer must know what connects the evidence to the conclusion and what could make the conclusion fail.

For persuasive or analytical work, let the reader feel the movement of the thought: what is happening, why it matters here, what is difficult about it, what you recommend, and what the recommendation costs or requires. A human voice often appears in the honest qualification, the chosen example, or the sentence that names the trade-off.

## Register matrix

| Output | Primary job | Voice and grammar | Particular danger |
|---|---|---|---|
| App message or microcopy | Help a person complete the next step | Brief, calm, literal, respectful; say what happened and what to do | “Something went wrong” with no recovery; fake cheer; labels written as paragraphs |
| Website page | Orient, answer, prove, and invite action | Reader-first, specific, scannable; headings carry meaning; claims sit near proof | Keyword stuffing, inflated promises, generic welcome copy, identical CTA text |
| Business plan | Let a lender, owner, or investor judge a proposition | Answer first; evidence, assumptions, risks, and trade-offs visible | Beautiful certainty hiding weak numbers or a generic founder story |
| Proposal or bid | Earn evaluator confidence within the ToR | Direct, compliant, tailored, measurable; benefits tied to deliverables and proof | Copy-pasted methodology, unearned capability claims, buried compliance gaps |
| Social post | Earn attention and a useful response in a native channel | Conversational but controlled; one idea, one audience, one action; use local texture when real | Content-factory cadence, forced humour, hashtag piles, empty inspiration |
| Research report | Help a reader understand what the evidence supports | Precise, sourced, transparent about limits and inference | Citation laundering, false balance, “key findings” without judgement |
| Political essay or speech | Make a serious public argument | Distinct thesis, institutional diagnosis, moral and public-interest stakes, fair countercase | Slogans, theatrical certainty, allegations without evidence, doctrine hidden in vague prose |
| SRS or technical document | Make behaviour and decisions buildable and testable | Exact terminology, explicit actors, states, constraints, and test oracles | “User-friendly”, “seamless”, or “reliable” without a measurable requirement |

## Anti-slop tests

Before release, ask:

- Could this paragraph belong to ten unrelated organisations? If yes, add the real actor, place, decision, constraint, example, or evidence—or cut it.
- Is the first sentence doing useful work, or merely announcing that the document will discuss something?
- Does the piece contain a judgement, selection, or implication that a competent human author actually chose?
- Are sentence openings, paragraph shapes, and transitions mechanically repeated?
- Have polished adjectives hidden a missing number, source, owner, mechanism, or exception?
- Does the conclusion earn its place, or does it simply repeat “in conclusion” and promise a better future?
- Would an intelligent reader enjoy the movement of the prose, or merely tolerate it?

Do not ban a word merely because a model often uses it. Ban default usage that adds no meaning. Do not add mistakes to simulate humanity. Edit towards clarity, particularity, rhythm, and honest judgement.

## Original before-and-after patterns

| Weak pattern | Better direction |
|---|---|
| “In today’s fast-paced world, businesses must leverage innovative solutions.” | Name the pressure, the business, the solution, and the decision it changes. |
| “We offer world-class services tailored to your needs.” | State the service, the client situation it addresses, the boundary of the work, and the next step. |
| “Your request has been submitted successfully.” | “Request sent. We’ll email the reference number within 10 minutes.” |
| “The project will enhance stakeholder engagement.” | “The district team will hold a monthly review with traders and publish the actions agreed.” |
| “The report explores several important issues.” | “The report compares the two procurement options and recommends the one that fits the six-month budget.” |

When this reference is applied, record: output type; reader and purpose; source/proof status; terms checked; substantive revision completed; read-aloud/proof status; unresolved language or evidence gaps; reviewer and date. A language pass cannot certify a weak proposition, unverified fact, or missing human approval.
