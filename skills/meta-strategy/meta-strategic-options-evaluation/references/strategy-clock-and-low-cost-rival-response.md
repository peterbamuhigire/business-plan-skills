# Strategy clock and low-cost-rival response

Parent: [Strategic Options Evaluation](../SKILL.md)

The strategy clock is Bowman and Faulkner's model, and the low-cost-rival response tree is Kumar's; both are used here as presented in Johnson, G., Whittington, R., Scholes, K., Angwin, D. and Regnér, P. (2017) *Exploring Strategy*, 11th edn, Pearson, chapter 7. D'Aveni's value lines come from the same chapter. The procedures and examples below are paraphrased and localised.

## 1. Strategy clock zones

Axes: **price** (horizontal) against **perceived benefits** (vertical). Prices are visible from outside where costs are not, so rivals can be plotted without their accounts.

| Clock position | Zone | Logic | Condition for success |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| About 7 o'clock | No-frills | Low price, low benefit for price-sensitive segments | Lowest cost base in the segment |
| About 9 o'clock | Low price | Standard benefit at a lower price | A real cost advantage; otherwise rivals follow the price down |
| About 10 to 11 o'clock | Hybrid | Higher benefit than low-price players at a lower price than differentiators | A named mechanism (scale, process, technology) that lowers cost while benefit rises |
| About 12 o'clock | Differentiation without premium | Higher benefit at market price to gain share | Benefit customers notice immediately; cost discipline elsewhere |
| About 1 o'clock | Differentiation with premium | Higher benefit, higher price | Premium stays below the value customers perceive |
| About 2 o'clock | Focused differentiation | Very high benefit at a high price for a narrow segment | Segment large enough and willing to pay |
| 3 to 6 o'clock | Non-competitive | Price rises without benefit, or benefit falls at the same price | Fails unless customers are locked in |

## 2. Plotting procedure

1. Define the segment and name three to six rivals, including informal or substitute offers.
2. Survey target customers on perceived benefits (for example 1 to 10) for each offer on the critical success factors. Use a ranking task rather than "what would you pay?".
3. Build a price index (segment average = 100) from observed shelf, menu or tender prices.
4. Plot each player; label its zone.
5. Choose a target position and a three-year path. Firms move around the clock: enter at 12 o'clock to gain trial, move to 1 o'clock once proof accumulates, keep a hybrid line to defend against imports.
6. Check the cost base supports the position. Being the second-lowest-cost player at a low-price position is a disadvantage against the lowest-cost player.

Value-line check (D'Aveni): when a rival makes an aggressive price or quality move, the market's value line shifts. A firm left behind the line must move or lose share. Treat positions as trajectories, not fixed points.

## 3. Responding to a low-cost entrant

Walk the tree in order and record the answer and evidence at each step.

1. **Will the entrant take our present or future customers?** Assess by segment. If no, monitor with a named indicator and review date.
2. **If yes, do not start a price war by default.** Incumbents can usually follow a price cut, but a price war damages both and often favours the lower-cost player.
3. **Are enough customers willing to pay for extra benefits?** If yes, intensify differentiation and restructure to cut the cost of delivering those benefits.
4. **If not, would a low-cost unit of our own share costs with the core?** If yes, launch a separately branded fighter offer that shares plant, distribution or systems. If no, choose between becoming a solutions provider (bundle service around the product), becoming a low-cost player outright, merging, or accepting a smaller, more profitable business.

Localised illustration (fictional): a Kampala water bottler facing cheaper regional imports finds the imports target small retail shops, not premium hotels. It keeps hotel prices, adds a dispenser-and-delivery subscription for offices, and considers an economy brand only because it can share bottling capacity. Sales-conversation probe: "If a cheaper competitor entered tomorrow, which of your customers would it take first?"

## 4. Link to generic strategies

The clock refines Porter's generic strategies (see `06-competitive-analysis/references/competitive-strategy-tools.md`). Cost leadership needs the lowest cost, not merely a low price. Differentiation needs the extra cost to stay below the price premium. Focus needs distinct segment needs and a segment value chain broad rivals find hard to copy.
