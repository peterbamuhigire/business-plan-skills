# Exit readiness and sale preparation

Parent: [Strategic Optionality & Exit Planning](../SKILL.md)

When to read: when an owner-managed business, family firm or growth company is preparing a sale, buy-out, investor exit or succession, or when an equity plan must show how investors will get their money back.

This is the engine's own exit programme. It is organised by what the owner must do and decide, in order: test whether the business is worth selling, score readiness, run a dated preparation programme, choose a route and buyers, write the sales memorandum, plan the negotiation and manage deferred-payment risk. The idea of planning a start-up with its eventual sale in mind, and the value questions, preparation habits and earn-out cautions behind it, are discussed in Barrow, C. (2009) *Get Backed, Get Big, Get Bought*, Capstone. The scorecard, timeline, route table, checklists and examples below are this engine's. Barrow wrote for the UK in 2009; his legal, tax, listing and multiple references are not used. **Valuation figures go to `meta-valuation`. Disposal tax, earn-out tax, share classes and statutory treatment go to the Chwezi finance doctrine and a qualified adviser.** This file never states a tax rule or a market multiple as fact.

## 1. Is there a saleable business?

Test early whether the business will be worth more than the owner's salary to someone else. Ask four questions and score each Weak, Adequate or Strong with evidence:

| Question | Strong evidence |
|---|---|
| Is the problem big enough to pay for? | Customers already spend effort or money on it |
| Can the model be repeated without the founder? | A written operating system that works in a second outlet, route or team |
| Is there enough money to move before imitators? | Funded plan for the next stage |
| Does something slow imitators? | Licence, brand, contracts, distribution reach, practices rivals cannot copy |

A lifestyle business whose owner does not intend to sell should say so and skip this file.

## 2. Readiness scorecard

Mark each area Red (absent), Amber (partial) or Green (in place), with evidence.

| Area | Green condition | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Results trend | Three consecutive years of rising revenue and profit with steady margins; any spike or dip explained | Management accounts, filed statements |
| Clean books | No personal costs or assets in the company; informal pay regularised; related-party dealings documented | Ledger review, payroll records, finance-doctrine review |
| Audit | Statements audited by a firm buyers and lenders recognise | Audit reports |
| Management depth | The business runs for a month without the founder | Organisation chart, delegation records |
| Customer concentration | No customer whose loss would erase a year's profit | Customer revenue analysis |
| Contracts and rights | Key contracts and leases transferable or free of change-of-control traps; IP and licences held by the company | Contract register |
| Compliance | Tax filings current, licences valid, disputes disclosed | Compliance register (verify via finance doctrine and `country-context/`) |
| Forward plan | Credible five-year plan and projections | Plan and model |
| Peer comparison | Ratios compared with peers; own credit report read as a buyer would read it | Benchmark file |
| Data room | Documents indexed and ready | Index (see `meta-due-diligence`) |

A Red in results trend, clean books or management depth makes a near-term sale conditional.

## 3. Preparation timeline

| Horizon | Actions |
|---|---|
| Three to five years out | Write an exit intention even without a sale plan; build management depth; reduce concentration; move to a recognised auditor; keep a five-year plan |
| One to three years out | Remove blemishes (loss years, bad debts, staff disputes, credit downgrades); make three years' results consistent; clean the books; compare with peers; build the data room |
| Six to twelve months out | Appoint advisers; list buyers; draft the sales memorandum; set minimum acceptable terms; watch market timing |
| During the deal | Talk to several buyers; negotiate; manage due diligence; agree warranties, retentions and any deferred payment with advisers |

Market mood can move value more than a year of the firm's own growth; sell in a rising market where possible and avoid opening talks in a falling one unless forced.

## 4. Routes and buyers

Buyers often come from the value chain: competitors, customers, suppliers or the firm's managers. Expect to approach many prospects; the easiest buyer is not always the best price.

| Route | Fits when | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| Sale to a strategic buyer | The buyer gains customers, capability, licences or reach | Rapid integration may change the business |
| Sale to a financial buyer or fund | Stable cash generation and management in place | Debt-funded structure and performance conditions |
| Management buy-out | Managers can run it and raise finance | Hard when the owner takes every decision; prove management first |
| Buy-in, or buy-in with buy-out | An outside manager brings capability and backing | Culture fit |
| Family succession | A capable, willing successor exists | Write payment terms down; a clean break usually works best |
| Employee ownership | Continuity is valued | May not maximise proceeds |
| Stock-exchange listing | Rare for SMEs; a funding event more than an exit | Verify current exchange and capital-markets rules through the Digital Research engine before mentioning it |

Equity-plan slot: "Likely acquirers include [named firms or firm types], which bought [comparable businesses] in [years]. We are building [the asset they value: customer base, licence, brand, capability] to fit that interest." Names and deals must be verified.

## 5. Sales memorandum

Include: what the business does, customers, markets, three-year results (with charts); the improvement a better-resourced owner could achieve; a tone informative rather than crowded with numbers. Withhold customer and supplier names and prices until a buyer is serious and bound by confidentiality. Tailor to the buyer type (a competitor needs less industry background). Management drafts, the corporate-finance adviser polishes, the design engine formats. All figures come from audited or reviewed accounts.

Opener slot: "[Company] is a [descriptor] business serving [customers] in [markets]. Over three years revenue grew from [x] to [y] with gross margin steady at [z]%. A new owner with [capability] could [growth lever]."

## 6. Negotiation variables

Open ambitiously; a price rarely rises once a buyer has sought internal approval. Negotiate several variables together:

| Variable | Seller's question |
|---|---|
| Price and payment form | Cash, shares or deferred? |
| Timing | When is each part paid? |
| Contingent element | What share depends on which measure, for how long? |
| Warranties and indemnities | What is warranted, capped and held back, for how long? |
| Service arrangements | Will the founder or managers stay, on what authority? |
| Completion adjustments | How are stock, receivables and debt treated? |
| Control during any deferred period | Who decides? |

Both sides should leave believing the deal is fair. Fix the walk-away point before talks.

## 7. Deferred and contingent payment safeguards

Deferring part of the price against future targets creates risks: serving two masters, losing control of cash and staff, the buyer loading central costs onto the measured unit, conflicting time horizons and complex tax timing. Safeguards:

1. Measure on gross profit, cash flow or new business, not a net profit the buyer can burden with charges.
2. Agree the founder's authority, budget and staff in writing for the period.
3. Escrow or ring-fence the deferred cash.
4. Set realistic targets and a dispute route.
5. Protect staff incentives.
6. Take tax advice through the finance doctrine and an adviser before agreeing the structure.
7. Otherwise prefer a lower, cleaner price.

**Illustrative example.** A Jinja packaging firm (family-owned, one customer at 45% of revenue) scores Red on customer concentration and management depth. The plan: two years to bring the top customer below a third of revenue, appoint a general manager, move to an audited statement, then approach three converters and a regional distributor with a sales memorandum, seeking cash on completion with a small retention.
