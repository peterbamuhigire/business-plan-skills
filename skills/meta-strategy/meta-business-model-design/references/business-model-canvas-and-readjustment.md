# Six-element canvas and readjustment levers

Parent: [Business Model Design and Scoring](../SKILL.md)

The six elements, green and red lights, the option-per-element canvas and the four readjustment levers follow Debelak, D. (2006) *Business Models Made Easy*, Entrepreneur Press, chapters 1, 7 and 8, paraphrased. The East African prompts and templates are this engine's own.

## 1. Write the story first

- **Three-sentence story:** "[Company] targets [customer group defined by two qualities]. [Offer] gives them [primary benefit]. They need it because [evidence of the need: what they struggle with or already spend]."
- **Concept line (40 words or fewer):** "We [do what] for [whom] so they [result], earning revenue from [revenue source]."
- Test both on someone outside the business. If they misread the concept, rewrite before modelling. Describe the model as similar to a known business, then state the difference.

## 2. Six elements with green and red lights

| # | Element | Favourable condition | Light |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Acquire high-value customers | Customers are easy to locate, accept a profitable price and try the offer with little marketing | Green: must be present |
| 2 | Offer significant value | A competitive advantage customers notice at once | Green: must be present |
| 3 | Deliver with high margins | Quality delivery with few chances for error | Green: must be present |
| 4 | Provide customer satisfaction | Service, training or installation provided efficiently, often by partners | Red: where models break |
| 5 | Maintain market position | A protected position or a steady stream of new offers | Red: where models break |
| 6 | Fund the business | Investment reasonable for the market size and risk, at start and ongoing | Red: where models break |

Red flags for element 5: two or three customers buy most of the output; potential competitors control distribution; fast technology change with risky development; well-funded rivals able to move in quickly.

Red flags for element 6 (Debelak's 2006 heuristics; verify for the sector): weak early return on investment; each increase in output needs large new investment; less than half the investment goes to revenue-producing areas (sales and production); investment made before sales commitments; poor industry-wide profitability. Plan from the investment the most efficient model needs, not from a fear of raising money; over-bootstrapped models often leave no room for profit.

Pricing stance: Debelak suggests starting plan pricing about 10 to 15% above competitors and then deciding what features or services justify it. Price reinforces a premium offer; it cannot create one. Test perceived value by asking customers to rank the offer among five or six comparable offers, not by asking what they would pay.

East African prompts for the canvas (checks, not facts): mobile-money collection and settlement design; informal wholesale and duka networks (for example Kikuubo wholesalers); upcountry after-sales reach; agent networks as entry points; SACCO, angel, grant and DFI funding routes; exchange-rate exposure on imported inputs.

## 4. Four readjustment levers

Change as few levers as possible; each change ripples into other factors.

| Lever | Use when | Typical move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Change target customers | Customers are hard to find, do not spend freely, do not prioritise the offer, or need too much support | Move to a group that already looks for the offer. A customer group's behaviour cannot be changed by the plan. |
| 2. Change value to customers | The offer is not important or not clearly better | Add features or services, become the complete solution, strengthen the advantage using customer ranking input |
| 3. Change sales and distribution | Customers are hard to locate, support-heavy, or buy slowly | Hard to find: sell through firms that already reach them. Support-heavy: dealer or franchise network. Unimportant alone: sell as part of a larger solution. Slow decision: attach to a more important purchase. |
| 4. Change how it is produced | Margins or investment fail | Core team plus contractors per job; outsource high-cost operations; vendor-funded tooling recovered per unit |

Pattern worth knowing (paraphrased from Debelak's EC Outlook case): a software firm failed selling software to large firms (sale too complex) and services to small firms (too much selling per unit of revenue), then succeeded selling large firms a service that converted data to and from their small suppliers. The fix was a different customer-value-channel combination, not more promotion.
