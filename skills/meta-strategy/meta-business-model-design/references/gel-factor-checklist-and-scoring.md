# Business-model viability scorecard (GEL-derived)

Parent: [Business Model Design and Scoring](../SKILL.md)

When to read: at steps 4 to 6 of the parent skill, before any plan section is drafted, and whenever a model is redesigned.

This scorecard is the engine's own test of whether a business model can work before money is committed. It asks four questions in the order a lender or investor would: can we reach buyers, will they buy readily, does each sale pay, and can we keep it going? It builds on the idea in Debelak, D. (2006) *Business Models Made Easy*, Entrepreneur Press, that a model succeeds when it has great customers, easy sales and a long life ("GEL"), each tested through several factors with compensating tactics for weak ones. The questions, grouping, wording, thresholds, register and examples here are this engine's. Thresholds are planning rules of thumb; verify sector norms through the Digital Research engine and the Chwezi finance doctrine.

## 1. The eighteen checks

Rate each check **Strong**, **Middling** or **Weak** against named competitors and cite evidence (records, interviews, tests, dated sources).

**Question R — Can we reach enough good buyers?**

| ID | Check | Strong looks like |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | How many buyers exist in the served market | Enough to meet the plan at a realistic share |
| R2 | How easily they can be found and reached | Clear gathering places, lists, associations or channels |
| R3 | Their willingness to spend on this category | They already spend freely on it |
| R4 | Value of a typical sale | High enough to pay for the effort of winning it |
| R5 | Frequency of repeat purchase | Many repeat purchases per customer |
| R6 | After-sale support each customer needs | Little |

**Question B — Will they buy readily?**

| ID | Check | Strong looks like |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | Priority of the purchase to the buyer | Among their top concerns now |
| B2 | Visible advantage over alternatives | Large and noticed at once |
| B3 | Perceived value for the price | Clearly above the price |
| B4 | Ways to start buying (trial, small first order, agent, mobile-money code) | Many and easy |
| B5 | Selling effort needed per sale | Little |
| B6 | Promotion needed to create demand | Little |

**Question P — Does each sale pay?**

| ID | Check | Strong looks like |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | Gross margin per sale | High for the sector |
| P2 | Add-on and upgrade potential | Substantial |
| P3 | Continuing cost of serving each customer | Low |

**Question S — Can we sustain it?**

| ID | Check | Strong looks like |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Investment needed to start | Low relative to the opportunity |
| S2 | Spending needed to hold position against rivals | Low |
| S3 | Spending needed to stay current (technology, design, regulation) | Low |

## 2. When a check is a real concern, and what can compensate

| ID | Warning signs | Compensating moves to consider |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | Low price with little repeat or add-on; unfamiliar category | Sell where buyers already go; join a network serving the same group; confirm other firms already serve this group profitably |
| R2 | No associations, lists, events or specialist channels | Bundle into a bigger purchase; customer referrals; joint marketing; classes or clubs; in East Africa, associations, churches, SACCOs, WhatsApp groups, trade fairs and market-day circuits |
| R3 | Practical, low-priority or one-off purchase | Add an emotional reason; sell the complete solution; make paying easier (trial, instalments, monthly fee); focus on larger buyers; time offers to harvest, school-fee and pay-day cycles |
| R4 | Mid-size tickets needing full selling effort | Package a complete solution; bring buyers to demonstrations; commission-only agents; broaden the range |
| R5 | Small tickets, costly service, low priority | Raise the purchase's importance; add worry-removing services; cheaper selling routes; wider territory; larger buyers |
| R6 | Buyer purchases a result; new, customised or integrated product | Intuitive design; set expectations in marketing; choose customers; train at your premises; service-capable dealers; ask recent customers why they bought and what they expected |
| B1 | Sold through distributors; delayable; buyer unsure how to buy | Emotional context; timed promotions; expert endorsement; target the segment where it matters most; look for evidence buyers already spend effort on workarounds |
| B2 | Same claim as everyone ("we save you money") | Narrow to where the advantage is distinct; tests, documented savings, testimonials; become the market's meeting point (events, classes, association roles). Against much larger rivals the advantage must be large and obvious |
| B3 | Optional purchase, many alternatives, unproven product | Add high-value elements; drop low-value features; cut cost; rethink the concept; test value by ranking variants, not by asking "what would you pay?" |
| B4 | Competitive or optional purchase with a short buying window | Go into the buyer's setting; partnerships; follow-up of prospects; easy first steps; cross-promotion; a price ladder so every visitor can buy something |
| B5 | Complex sale needing demonstration or reassurance | Own the full result; sell a service rather than a purchase; trained dealers; higher commission on hard sales; use recognised experts |
| B6 | Hard-to-find buyers, unpredictable timing, low ticket, new category | Recurring revenue; add items buyers already need; attract interest early with teaching content; show the result, not the product; one striking feature |
| P1 | Price-matching market; many intermediaries | Add real value; remove a major cost; shorten distribution; find a group that values it more; lower overheads |
| P2 | Rare purchases, little future contact | Consumable component; complementary own-label items; pair one-off and ongoing services; focus on high-yield buyers |
| P3 | Client staff turnover, integrations, fast change, unskilled users | Set expectations; price ongoing training and service openly; planned upgrades; self-help material (agencies: client-contact turnover is a running cost) |
| S1 | Untested concept, short product life, thin resources, a large rival | Partner with an established player; pre-sell to three or four major buyers before investing; win a small segment fast; monthly fees; outsource costly operations; share premises |
| S2 | Look-alike products, rivals with large budgets, no channel edge | One noticeable benefit tied to an important desire; compete only where different; partnerships; unconventional promotion; retreat to an affordable segment |
| S3 | Commodity category; fast technical change | Close ties to one segment; a customer advisory group meeting a few times a year; outside designers on royalty; spend on product before administration |

Recurring-revenue patterns worth testing for R5 and P2: consumables, service contracts, a service instead of a product, monthly spreading of cost (lease, maintenance plan), usage rewards, trade-in guarantees.

## 3. Compensating-move register

| Check ID | Rating | Move | Expected strength (1–3) | Cost and owner | Adopted? |
|---|---|---|---:|---|---|

Engine rules:

- A **Weak** check needs at least one move of strength 3; a **Middling** check may need a smaller move or none.
- Generate several moves per weak check, leave it for a few days, add more, then choose with input from three or four customers.
- Adopt **at most three** moves in total; a small business cannot run more well.
- A compensating move is always costlier and weaker than fixing the model itself; if more than three are needed, redesign the model.

## 4. Verdict

Label each check: **Advantage** (strong and better than rivals), **Par**, **Repaired** (weak but covered by a strength-3 move) or **Open concern** (weak and not covered).

The model **passes** only if all three gates hold:

1. **Must be advantages:** R2 (reachability), B3 (value for price) and P1 (margin).
2. **Must not be open concerns:** R3 (willingness to spend), R6 (after-sale support) and S2 (cost of holding position).
3. **Balance:** advantages outnumber open concerns by at least three to one across all eighteen checks.

Expect a sound model to lead clearly in about half its groups and hold par in the rest; a model that leads everywhere is rare and should be re-examined for optimism. If any gate fails, adjust the fewest levers (see the canvas and readjustment reference) and re-score. Re-score after a few days with fresh eyes, and at least every six months for an operating business.

## 6. Comparing alternative models

Score each candidate model in adjacent columns on the same evidence. Prefer the model that passes with the fewest compensating moves and the lowest funding need relative to market size; where two pass, prefer the one whose gate checks rest on stronger evidence.

**Illustrative example.** A Gulu agro-input shop compares (A) walk-in retail, (B) retail plus a village-agent network paid by commission, and (C) pre-season input packs sold through farmer groups with mobile-money instalments. A fails R2 and P1; B passes with one strength-3 move (agent training); C passes with no moves but needs working capital for pre-season stock, which goes to the finance model.

## 7. Margin arithmetic (feeds P1)

Sales needed for a target gross profit = target ÷ gross-margin %. At 40% margin, sales of 100 give 40; at 30%, about 133 of sales are needed for the same 40, and after selling costs the lower-margin firm earns less despite selling more. Lower prices are sustainable only from lower costs. Margin definitions and sector norms go to the Chwezi finance doctrine.
