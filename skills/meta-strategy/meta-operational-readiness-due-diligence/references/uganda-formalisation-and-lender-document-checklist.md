# Uganda formalisation and lender-document checklist

Parent: [Operational Readiness Due Diligence](../SKILL.md).

When to read: when a Ugandan business plan must show that the business is (or
will become) formally registered, tax-compliant and ready for a lender's or
funder's document request, or when a sole trader must decide whether to
incorporate. This is a procedure and checklist. Every fee, rate, threshold,
processing time, penalty and programme amount is volatile: verify it against
the current primary source (URSB, URA, NSSF, KCCA or the local council, NEMA,
UNBS, the sector ministry) through the Digital Research engine and the
source register `docs/source-registers/country-market-data.json`; route tax and
payroll statements to the Chwezi finance doctrine.

Source attribution: procedure distilled from the Norwegian Refugee Council's
*Self-Employment and Small Business Guide for Uganda* (May 2025) and business
interviews in *Uganda 2025* (World Business Journal), recorded in the
engine's former planning notes of 2026-03-10; the notes were retired on
2026-09-24 and their dated figures were not carried forward.

## 1. Formalisation sequence (verify each step's current requirements)

1. **Choose the legal form.** Compare sole proprietorship, partnership and
   company on liability, registration route, compliance load, set-up cost,
   investor-readiness, tax regime and foreign-ownership rules (table below).
2. **Register the name or incorporate** through the URSB online system; record
   the certificate type (registration or incorporation) and company type.
3. **Obtain a TIN**; decide whether VAT registration is required against the
   current threshold (finance engine confirms the threshold and rate).
4. **Register with NSSF** if the business employs staff; self-employed owners
   may register voluntarily (confirm current rules and rates).
5. **Obtain a trading licence** from KCCA in Kampala or the municipal or town
   council elsewhere; check exemptions and processing time.
6. **Open a business bank account**; banks ask for identity and certified
   registration documents.
7. **Obtain sector approvals** where the activity requires them: NEMA
   (environmental screening for manufacturing, construction, fuel, wetlands and
   similar), UNBS (product standards and certification), Ministry of Health or
   the relevant regulator (food, pharmaceutical, health), and the sector
   licence portal. Apply the engine's sector regulatory gates.
8. **Register data processing with the PDPO** if the business collects
   personal data (see the marketing orchestrator's location and scope
   calibration, claim PL-01).

## 2. Legal-form comparison (criteria to evaluate, not conclusions)

| Criterion | Sole proprietor | Company |
|---|---|---|
| Liability | Unlimited; personal assets exposed | Limited |
| Registration | Business name plus trading licence | Incorporation through URSB |
| Compliance load | Lighter | Annual returns, statutory records, audit where required |
| Set-up cost | Lower | Higher (legal and filing costs) |
| Investor readiness | Weak; usually needs restructuring | Stronger |
| Tax regime | May qualify for a small-business regime below a turnover threshold (finance engine to confirm) | Corporate income tax (finance engine to confirm rate) |
| Foreign or refugee owners | Check current eligibility rules | Check current ownership rules |

## 3. Record-keeping minimum (why and what)

Records support profit measurement, fair tax assessment, budgeting and loan
applications. Keep receipts and invoices, contracts, bank statements, staff
appointment letters, utility bills, stock records, asset registers, import
schedules, income statements, balance sheets and payroll records. Income
records show date, items, quantity, unit price and total; expense records also
show supplier and buyer details. Confirm the statutory retention period and
any e-invoicing (EFRIS) obligation with the finance engine.

## 4. Lender and funder document checklist

Before a plan goes to a bank, SACCO, DFI or investor, confirm which of these
exist, their dates and their validity:

- Certificate of registration or incorporation, and company records.
- TIN certificate; tax clearance certificate where requested (it expires and
  must be renewed).
- Trading licence (current year).
- NSSF registration and clearance if employing staff.
- Sector permits: NEMA, UNBS, health or other regulator approvals.
- Financial statements (income statement, balance sheet) and 3–6 months of
  bank statements (confirm each lender's requirement).
- Proof of address and owners' identity documents.
- Collateral evidence; where formal land title is absent, note alternative
  security routes offered by the lender or group-guarantee models (verify).
- Board or approval minutes where the lender requires them; note that credit
  approval times can lengthen when governance rules change (verify current
  practice with the lender).

## 5. Funding-programme screen

Government and development programmes (for example parish-level development
funds, group-based enterprise funds, skilling programmes, foundation credit
linkages, innovation-hub grants) change eligibility and amounts often. For each
candidate programme record: owner institution, current eligibility, amount and
terms, application window, evidence required and source date. Never state an
amount from memory or from an old guide.

## 6. Consumer-protection and dispute points to check

Check current consumer-protection and competition obligations (disclosure of
material facts, reasonable quality, no deceptive advertising); the route for
commercial disputes (commercial court, arbitration or mediation, small-claims
procedure and its current ceiling); and intellectual-property registration
steps and fees. NGOs and community organisations have their own registration
and reporting rules; confirm the current regulator.

## 7. Stress-test prompts for Ugandan plans

Use these as scenario prompts in `meta-financial-stress-test`, each with a
dated source before any number is used: exchange-rate depreciation on imported
inputs or USD-priced costs; power-tariff and raw-material cost shocks for
manufacturers; high lending rates; input-price inflation; post-harvest losses
for fresh produce; export-market regulation (for example deforestation-free
rules for coffee to the EU); logistics bottlenecks; delayed large projects.
