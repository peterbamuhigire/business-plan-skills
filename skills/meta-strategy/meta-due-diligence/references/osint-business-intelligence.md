# Open-Source Intelligence for Due Diligence and Business Intelligence

Task reference for legal, open-source research used in a business plan: vetting partners, suppliers, hires and acquisition targets; validating competitor and market claims; and preparing the business for the same scrutiny by lenders and investors. Techniques below use public information only.

## 1. Investigation method

Use a five-step cycle (CRAWL: Communicate, Research, Analyse, Write, Listen; Hetherington).

1. **Communicate:** fix scope before starting: subject, purpose, budget, timeline. Record it in a statement of work. State what can and cannot be found. Keep asking why the work is being done and why not something else.
2. **Research:** the bulk of effort (roughly 70 per cent as a planning assumption). Sources: search engines, databases, social media, court records, field interviews. Keep the scope neither too narrow (misses data) nor too broad (noise). Build a keyword taxonomy at the start and extend it. Track changes over time and prioritise leads by common sense. Never fabricate or extrapolate.
3. **Analyse:** turn data into knowledge; do not merely summarise (roughly 20 per cent of effort). Use a structured framework (section 4), not impressions. Corroborate each finding in at least two independent sources. Grade material as evidence (high confidence), intelligence (trusted secondary source) or hearsay (unverifiable; label it).
4. **Write:** non-biased, organised, professionally formatted. Key findings first, most unfavourable first, then recommended next steps. Use qualifying language for social media ("it appears that"). Date-stamp and location-stamp all searches, as results vary.
5. **Listen:** follow up on whether the report answered the real question; consult experts, former employees and local contacts for nuance databases miss; keep listening if the situation is still evolving.

## 2. Three phases

- **Phase 1, online:** search engines, news archives, company sites, social media (Facebook, LinkedIn, X, Instagram, TikTok), government registries, court systems, licensing boards, local to international and trade media, litigation records, forums, web archives, domain registries. Compile: academic credentials, board and non-profit roles, business and personal affiliations, civil and criminal filings, corporate and property records, securities filings, government litigation, intellectual property, liens and judgments and secured-transaction filings, media history and adverse press, sanctions and regulatory watch lists, vendor and supplier relationships, global compliance records. Report in four parts: identifiers and assets; financial history (debt, bankruptcy, property); civil and criminal filings; reputation.
- **Phase 2, field work:** discreet interviews, reference calls, on-site retrieval of court documents. Trigger when Phase 1 leaves unresolved red flags, the subject is foreign with sparse online records, or nuance needs human sources. Many courts do not provide historical dockets online (a widely cited planning figure is about 30 per cent; verify locally), so physical retrieval is often needed.
- **Phase 3, monitoring:** when findings are inconclusive or the situation is moving: watch forums, social groups and employee-review sites; track court and regulatory databases for new filings; set media alerts on the subject and related entities.

## 3. Five investigation types

1. **Competitor:** map vendors, clients and revenue streams over the past 6 to 12 months; identify trends and project the next 6 to 12 months; produce a SWOT of the competitor's position; recommend monitoring.
2. **Competitive or market intelligence:** whole-industry view with statistical depth; multiple players and global dynamics; sources include commercial company databases, the SCIP professional network, Kompass and trade journals. Use to test market-size claims in the market-analysis section.
3. **Potential acquisition:** search media for malpractice, misconduct and reputational damage first; review ESG reports (self-published and third-party); examine financial health (dependence on venture funding, burn rate); analyse marketing and website positioning; treat glowing reviews with poor sales as a management warning.
4. **Fraudulent or defaulted company:** trace tangible assets that may have moved to shareholders; identify every responsible party, their wealth, vehicles (including boats and aircraft), homes, other businesses, family and associates; treat trademarks, patents and copyrights as traceable assets.
5. **Background check:** company (ownership, management, investors, corporate actions); person (career, credentials, criminal and civil history, assets, reputation); location (property ownership, taxes, local compliance).

## 4. Analytical frameworks

**SWOT** for competitors, market position and acquisition targets. Strengths: what the subject does well, assets, position. Weaknesses: revenue below peer benchmarks, compliance gaps, management deficiencies. Opportunities: adjacent markets, acquirable property, under-used distribution. Threats: regulatory exposure, aggressive rivals, reputational risk. Rule: revenue far below similar-sized peers (a gap near half is a strong signal) is a weakness or threat indicator; never accept company-supplied revenue without cross-checking a credit-reporting or industry source. SWOT is unsuitable for fraud cases, which need critical and creative thinking.

**CARA** for vetting individuals (partners, key hires, suppliers, investors):

| Element | Look for |
|---|---|
| Characteristics | Professional standing, lifestyle signals, litigation history, awards |
| Associations | Socioeconomic circle, close colleagues, family |
| Reputation | Trustworthy, reliable, combative; media portrayal; employee accounts |
| Affiliations | Professional bodies, boards, alma mater, charitable giving |

Associations with people under investigation justify scrutiny even without direct evidence, but present them with qualifying language and never as accusations.

**Supply-chain analysis** for supplier vetting, resilience and pre-investment manufacturing diligence. Seven components: inbound logistics (sourcing, handling, storage, regulatory breaches); outbound logistics (packaging, shipping, vetting of distributors); operations (who really makes the product; hidden subcontractors); support teams (R&D, unions, immigration compliance for labour-intensive work); human resources (tools, safety, leadership); infrastructure (location risk, recovery and continuity plans, ISO certification); technology (CRM, tracking, data management). A supplier with no contingency for a single point of failure is a high operational risk.

**Value-chain analysis:** examine the functions that add value from raw material to consumer to find the weakest link, judge which partner creates or destroys value, and evaluate one element of an operation before acquisition.

## 5. Company research

| Entity | Sources |
|---|---|
| Listed corporation | Securities-regulator filings: annual and quarterly reports, event reports, proxy statements (executive pay) |
| Private company | Commercial databases (self-reported; treat with caution), registry filings |
| Non-profit or NGO | Tax-return disclosures and charity databases: executive pay, donors, grants |
| LLC or partnership | Registry filings; check original paper documents, as corrections are invisible in digital copies |
| Foreign company | Global company databases; local investigators for full records |

**Commercial and free databases (verify current coverage and price):** Dun and Bradstreet (self-reported; search by principal's name to reveal connected entities; search by fax number, since fraudulent companies reuse fax numbers; search by address, since many companies at one address is a flag); Kompass (free searches by company, executive, product, trade name, region; useful start for East African and emerging markets); Sayari (multi-country public records, litigation, property, IP, trade, sanctions screening across EU, UN, US lists); Moody's Orbis (ownership structures, M&A, financials); SkyMinder (credit, payment history, banking, litigation, sometimes in-country researchers); OpenCorporates (free cross-jurisdiction links and corporate family trees); ZoomInfo (people-to-company links).

**Domain and web presence:** check owner, creation date, registrar and registered address through WHOIS or domain-history tools. They should match the claimed business location and age. A polished website proves nothing; many companies at one address suggests a shell or mail-drop. Co-working and serviced offices are legitimate but also used by fraudulent entities to look established.

## 6. People research

**Four subject types:** the privacy seeker (low profile, not suspicious in itself; needs deeper record searches); the limelighter (cultivates media; press releases and executive quotes are marketing, so read what others wrote, especially older articles); the fraudulent one (charismatic, claims special knowledge, runs seminars or pitches; investigate before money moves; records may sit in another jurisdiction under a variant name); the incompetent one (overextended, unaware of compliance rules, or an overachiever who ignores limits).

**Sources:**
- Credentials: licensing boards (status, complaints, discipline); university registrars (confirm degrees directly, not from CV or LinkedIn); financial-professional registers; medical and legal disciplinary databases. A missing licence where one is required is a significant flag.
- Courts: national and state or regional court systems; search civil and criminal records in every jurisdiction where the subject lived or worked; civil records show disputes, defaults and patterns.
- Assets: property and tax-assessor records (liens show financial pressure); secured-transaction filings (loans secured on assets); patent and trademark registers (often overlooked); non-profit filings for affiliated bodies.
- Boards: proxy statements list directors and pay. Look for members who joined together, directors who are also vendors, family members on one board, and non-profit grant flows into projects the subject was contracted to build.
- Political and charitable giving: national and state donation databases and open-data sites; charity can be sincere or a front for influence or laundering.

**Social media intelligence (SOCMINT):** LinkedIn (career, boards, former employees); Facebook (lifestyle, assets, groups, behaviour); X (statements, commentary); Instagram (geo-tagged posts, assets); TikTok (behaviour, associations); public payment-app feeds (connections and spending, where privacy is misconfigured).
Techniques: search consistent handle variants across platforms; use site-restricted web search to avoid logging in; check abandoned early accounts; search industry hashtags; note photo metadata where platforms retain it.
Cautions: profile fraud is rampant, so verify profiles before relying on them; qualify findings; never friend or contact the subject; view public content only; some jurisdictions (for example Germany) bar employers from viewing candidates' profiles, so check local law.

**Audit your own footprint:** run CARA on your own business; read what former employees wrote on review sites; check that domain registration matches the registered address; search the company name with "complaint", "scam", "lawsuit", "fraud".

## 7. Financial intelligence

**Fraud types visible through open sources:** market manipulation; pump-and-dump; Ponzi schemes (returns paid from new investors, no profitable enterprise); backdating; inflated revenue; shell-company layering.

**Distress and deception signals:** revenue far below peers; frequent changes of address or name; several companies sharing a fax, phone or address; CEO or CFO with regulatory discipline, enforcement action or past bankruptcy; annual returns filed late; no verifiable clients or references; a firm claiming large revenue yet absent from every database.

**Traceable assets and liens:** property records; secured-transaction filings; unpaid court judgments; bankruptcy records; vehicle and vessel registers (commercial vessels via maritime authorities); IP registers; disclosed shareholdings above the public threshold in listed companies.

**Regulatory signals:** licensing-board discipline; workplace-safety violations (manufacturing, food processing); food, drug and device regulator citations; securities enforcement (financial services); import and export data (trade-data providers) to test claimed trade; government exclusion lists and UN, EU and US sanctions lists.

## 8. Competitor intelligence checklist

**Identity and structure:** [ ] confirm legal name, registration number, registered address [ ] list trading names and brands [ ] determine entity type [ ] trace subsidiary or parent chain [ ] domain registration matches stated address [ ] identify directors and cross-check their other interests.
**Financial health:** [ ] business credit reports [ ] revenue against peer benchmarks [ ] judgments, liens, secured filings [ ] supplier late-payment patterns [ ] past bankruptcies of company or principals [ ] financial-regulator actions.
**Operations and supply:** [ ] key suppliers (trade data, directories) [ ] capacity: staff, sites, equipment [ ] licensing, health and safety, environmental permits [ ] ISO certification [ ] safety or environmental citations for manufacturers.
**Reputation:** [ ] search name plus "lawsuit", "complaint", "fraud", "fine" [ ] news archives for adverse media [ ] employee reviews [ ] industry forums and trade-association complaint channels [ ] consumer-protection records.
**Digital footprint:** [ ] audit social accounts for consistency, engagement and tone [ ] key personnel's public profiles [ ] customer praise or complaints in unmoderated spaces [ ] activity consistent with claimed size and revenue.
**People:** [ ] CARA on directors and management [ ] verify credentials with registrars [ ] licence status [ ] court records in all jurisdictions [ ] board conflicts, family clusters, vendor overlaps [ ] donations and charitable affiliations.

## 9. Preparing your own business for scrutiny

Lenders and investors will run at least a Phase 1 review covering: registry records (do they match your claims; are returns filed on time), principals' criminal, civil and regulatory history, credentials, court records, domain and web presence, social footprint, adverse media, sanctions lists, plausibility of revenue against size, non-profit or charity ties.

Preparation:
- **Registry:** filings current; filed address equals operating address (or explain); director changes filed promptly.
- **Credentials:** certified copies of qualifications; licences in good standing; brief co-founders on background checks and disclose any history proactively.
- **Digital footprint:** review the first three pages of results for your name and company; search name plus complaint, scam, lawsuit, bad debt and address what appears; align social claims on size, revenue and clients; review directors' personal accounts; use a genuine business address in domain records, not a mail drop.
- **Court and regulatory:** pull your own record in every relevant jurisdiction and prepare a factual explanation; keep licences and permits current.
- **Financial records:** revenue claims must reconcile to bank statements and tax filings; clear judgments and tax debts before fundraising; secured filings match actual pledged assets.
- **References:** offer three to five credible customers, briefed on context; check what former employees say publicly.

## 10. Supplier and vendor red flags

**Primary (do not proceed without investigation):** several companies at one address, especially a mail drop or shared office; shared fax numbers; new company with large revenue claims and no history; director with prior fraud conviction, even under a variant name or in another jurisdiction; claimed licence not on the register; no import or export records despite claimed cross-border trade; domain registered within the last one to two years for a supposedly long-established firm; enforcement action against principals; sanctions-list match (screen consolidated lists before any significant payment); non-profit filing irregularities with grants flowing to projects controlled by the same party.

**Secondary (investigate further):** revenue per employee far above or below sector norms; director with no verifiable employment history; registration in a tax-haven jurisdiction without operating rationale; polished website with no traceable office, named staff or references; director sitting on a key client's or supplier's board; absence of adverse media (it may mean nobody has looked; absence is not validation).

**Behavioural:** reluctance to give references, registration documents or audited accounts; full prepayment demanded with no escrow; prices well below market without explanation; pressure to close before checks finish; last-minute payment-instruction changes (business email compromise pattern); inability to explain inconsistencies in their own materials.

## 11. Uganda and East Africa: limits and workarounds

Western-built methods must be adapted to thin public records.

- **Company registry:** the Uganda Registration Services Bureau (URSB) holds companies, business names and partnerships. Online search is basic; full filing history may need an in-person request in Kampala. Late annual returns are common (a yellow flag, not proof of closure). Kenya's Business Registration Service is more accessible and a useful regional comparator.
- **Courts:** Ugandan records are largely undigitised (some online cause lists; full records need physical access). Kenya's e-citizen portal offers better online access.
- **Financial records:** most private firms have no public filing regime. Bank of Uganda publishes licensing data for financial institutions; the Capital Markets Authority holds listed-securities records; the tax authority's data is not public but tax clearance certificates can be requested and verified.
- **Regulatory records:** Uganda Investment Authority (investment licences), UNBS (product certification), NEMA (environmental compliance), ICPAU (accountant credentials). Verify each source's current online availability.

Workarounds:
1. Trade references on mobile-money behaviour: consistent payment patterns are positive; unusual routing requests are a flag (the data itself is not public).
2. Social media is often richer than registries: Facebook business pages, groups and public posts show operations, customers and reputation; also LinkedIn.
3. Physical verification (Phase 2) matters more: visit the address, observe operations, ask neighbouring businesses.
4. Trade associations (manufacturers, chambers of commerce, traders' associations) hold informal reputation networks; call them.
5. Regional databases: Orbis, Sayari, OpenCorporates, Kompass (partial East Africa coverage); development-bank and World Bank procurement lists for larger suppliers; sanctions lists for cross-border counterparties.
6. Local journalists and archives (Monitor, Daily Nation, The EastAfrican) often outperform databases.
7. Trade data: national customs data, ITC TradeMap for aggregate flows; commercial trade-data services have limited but growing coverage.

Local cautions: date of incorporation is on file but pre-2010 records may be undigitised; director names vary in spelling across documents, so use phonetic and variant searches; several businesses at one address is common (shared offices) and less diagnostic than in Western markets, so judge in context.

## 12. Report standard

Structure: (1) cover sheet: date, title, investigator, client; (2) confidentiality and scope statement (online only, or with interviews); (3) key findings, unfavourable first, then neutral, then positive; (4) recommended next steps; (5) detailed findings by category (identity, financial, legal, reputational, social); (6) exhibits: screen captures, copies of public documents, sources; (7) disclaimer that database information may be incomplete or wrong.

Language: cite the source ("based on proprietary databases, the subject appears to reside at"); qualify social media and unverified items; make no direct accusations from associations or circumstantial evidence; for common names state that results cannot be confirmed as relating to the subject.

Investigation sequence: (1) pull profile reports from public-record vendors; (2) for companies, pull corporate database reports; (3) search local registries and courts where the company sits; (4) cross-check addresses across databases (consistency raises confidence); (5) identify relatives, associates and friends; (6) locate and verify assets; (7) research history: criminal, regulatory, litigation, media, social; (8) draft key findings then next steps.

## 14. Legal and ethical limits

Generally lawful: viewing public profiles and posts; public registry and court records; news archives; public non-profit and financial disclosures; public WHOIS; public IP registers; sanctions and exclusion lists.

Limits: consumer-credit laws (for example the US FCRA) bar access to bank or credit files without a permissible purpose; data-protection law (GDPR and equivalents, including Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act) restricts personal-data use for background checks; do not friend or contact subjects; no GPS tracking, hacking or access to protected systems; privacy rules in some jurisdictions make standard OSINT risky, so take local counsel's advice before Phase 2 work abroad.

Trust test: would the subject, knowing what you researched and how, regard it as reasonable due diligence? If not, change the method.
