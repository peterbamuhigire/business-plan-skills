# Transaction Due Diligence: Verifying the Plan, Shaping Deal Terms, Seller Self-Review

Use this file to make a business plan verification-ready, to design a due diligence (DD) plan and questionnaire for any significant transaction, and to translate findings into deal terms. It complements the workstream reference in due-diligence-ma-howson.md. Legal and tax points are general; confirm with local counsel and the Chwezi finance engine.

## 1. Scope: what DD covers and when

DD is the work of understanding four things before committing to a transaction: the company (legal and financial structure), its operations (products, assets, services, how it functions), the valuation (of the business, assets and deal), and the people (key personnel, capability, employment matters). Every question maps to one or more of these four cornerstones; tag each DD item accordingly.

| Cornerstone | DD goal |
|---|---|
| Company | Understand structure; find organisational and structural risk |
| Operations | Understand products, assets, services and processes; find legal, financial and business risk |
| Valuation | Test that the valuation is supportable; find financial risk |
| Personnel | Identify key people and their capacity to run the business and deliver the plan |

Transactions where DD is routine: loans; purchase of a business or its assets; company purchase (merger, buyout); purchase of shares or membership interests (private placement); real estate purchase.

Transactions where DD matters equally but is often skipped: franchise purchase; founding a company with a partner; licensing technology or IP in or out; joint ventures; commissioning third-party design or development; third-party manufacture or supply; material long-term contracts.

Rule: the nature of the transaction, not its size, sets the scope. Two loans secured on the same type of collateral need the same questions at any amount.

## 2. Reading the plan as an investor does

An investor reads a plan to find what must be independently checked, not to be persuaded. The sequence is: plan generates the DD plan; the DD plan generates the questionnaire; the questionnaire generates findings; findings decide whether the deal proceeds, on what terms and at what price.

Claims that become investigation targets: revenue and growth; customer relationships and contract status; ownership of key assets (IP, software, equipment); management record; regulatory compliance; market size and competitive position; projections and their assumptions.

### 2.1 Verification-ready plan (self-test)
1. Every material claim is sourced (market data, benchmarks).
2. Documents prove ownership (contribution agreements, IP assignments, incorporation documents).
3. Contracts are assignable to a new owner.
4. Financial records reconcile to tax returns.
5. Written employment agreements with IP-assignment clauses exist for all staff.
6. Evidence for every projection assumption can be produced on request.

### 2.2 Section-by-section verification table

| Plan section | Claims to verify | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| 01 Executive summary | Concept, opportunity, financial headline | Consistency with every other section |
| 02 Company overview | Structure, ownership, history, good standing | Formation documents, good-standing evidence, capitalisation table, minutes |
| 03 Products and services | IP ownership, description, uniqueness | Assignment agreements, registrations, demonstrations or certifications |
| 04 Market analysis | Market size, growth, trends | Independent research with citations |
| 05 Target market | Segments, buying behaviour | Customer references, sales data, sources |
| 06 Competitive analysis | Competitors, advantage claims | Independent research; customer references confirming preference |
| 07 Marketing and sales | Pipeline, effectiveness, customer acquisition cost | Sales reports, CRM data, conversion history |
| 08 Operations | Capacity, systems, workflow | Site visit; workflow documents; equipment titles and valuations |
| 09 Management team | Qualifications, record, experience | CVs, references, background checks, prior-company performance |
| 10 Financial projections | Forecasts, cost assumptions, profitability timeline | Historical statements reconciled to tax returns; documented assumptions; sensitivity analysis |
| 11 Funding request | Amount, use of funds, repayment capacity | Use-of-funds schedule; DSCR calculation; collateral documents |
| 12 Risk analysis | Risks and mitigation | Cross-check with legal, financial and market findings |
| 13 Implementation timeline | Milestone realism, resources | Supplier and contractor contracts; staffing plan; regulatory timeline |
| 14 Appendices | Supporting documents | The actual documents behind each claim |

Investor's test: take the most optimistic statement in the plan and ask what document, data point or independent source would prove or disprove it. If the answer is "nothing", the claim is unverifiable and will be treated as a risk.

## 3. The DD plan

Draft a DD plan before the questionnaire, even a one-page outline for a small deal. It identifies the key aspects of the transaction, maps each to the four cornerstones, flags known early issues and sets the scope of the questionnaire.

Outline:
1. **Corporate structure:** formation and governance documents; ownership and capitalisation; approval rights and restrictions.
2. **Business operations:** nature of the business and revenue model; products, services, assets; key contracts and assignability; operating workflows and policies.
3. **Valuation:** three to six years of financial statements; revenue by source and location; top customers by revenue; asset valuations; growth assumptions.
4. **Personnel:** organisation chart; key-employee terms and retention risk; employment-law compliance; IP and confidentiality agreements with staff.

## 4. Standard questionnaire (all transaction types)

**Company information.** Legal name, assumed or trading names and place of formation; good-standing evidence for every operating jurisdiction; formation documents and amendments; minute books, resolutions and consents; all changes of identity or structure; capitalisation table (holders, options, warrants, convertibles); shareholder, investor-rights and voting agreements; rights of first refusal over assets or equity. Reason: a lien filed under a trading name is missed if you search only the registered name, and an unknown right of first refusal can unwind a deal after closing.

**Asset ownership.** Title, bill of sale or contribution agreement for each material asset; formal assignment of all founder and partner contributions to the company; IP ownership documents (invention assignments, copyright and patent filings); liens, encumbrances and third-party claims; lien searches under the company name and every trading name.

**Financial and tax.** Three to six years of statements and tax returns (all jurisdictions); receivables ageing; ten largest customers by revenue per year for five years; top five revenue sources by product or service for five years; notices of financial difficulty from major customers (including insolvency filings); tax liens, arrears and open audits; loan agreements, credit lines and personal guarantees; capital expenditure plans.

**Contracts.** All material revenue-critical or operationally critical agreements; for key customer contracts, assignability and termination on change of control; supplier agreements; property and equipment leases (expiry, renewal, assignability); licences in and out (assignability, change of control); agreements granting third parties rights over the business or its assets; non-compete or exclusivity terms binding the company or key people.

**Claims and proceedings.** Pending or threatened civil, criminal or administrative actions; monetary and equitable judgments; government investigations (tax, environmental, labour); insolvency filings in the last ten years (voluntary or involuntary); settlements and consent decrees.

**Employees and key personnel.** Organisation chart; key-person agreements; confidentiality, non-disclosure, invention-assignment and non-compete agreements for all employees, contractors and former staff with IP access; payroll and benefits compliance; pending employment claims; worker classification (employee or contractor); post-deal retention risk.

## 5. Financial DD: does the story support the valuation?

Central question: do the financials support the valuation behind the deal? For a lender, can the borrower repay and is collateral sufficient; for a buyer, is the price justified by present and projected performance; for an investor, do projected returns rest on supportable assumptions? Unsupported valuation assumptions justify renegotiation or termination.

### 5.1 Findings and typical deal responses

| Finding | Typical response |
|---|---|
| Liabilities above those represented | Smaller loan; personal guarantee; escrow holdback |
| Declining revenue | Lower price; milestone-based payment (earn-out) |
| Excessive operating expenses | Expense covenants; lender approval thresholds; management pay deferral |
| Shrinking margins | Renegotiate or withdraw |
| Collateral value unsupported by appraisal | Smaller principal; extra collateral |
| Revenue concentrated in one customer | Concentration discount; earn-out tied to retention |
| Management pay inconsistent with company stage | Pay reduction or deferral as a condition |
| No financial controls | Controls as a pre- or post-closing condition |
| Tax issues (audits, arrears, indirect-tax exposure) | Tax opinions; escrow; warranties |

### 5.2 Questions for projections
1. Are revenue assumptions consistent with history?
2. Are growth rates supported by market data or customer contracts?
3. Are cost assumptions complete (indirect costs, taxes, depreciation)?
4. Is working-capital need included?
5. How sensitive is the forecast to losing key customers or staff?
6. Do historical earnings include one-off items that inflate the baseline?
7. Do projections assume relationships that are not contractually locked in?

When projections are disputed, use an earn-out (section 8.4).

## 6. Commercial and market DD

Customer analysis: request monthly revenue by location, top ten customers per year for five years, insolvency or distress notices for top customers, and top five revenue sources per year for five years. This reveals concentration risk, shifts in the customer base and why, financial fragility of customers, and whether growth assumptions match customer trajectory.

Market position: look for independent data behind market-size (TAM/SAM) claims; whether stated advantages are durable and documented; whether key contracts support the claimed position; and what happens if one major contract is lost.

Revenue-model stress tests: current demand; room for growth or saturation; dependence on a competitor's absence or on a regulatory condition that may change.

## 7. Legal and compliance DD

### 7.1 Contract deal-killers
1. **Assignment clauses:** consent needed to transfer a material contract. Without it the buyer gets the business but not the relationship. Do not accept a seller's oral assurance; read each contract.
2. **Change-of-control clauses:** automatic termination on a change of ownership (common in franchise and many commercial contracts).
3. **Exclusivity provisions:** exclusive rights that lapse on assignment.
4. **Expiry risk:** key contracts or leases near expiry; will they renew on the same terms?

### 7.2 IP and intangibles
- Has a formal assignment been signed for all software, patents and creative works made by founders and early staff (especially work created before incorporation)?
- Did any former partner contribute IP without a written agreement?
- Have all employees and contractors signed IP assignments?
- Are registrations current (search the registry)?
- Are there conflicting claims, third-party licences or liens?
- Is licensed IP assignable, or does it revert on change of control?

A founder who wrote software before incorporation and contributed it informally leaves the company unable to prove it owns its main asset. This is the most common single trap.

### 7.3 Regulatory and environmental
Licences and permits held; pending inspections, violations or investigations; environmental liabilities (buried tanks, hazardous substances, remediation orders); employment-law compliance (payroll taxes, benefits, worker classification); data-privacy obligations where customer data is held. For real property, obtain phase 1 and, where indicated, phase 2 environmental assessments; require seller indemnity and escrow against unknown environmental claims, because uncertain remediation cost can make a deal unviable.

## 8. Management and people DD

### 8.1 Background verification for directors, managing owners and major shareholders
Criminal history; insolvency history; pending suits (personal); government investigations (regulatory, securities, administrative); tax liabilities and audits beyond the current year; material disputes with potential liability above a stated threshold; companies in which they hold a material stake (for example 5% or more); personal guarantees in force; non-compete or non-solicitation obligations; consent to credit and full background checks. Set thresholds and legal permissibility locally.

### 8.2 Track record
Performance of the team here and at prior companies; references from customers, suppliers and former partners; industry reputation (and, for franchises, the franchisor's reference).

### 8.3 Retention and conflicts
Retention tools: post-closing employment agreements; price holdback released if key staff stay for a defined period; non-solicitation agreements; equity or profit-share. Conflict checks: related-party agreements between insiders and the company; personal guarantees that misalign incentives; non-competes that stop founders helping after closing.

## 9. Turning findings into terms

**Pre-closing conditions** (fix before closing): missing operating or shareholder agreement (execute it); former bookkeeper with bank signing authority (remove by resolution); franchisor or counterparty consent to ownership change (obtain it); outstanding health or regulatory violations (resolve); former partner threatening competition (obtain a non-compete).

**Post-closing covenants** (documented obligations): unfinished cross-marketing agreement (seller helps finalise); software update in progress with a vendor (seller pays fees and cooperates to completion); missing invention assignment from a partner (obtain within a set period, with price held in escrow); key-employee flight risk (holdback released on retention); weak budgeting (financial plans and budgets by a set date for investor review); no written workflows (written policies by a set date).

**Representations and warranties:** the seller gives written statements on the state of the business; a false statement gives a claim for breach. Indemnities back them for undisclosed liabilities and misrepresentation. Escrow holdbacks (commonly 10 to 20% of price for a defined period; treat as a negotiable planning assumption) secure the indemnity.

**Earn-out:** split the price into an upfront amount based on verified history and later payments contingent on post-closing revenue or profit milestones. It shifts valuation risk to the seller.

**Price adjustments:** adjust for the difference between closing net working capital and an agreed target; write a precise mechanism into the agreement to avoid disputes.

## 10. Checklists

### 10.1 Financial
- Three to six years of statements (balance sheet, P&L, cash flow) and tax returns, all jurisdictions; management accounts for the last 12 to 24 months.
- Receivables ageing; payables schedule.
- Ten largest customers (five-year history); insolvency or distress notices; top five revenue sources (five-year history).
- Loan agreements, notes, credit lines; personal guarantees by owners or directors.
- Judgments, tax liens, secured-creditor filings; ongoing or threatened tax audits.
- Capex plans and asset schedules; payroll records and employer tax compliance.
- Accrued unpaid liabilities (bonuses, pension or retirement contributions, statutory obligations).

### 10.2 Legal
- Registration certificate and amendments; operating or shareholder agreement and amendments; good-standing evidence; capitalisation table with full history.
- Shareholder, investor-rights and voting agreements; rights of first refusal, options, warrants, convertibles.
- Ownership documents for material assets; IP registrations; invention assignments (founders, employees, contractors).
- All material contracts and leases (assignability, change of control, expiry, renewal); licences in and out.
- Regulatory licences and permits; environmental reports (phase 1 and 2 for real property).
- Pending or threatened litigation; judgments, orders, settlements, consent decrees; government notices, compliance orders, fines.
- Privacy policy and data-handling compliance.
- Searches: liens under every name used; judgment and insolvency searches; tax-lien searches (national and local, using the equivalent Ugandan registries).

### 10.3 Commercial
- Revenue by customer, product or service and location (five years); concentration analysis (flag a single customer above about 20% of revenue).
- Customer contract review (assignability, termination, exclusivity); supplier concentration; key-contract renewal schedule.
- Independent evidence for market size and growth; top three competitors and shares; barriers to entry.
- Pipeline of signed letters of intent, proposals or contracts not yet recognised as revenue; churn for recurring revenue; complaints, warranty claims, product liability.

### 10.4 People and management
- Organisation chart; key-person agreements; compensation schedule (salary, bonus, equity, benefits).
- Invention-assignment and confidentiality agreements; non-competes (scope and enforceability).
- Background-check consents; criminal, insolvency and tax-compliance confirmations for directors and major shareholders; references.
- Key-employee retention plan; employee manual and HR policies; payroll and benefits compliance (payroll tax, pension, leave); pending employment claims; worker-classification review.

## 11. Twenty findings that kill deals or cut valuation
1. IP never formally assigned to the company.
2. Key contracts not assignable.
3. Change-of-control termination in critical contracts.
4. Revenue concentrated in one or two customers.
5. Revenue or profit declining just before the deal (the seller may be racing the deterioration).
6. Records missing or inconsistent with tax returns.
7. Undisclosed liabilities (tax arrears, lawsuits, third-party guarantees, warranty claims).
8. No governance documents (no operating or shareholder agreement).
9. Former partner with unresolved claims (IP, employment, equity) and no release.
10. Founders' personal tax liabilities (a personal lien can reach business assets in some jurisdictions).
11. Environmental liabilities with open-ended remediation.
12. Management pay inconsistent with company stage.
13. No financial controls (a single person with unrestricted account access, no approval limits, unenforced pricing).
14. No documented workflows (the business lives in the founder's head).
15. Key-employee retention risk.
16. Pending or threatened litigation, especially regulatory, employment or IP.
17. Unsupported projections.
18. Undisclosed related-party transactions (owner as supplier, customer or landlord).
19. No IP-assignment agreements with developers, designers and technical staff.
20. A corporate structure that blocks investment (no share classes for investors; agreements that bar investor protections).

## 12. Seller self-review: 90-day preparation plan

Run your own DD before the buyer does. Adopting good practice along the way beats cleaning up under pressure, and disciplined policies sharply cut the chance that a buyer's DD finds value-destroying issues. Assemble the right advisory team early: it improves the quality of the DD and lowers the risk of costly surprises after closing.

**Corporate housekeeping (days 1 to 30).** Obtain good-standing evidence everywhere you operate; update formation documents; confirm the capitalisation table is accurate (all equity, options, warrants); map rights of first refusal, drag-along rights and supermajority approvals; document all equity issued to founders, employees and advisers with current vesting; register any trading name in all relevant places; run lien searches under all names and clear surprises.

**Intellectual property (days 1 to 30).** List all IP relied on (software, brands, patents, trade secrets, creative works); confirm a written assignment or contribution agreement for each; collect signed assignments from current and former employees and contractors; search the registries (URSB, ARIPO, national patent office) for current, unencumbered registrations; check IP licences for change-of-control terms; obtain a written assignment from any former partner who contributed IP.

**Contracts (days 15 to 45).** List material contracts; review each for assignability, change of control, expiry and renewal; approach counterparties about problematic clauses before any deal is visible (leverage falls once a deal is known); confirm lease assignability and landlord consent; start renewals for anything expiring within 12 months.

**Financial records (days 15 to 60).** Have an accountant review three years of statements and reconcile them to tax returns; clean the receivables ageing and settle disputes; prepare revenue by customer and by product for three to five years; document one-off items that inflate earnings; clear tax arrears (PAYE, VAT, withholding tax) and file any outstanding returns; document every projection assumption; confirm all bank accounts are held by the company, not a founder, and remove ex-signatories.

**Employment and HR (days 30 to 60).** Signed employment agreements (with IP and confidentiality clauses) for everyone; organisation chart with roles and pay; employee manual; payroll-tax compliance (PAYE, NSSF); review contractor classification; assess key-employee retention and consider retention agreements or bonuses.

**Legal compliance (days 30 to 75).** Current licences and permits (trading, sector, environmental); resolve regulatory violations and inspection findings; summarise pending or threatened litigation with counsel's exposure view; surface any tax notices or audit letters yourself; Uganda: confirm NIN or BRN chain, TIN, EFRIS enrolment where applicable, UNBS certification for standardised products (verify current requirements).

**Operations documentation (days 45 to 90).** Document key workflows (orders, production or delivery, returns, inventory); write policies for pricing authority, expense limits, bank access and data handling; back up files, software and data off-site or in the cloud; secure proprietary source code with controlled access; build the data room (section 15).

**Founders and directors (days 60 to 90).** Confirm personal tax compliance; list personal guarantees in force; identify prior-employer non-competes; prepare a net-worth statement; brief your lawyer on any criminal, civil or regulatory history now, because a surprise found by the buyer destroys trust.

## 13. Execution readiness: gap between plan and infrastructure

A plan states intentions; DD tests whether the business can execute them. A plan is executable when:
1. **Workflows are documented:** orders can be filled, inventory managed, returns processed and staff hired without the founder present.
2. **Financial controls exist:** pricing authority is defined and enforced; expense approval thresholds are written; bank signatories are limited; management cannot spend unilaterally.
3. **Functions are not concentrated:** losing any one person, including the founder, does not collapse the business; every critical function has a documented backup.
4. **Revenue is contractually supported:** key relationships are in contracts, not informal understandings that may not transfer.
5. **Records are current:** books closed monthly, bank reconciliations current, no six-month catch-up needed.
6. **Compliance is proactive:** licences renewed before expiry, tax filings current, no outstanding violations.

| Execution gap | What DD finds |
|---|---|
| No workflow documentation | Revenue leakage, complaints, inventory shrinkage, unfilled orders |
| No financial controls | Unauthorised price variation; unrestricted bank access; management unaware of key events |
| Key-person dependency | Departure risk with no transition plan; value sits in one person's relationships |
| Plans not backed by contracts | Projections based on informal assurances |
| Operations in the founder's head | Business cannot be replicated, scaled or transferred |
| Informal bookkeeping | Statements cannot be reconciled; tax returns diverge from management accounts; projections lack foundation |

Unrestricted access to company accounts by staff or officers is more common than expected, and undocumented pricing policy can silently cost revenue. Investors price in missing controls or treat them as deal-killing.

## 14. Uganda and East Africa context

### 14.1 Typical SME gaps and their DD consequences

| Gap | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Unregistered business or trading under a personal name | No entity to contract or hold collateral |
| No written partnership or shareholder agreement | Likely ownership disputes; exit rights undefined |
| IP informally held by the founder | Investor cannot rely on ownership; deal can collapse |
| Mobile-money records as primary books | Revenue unverifiable; no audit trail; tax status unclear |
| No employment contracts | No IP assignment, non-compete or confidentiality protection |
| Contracts oral or on messaging apps | Not assignable; terminable at will; no contractual basis for revenue |
| Land in customary tenure (agribusiness) | Title cannot be transferred or pledged |
| Outstanding PAYE, VAT or corporate tax | Lien found in DD; escrow; delay; lower net proceeds |
| No documented procedures | Not transferable; growth plan not credible |
| NSSF non-compliance | Undisclosed contingent liability; possible regulatory clearance needed |

### 14.2 Priority actions, in order of deal impact
1. Formalise the entity: incorporate with URSB or confirm good standing; obtain TIN, trading licence and EFRIS enrolment where applicable.
2. Assign all IP to the company in writing (software, brand, formulas, creative work).
3. Obtain signed invention-assignment and confidentiality agreements from all employees and contractors.
4. Review key contracts for assignability; put oral agreements in writing; renegotiate non-assignable ones before engaging investors.
5. Reconcile records to tax returns; rebuild mobile-money activity into formal accounts; file outstanding returns.
6. Write a simple manual for the three most critical processes.
7. Clear regulatory violations (KCCA notices, health certificates, UNBS compliance, environmental permits).
8. Prepare a capitalisation table; document any informally promised equity and map rights of first refusal.

### 14.3 Uganda-specific checks (verify current rules with the regulator)
- NIN or BRN gate: an identification or registration number is needed before TIN, trading licence or bank account; DD confirms the chain.
- EFRIS: URA's e-invoicing system; non-compliance creates contingent penalties (the rate changes; verify with URA).
- Land: customary tenure is not registrable title and cannot serve as collateral without conversion.
- NSSF and PAYE: arrears are treated as contingent liabilities.
- Related parties: an owner who is also landlord, main supplier and main customer requires normalised EBITDA adjustments for non-arm's-length rates.

## 15. Exclusivity (no-shop) clause and data room

**No-shop:** once DD starts, the buyer or investor typically requires the target to stop soliciting other offers. Variables to negotiate: duration (often 45 to 90 days; treat as a planning assumption); scope of prohibited solicitation and negotiation; remedy for breach (from cost reimbursement to injunction or specific performance); and whether there is a duty to negotiate in good faith or only to refrain from shopping. Sellers should seek the shortest reasonable period and a clear process if the buyer has not proceeded by the end of the window.

**Data room:** a secure electronic repository of DD documents. For an SME, a structured shared-drive folder is enough. A well-organised room signals maturity and shortens DD. Index:
1. Corporate: certificate of incorporation; operating or shareholder agreement; good standing; capitalisation table; minutes and resolutions.
2. Financial: three years of statements and tax returns; recent 12 months of management accounts; revenue by customer (five years); loan agreements.
3. Contracts: key customer, supplier, lease and licence agreements.
4. IP: invention assignments; registrations; IP licences.
5. Employment: key-staff agreements; organisation chart; NSSF and PAYE compliance; employee manual.
6. Legal: litigation summary; permits and licences; regulatory correspondence.
7. Personal DD: director CVs; background questionnaires; net-worth statements.
