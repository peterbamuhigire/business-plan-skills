# Due Diligence Reference: Workstreams, Red Flags and Investment Readiness

Use this file to pre-empt the questions an investor, lender or acquirer will ask. Read it from the sell side: each question is a gap to close in the business plan or data room before the first investor meeting. Applies to DFI, private equity, bank-loan and acquisition contexts. Legal, tax and accounting statements are general; verify with the Chwezi finance engine and local counsel.

## 1. Purpose and scope of due diligence

Due diligence (DD) is the structured investigation of a business before an acquisition, equity investment or loan. It produces three things: negotiating leverage (findings move price and terms), deal-breaker identification, and a list of fixes for the period after closing. It looks forward as well as back, because buyers pay for future profit.

DD is not an audit.

| Dimension | Statutory audit | Financial DD |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Opinion on whether accounts are true and fair | Establish underlying profit; find hidden exposures |
| Time focus | Past | Past and future |
| Scope | Fixed by law | Selected key issues |
| Access | Unrestricted | May be limited |
| Evidence | Testing of transactions and controls | Heavily interview-based |

### 1.1 Choosing coverage and depth
Decide which areas to cover and how deep to go, driven by deal size, sector risk and existing knowledge. Typical investor priority order: market and customers; management; financial records and projections; competition; product or technology; legal; environmental.

### 1.2 Workstreams and who leads them

| Workstream | Focus | Usual lead |
|---|---|---|
| Commercial | Market, competitive position, customer quality, prospects | Strategy or market specialists |
| Financial | Quality of earnings, balance sheet, cash, forecasts | Reporting accountants |
| Legal | Contracts, liabilities, IP, compliance, title | Lawyers |
| HR | Terms, culture, key-person risk, pensions and social security | HR specialists or lawyers |
| Management | Capability, depth, succession | Independent assessors |
| Tax | Liabilities, compliance, VAT, payroll taxes | Tax specialists |
| Environmental | Contamination, permits, remediation cost | Environmental consultants |
| IT | Adequacy, scalability, cyber risk, data and software ownership | IT specialists |
| Pensions and retirement funds | Funding, hidden liabilities | Actuaries |

Deal-specific non-financial exposures that most often become deal breakers: incompatible IT systems, onerous or undisclosed contract commitments, promised changes to employment terms, redundancy exposure, hedging and financial instruments, environmental matters, legal non-compliance, and outstanding litigation.

### 1.3 Planning assumptions for timing (re-verify per deal)
Commercial DD commonly takes a few weeks and can precede heads of terms; financial and legal DD run in parallel for a month or two; a full programme often takes one to three months. State these as assumptions in the plan's readiness timeline.

## 2. Commercial DD (highest priority)

Commercial DD tests markets and competitive position, then converts them into a view of future prospects that feeds the forecast and valuation.

```
Market analysis + Competitive position -> Prospects -> Forecast -> Valuation
```

Aims: reduce risk (is revenue threatened by customer migration?), support valuation (is the growth rate in the DCF market-based?), and plan integration (what must be fixed to capture the benefits?).

### 2.1 Market and position assessment grid
Assess each factor separately for its effect on price (margin) and on volume.

Price, market factors: rivalry (number of firms, relative shares, differentiation, life-cycle stage, supply-demand balance, fixed-cost intensity, exit barriers); buyer power (size, concentration, profitability, cost of your product relative to their total cost, importance to their own product, ease of backward integration, switching cost); threat of entry (know-how cost, technology, capital cost, experience effects, scale economies, commoditisation).

Price, competitive position: relative share; rationality of competitors; performance against customers' purchase criteria; product strength; distribution strength (delivery reliability, distributor network, sales force, back office, location, customer relationships); brand; relative cost base and proprietary know-how.

Volume, market factors: current share, market growth, product life-cycle stage, threat of new products, technology shifts, cyclicality, regulatory change.

Volume, competitive position: segments served and unserved; new territories, applications and products.

### 2.2 Key purchase criteria (KPC) scoring
Procedure:
1. Interview past, current and lost customers to list the criteria they use to choose a supplier.
2. Ask each to weight the criteria (weights sum to 100%).
3. Ask each to score the target and each named competitor per criterion (for example 1 to 10).
4. Weighted score = sum of (weight x score). Compare totals and read the pattern by criterion.

Read the result by criterion: where the target leads, where it trails, and whether the leading criteria carry high weights. A plan claiming market leadership must show KPC evidence that supports the claim. Illustrative reading: a target that is outscored on price (the heaviest weight) but leads on reliability (a low weight) is not a market leader whatever management says.

### 2.3 Information sources (roughly in order of value)
Customers (past, present, prospective); competitors; former employees; industry observers (trade press, academics, consultants); suppliers and corollary suppliers; distributors; specifiers (who influences specification in construction or IT); regulators; market leavers (why they exited); pressure groups. Secondary sources (trade journals, broker and market-research reports) are cheaper but dated and rarely niche enough; cite the publication and date.

### 2.4 Outputs
A commercial DD report contains: facts and analysis (market size and growth, KPC scores, position, distribution, management view); opinions (sales-growth and pricing prospects); conclusions (the forward narrative supporting the DCF, deal risks). The closing question: what does the future hold for this business, and for the deal?

## 3. Financial DD

Objectives: verify the numbers behind the offer; find deal breakers; supply negotiating evidence; give confidence in underlying performance and future profit.

### 3.1 Maintainable (underlying) profit
Maintainable profit is what the business can repeatably earn, stripped of distortions. This is the concept usually called quality of earnings (QoE). Adjust for: group or management charges; owner personal expenses run through the business; reorganisation and transfer provisions; intra-group transfer pricing; one-off income and expenditure; disposals of fixed assets; pension accounting; insurance claims; stock write-offs; changes in accounting policy. Show each adjustment transparently in the financial section.

### 3.2 Profit and loss manipulation: red-flag table

| Pattern | What it signals |
|---|---|
| Sales pushed into the channel before sale ("channel stuffing") | Credit notes and returns rise, and sales fall after the deal |
| Sales reliant on pre-sale effort, not contracts | Sales may fall when the push stops |
| Pre-deal sales drive has failed (for example, added advertising did not lift sales) | The business is less attractive than presented |
| Revenue recognised ahead of matching costs (long contracts) | Early revenue, later loss |
| Depreciation set too low | Assets need replacing soon; profit will fall |
| Costs deferred in closing stock | See stock table below |
| Costs capitalised (R&D, interest, own labour) | Later charge to the P&L |
| Few suppliers on pre-sale terms | Terms may be renegotiated after a change of ownership |
| Labour cost held artificially low | Promised pay rises, unpaid bonuses, social-security arrears |
| Looming liabilities not provided for | Loss recognised only when realised |

### 3.3 Balance sheet review

Fixed assets: are intangibles reasonably valued; is the composition understood; are asset registers kept; are there recent independent valuations; are depreciation policies reasonable and consistent; is title clear; has interest or own labour been capitalised; what near-term capital expenditure is needed.

Stock: test overhead absorption and allocation (wrong conversion cost overstates margin); year-end build-up that defers expense; production inefficiencies hidden in stock; obsolete or slow-moving stock under-provided; non-existent stock (fraud or deterioration). Require physical verification, count-loss history and valuation basis.

Receivables: age profile; credit-term system and enforcement; basis of bad-debt reserves; bad-debt history; exposure to a large debtor defaulting.

Payables and liabilities: special credit terms; creditor pressure; long-term liabilities (hire purchase, finance leases); shareholder loans repayable short term; adequacy of provisions; warranty provision against product history; deferred income calculation.

### 3.4 Cash flow
Reported profit and cash generation must reconcile. Check capital expenditure against maintenance need, working-capital absorption by growth (debtors rising faster than creditors), overdraft-limit breaches, exceptional inflows and outflows, and the main uses of operating cash. Test whether year-end cash is representative of the month or year.

### 3.5 Forecast testing
Forecasts of the "hockey stick" shape are the norm, and sell-side forecasts more so. Test: arithmetical accuracy; reasonableness of assumptions; consistency of accounting policies with history; past forecast accuracy; consistency with current management accounts; key sensitivities and vulnerabilities; whether growth is volume- or price-led; whether margin changes are backed by known price rises, cost cuts or firm contracts; whether growth relies on share gains, new products, markets or customers; whether projected cuts in stock or debtor days or rises in creditor days are justified; whether savings depend on actions not yet taken; whether the Board approved the forecast; whether it was built for the DD or is the normal budget.

### 3.6 Long-form financial DD report: coverage
History; organisation and structure; strategy; markets and competitors; sales and marketing; purchasing; production; premises; HR; information systems; accounting policies; management information and controls; budgeting; three to six years of trading; balance sheets; cash flows; tax position; prospects; other matters (litigation, contingent liabilities, capital commitments). The business plan is the self-authored equivalent and should anticipate each heading.

## 4. Legal DD

Objectives: uncover actual, future and contingent liabilities; find legal or contractual obstacles to the deal; provide the basis for the sale and purchase agreement.

- **Warranty:** a seller's statement of fact; a breach lets the buyer claim damages or adjust price retrospectively. The disclosure letter doubles as an information dump.
- **Indemnity:** a guaranteed remedy for a specific liability (tax, environmental clean-up, pending litigation), regardless of effect on value.
- **Other protections after adverse findings:** price adjustment; retention from price; earn-out; third-party guarantees; insurance; asset rather than share purchase; exclusion of assets or liabilities; rectification at the seller's cost.
- **Misstatement risk:** misleading forecasts, promises or statements in an information memorandum can create criminal or civil exposure (in the UK under the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000; in Uganda under company and general fraud law; verify current provisions). Assert only what can be supported.

## 5. HR DD

Employee problems contribute to many failed acquisitions, and "soft" factors (team selection, culture, communication) often affect outcomes more than the "hard" ones (synergy models, DD itself). Objectives: find deal breakers (unfunded pensions, redundancy exposure); quantify liabilities; assess cultural cost and risk; list integration issues.

Investigate: organisation chart (documented, current); directors and senior managers (experience, qualifications, duties, pay, service agreements, benefits, pension); all staff (age, start date, service, pay, benefits, notice, department, location); dismissals, redundancies, disciplinary and grievance record over six months; staff turnover and trend against the industry; union agreements and membership; pay structure and grading; personnel policies and handbooks; holiday pay; promised changes to terms or pending pay rises; training spend; skill-shortage threats to growth; regular consultants and agents; pension or retirement-fund funding; industrial-relations record; staff on long leave or secondment.

## 6. Management DD

Management usually drives investment decisions more than any other factor, and key managers often leave within a few years of a deal (treat published departure rates as an assumption to re-verify). Assess before investing.

Appraisal methods: document analysis (CVs, evaluations); structured past-behaviour interviews; multi-source references (customers, analysts, colleagues); work samples (hypothetical cases such as integrating two companies); observation of the team at work.

Competencies to assess: strategic thinking; leadership; commercial and customer orientation; financial literacy; communication and influence; adaptability; integrity and governance.

Questions: who are the key people and what if one leaves; is there a succession plan; do management meetings happen and get minuted; are strategic goals clear; are senior performance metrics documented; are incentives aligned (options, bonuses, retention); what is management's reputation with customers and suppliers.

## 7. Tax DD

Use a tax specialist alongside the financial team. Objectives: review tax affairs, test adequacy of provisions, assess risk of new liabilities.

Checklist: tax provisions for three to six years; reconciliation of the accounting tax charge to statutory rates; deferred-tax policy; computations agreed with the revenue authority and open disputes; years still open to assessment; avoidance schemes; timeliness of payment and any penalties; VAT returns and accounts (including bad-debt relief), payroll-tax and social-security accounts, no default notices; control visits and investigations; intra-group and non-arm's-length transactions and transfer-pricing policy; special arrangements and compliance with them.

Uganda: typical adverse findings are e-invoicing (EFRIS) non-compliance (penalty rates change; verify with URA), PAYE arrears and withholding-tax disputes. DFIs commonly require a tax clearance certificate before disbursement.

## 8. Environmental and IT DD

Environmental (critical in manufacturing, extractives, agriculture, chemicals): past assessments; notices of claims, violations or prosecutions; employee or insurance claims; correspondence with regulators; directors' awareness of problems; site evaluations and filings on hazardous or toxic substances. Uganda: NEMA permits and an Environmental and Social Management Plan (ESMP) are common lender requirements above thresholds that vary by lender; confirm the threshold with the lender.

IT: timeliness and reliability of management information; integration of systems; dependence on third-party maintenance; weaknesses flagged in auditors' management letters; sole ownership of software and technology and non-infringement; KPIs used; cash-management systems; inventory of main systems and maintenance contracts; software ownership; back-up arrangements; fitness for present and future needs; consultancy reports on internal controls.

## 9. Information request checklist by area

Use this as the data-room index. Each area lists what an investor will ask to see.

**Financial and accounting**
- Auditors' management letters (last two years); accounting manuals; key policies and any recent changes and their effect on profit.
- Audited accounts (three years); latest management accounts; year-end journals and the reconciliation between management and statutory accounts.
- Capital: current capex budget; capex over the last three years and expected in the next three; capital commitments; contingent liabilities.
- Cash: monthly bank and cash-book position (current and two prior years); latest bank reconciliation with statement; explanation of major and intra-month variations (seasonality); exceptional flows; principal uses of operating cash.
- Working capital: representativeness of balance-sheet cash; overdraft facilities; stock and work-in-progress valuation basis; recent physical verification and loss levels; stock movements and reasons; latest aged debtors and trend; large-debtor vulnerability; bad-debt provisions; aged creditors; unusual accruals; pressure to pay faster.
- Debt: all facilities (amount, repayment, conversion, rate, covenants, security); charges over assets; lender correspondence; guarantees or indemnities between insiders and the company or given to third parties; short-term shareholder loans; government grants; fit of facilities to cash needs.
- Projections: see section 3.5; budgets regular, variances tracked and updated; current-year budget and trading estimate; one to two year P&L and cash projections with assumptions; three to five year forecast; prior forecast accuracy; sensitivity to external factors.

**Legal and corporate**
- Corporate records: exact name and address; constitutional documents and amendments; certificate of incorporation; statutory books; legal structure and beneficial owners; board, committee and shareholder minutes; material information given to shareholders and directors (two years); registrar filings; recorded charges; corporate-governance compliance.
- Agreements: major customers; distribution, agency and franchise; warranties; suppliers and subcontractors; partnerships and joint ventures; sponsorship; property leases and maintenance; standard service and employee terms, unions, management contracts; acquisitions or disposals (six years); hire purchase, leasing and loans; IP licensing.
- Consents: licences, permits and authorisations; filings with regulators; regulatory matters (quotas, health and safety, consumer credit, licensing, price control).
- Disputes and litigation: disputes with customers, suppliers, subcontractors, employees, neighbours; employment claims; actual, threatened or pending litigation; administrative proceedings and government inquiries; complaint correspondence; litigation frequency.
- Insurance: property, liability, operations and product cover; key-person and director cover; recent review of cover levels.
- Intellectual property: patents, trademarks, copyright (countries and dates); domain names and unregistered rights; sufficiency of protection; restrictions; trade secrets; licences and NDAs; challenges and infringement claims; protection procedures; R&D and patentability; renewal fees; IP clauses in employment contracts.
- Property: premises (address, use, tenure, size, dilapidations, rent and rates, valuations); valuation against book value; appraisals; spare land; future needs.

**Commercial and operational**
- Customers: concentration; satisfaction evidence; major wins and losses (three years); special terms; management's KPC view; agreements with major customers and summaries; trade-association memberships.
- Sales: mix changes; returns, guarantee and warranty claims; non-recurring revenue; price pressure; discounting and changes in its basis; seasonality; exchange-rate effects; related-party sales.
- Markets and competitors: shares, profiles, winners and losers and why, technology leaders; management's market view (size, growth, new segments, price sensitivity, drivers, entry barriers, R&D needs, regulatory change, competitive forces, cyclicality, economic sensitivity).
- Distribution: licensing and distribution agreements; selling agents and goods they sell.
- Strategy: existing plans; the essence of the strategy; who sets it; last update; recent change; external adviser input.
- Operations: production methods relative to the leading edge; recent problems; capacity constraints; immediate capex.
- Purchasing: supplier concentration; alternatives for single-sourced inputs; recent supply disruption; subcontractor alternatives; price stability; unusual payment terms; forward commitments; volume rebates; non-arm's-length purchases.

**People**: see section 5 and the management questions in section 6 (former directors and executives who left in three years; management succession plans).

**Tax**: see section 7.

## 10. Red flags and deal breakers

**Financial:** aged receivables unprovided (over about 90 days) or concentrated in one debtor; understated liabilities (thin warranty provisions, undisclosed contingencies); pension under-funding; aggressive revenue recognition; channel stuffing (credit notes spike after DD); unsustainably low depreciation; working-capital window dressing at year end; related-party dealings off arm's length (owner draws disguised as management charges); open tax years, assessments or under-declared VAT; hockey-stick forecasts with no market evidence.

**Commercial:** high customer concentration without long-term contracts (a common test is the top three customers above 60% of revenue; treat as a planning threshold); no evidence of leadership on any purchase criterion; structural decline masked by one-off events; lost accounts not replaced; discounting as the main weapon (commodity positioning); single-source key inputs; management claims contradicted by customer interviews.

**Legal and regulatory:** undisclosed litigation; IP owned by individuals rather than the company or unregistered; licences not transferable; employment contracts unwritten or non-compliant; environmental violations; data-protection breaches; shares held by undisclosed parties or encumbered.

**Management:** key-person dependency; no second tier; high turnover; no articulated strategy; no board minutes; incentives misaligned with investors; resistance to information requests (a universal red flag).

**Material adverse change (MAC):** clauses let an investor withdraw between signing and closing after a significant performance deterioration, loss of a major customer or contract, regulatory action or licence suspension, departure of key management, or material litigation. Apply the same logic to the risk section: identify and mitigate these events before asking for funding.

## 11. Sell-side preparation (investment readiness)

Run your own DD first (vendor due diligence, VDD): an independent review commissioned by the seller and shareable with buyers. It speeds the process, controls information flow, allows fixes before buyers find issues and reduces late surprises. For a Ugandan business seeking DFI or PE funding, an independent business review by a reputable local accounting firm is the practical equivalent.

### 11.1 Documents to have ready

Tier 1, before the first meeting: three years of audited statements (or audited management accounts if younger); management accounts under about 60 days old; a business plan with sourced assumptions; incorporation documents, articles and share register; registrar good-standing evidence (URSB); tax clearance certificate (URA); all operating licences and permits; ownership or lease evidence for premises and key assets; written contracts for key staff.

Tier 2, during DD: top five customer contracts; key supplier contracts; IP registrations (URSB, ARIPO where relevant); 24 months of bank statements; aged debtor and creditor schedules; fixed-asset register; insurance schedule; organisation chart; director and senior-manager CVs; succession plan.

Tier 3, DFI-specific: ESMP where there is environmental impact; board and independent-governance documentation (thresholds vary by lender); gender and social-inclusion data (women, youth, vulnerable-group sourcing); development-impact narrative (SDGs, jobs, sector contribution); collateral documents (titles, valuations, pledges).

Data-room principle: disclose as much non-sensitive material as possible early; hold genuinely sensitive material until a shortlist exists.

### 11.2 Management presentation order
1. Business overview and model. 2. Market evidence, including KPC scoring against competitors. 3. Financial performance: maintainable earnings, working-capital trends, cash conversion. 4. Forecast and assumptions, acknowledging and justifying any hockey stick. 5. Management team: qualifications, record, personal stake. 6. Use of funds, costed and traceable. 7. Return or exit: equity return and exit route; debt service cover (DSCR) and collateral cover.

Credibility tests investors apply: internal consistency (does market growth support the revenue forecast); assumption grounding (source of market size); honesty (risks disclosed); depth (each manager can answer for their domain); commercial awareness (you know competitors' KPC scores).

### 11.3 Claims that fail DD

| Claim | Likely finding | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "The market is worth X and growing at Y%" | No source | Cite UBOS, sector reports or UDB data; define the addressable segment |
| "We have no significant competitors" | Competitors exist and win customers | Document them; state your differentiation |
| "Projections are conservative" | Margins exceed industry benchmarks | Reconcile to norms or explain the structural premium |
| "Strong customer relationships" | Top three customers dominate revenue, no contracts | Provide letters of intent or framework agreements and relationship history |
| "Management has 30 years' combined experience" | CVs do not show relevant experience; no second tier | Show specific track record; document succession |
| "We comply with all regulation" | No tax clearance; social-security arrears; unlicensed premises | Clean up before DD |
| "Strong cash flow" | Profit and cash diverge | Reconcile profit to cash and explain working-capital investment |

### 11.4 Five findings that most often stall Ugandan deals
1. Unaudited financials, or accounts that do not reconcile to tax returns.
2. Key-person dependency with no second tier.
3. Customer concentration without contracts (evidence of repeat purchase and written documentation is the minimum).
4. Missing compliance: NEMA permits, UNBS certification where required, EFRIS.
5. Hockey-stick forecasts detached from market share and competitive strategy.

### 11.5 Investment-readiness score
Rate each dimension 1 to 5 (1 very weak, 3 adequate, 5 investment-ready). This scale is the engine's own planning device.

| Dimension | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial records | Unaudited, incomplete | Two years audited plus current management accounts | Three years audited, monthly management accounts, tax reconciliation |
| Market evidence | Undocumented estimates | Industry data cited | Primary research (customer interviews, KPC scoring) plus secondary sources |
| Customer quality | Oral, undocumented | Written contracts for major customers | Long-term contracts, diversified, satisfaction measured |
| Management depth | Founder only | Founder plus one or two functional managers | Documented team, CVs, org chart, succession plan |
| Regulatory compliance | Multiple gaps | Minor gaps being closed | Full compliance documented; clearance certificate, permits, social security current |
| Plan assumptions | Unsourced | Partly sourced | Every assumption sourced, sensitivity analysis provided |

Total 24 to 30: ready to approach investors. 18 to 23: conditional; close gaps first. Below 18: preparation stage, typically 6 to 18 months (planning assumption).

### 11.6 Briefing checklist before a formal investor presentation
- Plan and model final and internally consistent; all assumptions sourced.
- Management briefed and rehearsed on their domains.
- Data room assembled and indexed; confidentiality agreement template ready.
- Reasons for seeking investment stated, beyond "we need money"; strategic rationale (what the investment enables that organic growth cannot).
- Post-investment plans: asset acquisition, expansion milestones, hiring.
- Investor's role in governance; information you will provide after investment.
- Investor's existing sector knowledge (what to explain versus assume); audience for DD reports (board, bankers, DFI committee).
- Materiality threshold for proactive disclosure; stance on risk (conservative or aggressive assumptions).

## 13. Terms

| Term | Meaning | Plan implication |
|---|---|---|
| Maintainable profit | Underlying profit after removing one-off and owner-distorted items | Show normalised earnings and each adjustment |
| Quality of earnings | How reliably profit is recurring and converts to cash | Show EBITDA-to-cash conversion; split contract from spot revenue |
| Hockey stick | Forecast growth far above history | Justify each inflection with evidence or a named catalyst |
| Key purchase criteria | Weighted factors customers use to choose | Document your advantage in the competitive section |
| Customer concentration risk | Dependence on few customers | Show diversification and durable relationships |
| Material adverse change | Post-signing event that materially worsens prospects | Cover in risk analysis with contingencies |
| Vendor due diligence | Seller-commissioned DD shared with buyers | Commission an independent review before approaching DFIs |
| Long-form report | Main financial DD output | The plan is your equivalent and must anticipate it |
| Deal breaker | Finding serious enough to stop the deal | Fix before approaching investors |
| Indemnity | Guaranteed remedy for a specific liability | Disclose known liabilities and document provisions |
