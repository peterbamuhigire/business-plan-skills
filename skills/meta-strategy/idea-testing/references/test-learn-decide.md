# Test Cards, Learning Cards, Evidence Strength and Decisions

Use this reference to run idea testing as a disciplined cycle: map assumptions, write a test card before each experiment, record a learning card after it, grade the evidence and decide. The card format follows the test-card and learning-card approach of Bland and Osterwalder (Testing Business Ideas, 2020); the fields are restated here in the engine's own wording.

## 1. Assumptions mapping

Before choosing experiments, list every assumption behind the idea and rank by risk.

Step 1: list assumptions and ask of each "if this is false, does it kill the idea?"
- Desirability: customers have the problem; it is painful enough to pay to solve; the solution addresses the key pain; customers will change their current behaviour.
- Feasibility: inputs can be sourced at the assumed cost; the service can be delivered at the assumed quality; the technology or process is possible; the skills can be hired locally.
- Viability: customers will pay the assumed price; enough customers exist for the target revenue; unit economics are positive (revenue per customer exceeds cost per customer); the business scales without proportional cost growth.

Step 2: score each assumption.
- Importance: 1 (low) to 3 (high).
- Evidence held: 1 (none) to 3 (strong).
- Priority = importance x (4 - evidence). The higher the score, the sooner it is tested.

Step 3: test in priority order. Do not build until the top five to ten assumptions have at least moderate evidence.

## 2. Test card (complete before running the experiment)

Fields: idea or hypothesis area; experiment number; date; owner; then four statements.
- We believe: one specific, falsifiable hypothesis naming the segment, behaviour and context. Example: small retailers in a named market will pay a stated monthly fee for an inventory-tracking service delivered by messaging app.
- To verify this, we will: the exact experiment. Example: send a broadcast to 200 named traders directing them to a simple page with a pay-now option.
- We will measure: the metrics collected. Example: visits, clicks on "subscribe", completed mobile-money payments.
- We are right if: a pass or fail threshold set in advance. Example: at least ten traders (5 percent) pay within 14 days.

Rules: one hypothesis per card; specific enough to fail ("customers will love it" is not); set criteria before seeing results.

## 3. Learning card (complete after the experiment)

Fields: link to the test card; date; owner; then four statements.
- We observed: raw facts without interpretation. Example: 87 visits, 12 clicks on subscribe, 3 completed payments.
- From this we learned: what the data means for the hypothesis. Example: 3 percent conversion is below the 5 percent threshold, but exit interviews show traders want weekly billing, so price framing rather than value is the problem.
- Therefore we will: the specific next action. Example: retest with weekly billing and hold five more interviews.
- We still need to validate: the open hypotheses. Example: whether traders will pay weekly and whether collection through mobile-money agents is feasible.

## 4. Evidence strength

Four dimensions from weaker to stronger:
- Type: opinions and self-reports, then observable behaviour.
- Behaviour: what people say they would do, then what they actually did.
- Volume: one data point, then many independent ones.
- Method: one experiment type, then several converging types.

### Confidence levels

| Level | Typical evidence | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Low | One to three conversations; unstructured opinions; a survey with a leading question; your own assumption stated as fact | Do not decide or build; run more experiments |
| Medium | 15 to 20 or more interviews with consistent themes; clicks showing interest by action; prototype task-completion data; a survey of 50 or more with pre-set criteria | Proceed to the next hypothesis or raise fidelity |
| High | Real payments (even small); signed letters of intent from business buyers; several experiment types agreeing; sustained behaviour across repeat interactions | Persevere and invest; the hypothesis is validated |

### Evidence by experiment type

| Experiment | Evidence | Typical strength |
|---|---|---|
| Customer interview | Opinions, stated intent | Weak to moderate |
| Discovery survey | Opinions at scale | Weak to moderate |
| Online ad (clicks only) | Interest | Moderate |
| Email or broadcast (open and click) | Interest | Moderate |
| Landing page sign-up | Interest and intent | Moderate |
| Prototype feedback | Usability, stated intent | Moderate |
| Validation survey | Stated intent at scale | Moderate |
| Mock sale (click to buy) | Purchase-intent action | Moderate to strong |
| Presale (payment collected) | Real behaviour | Strong |
| Concierge (delivery and satisfaction) | Real behaviour | Strong |
| Letter of intent (signed) | Business-buyer commitment | Strong |
| Crowdfunding (funded) | Real payment | Strong |
| Single-feature product with payment | Real purchase | Very strong |

## 5. Decide

After each learning card, choose one:
1. Persevere: evidence clearly supports the hypothesis with high confidence. Test the next riskiest hypothesis or raise fidelity.
2. Pivot: evidence refutes the hypothesis but points to a better direction. Pivot types: customer segment (same solution, different customer), value proposition (same customer, different core benefit), pricing (different price point or billing model), channel (different distribution), revenue model (different monetisation).
3. Kill: repeated experiments refute desirability, feasibility is impossible, or no viable revenue model appears despite thorough testing. Kill early and cheaply: a failed experiment costing a small sum beats a failed business costing a very large one.
4. Continue testing: evidence is mixed or the sample too small. Re-run with a different or higher-fidelity experiment.

## 6. Experiment board

Track experiments on a board visible to the team with columns: backlog, running, analysing, decided (with the decision recorded beside each card). Limit to two or three experiments running at once; finishing experiments matters more than starting new ones.

## 7. Plan-section prompts

- Which are the top five assumptions and their priority scores?
- What did each experiment observe, learn and decide?
- What evidence level supports the recommendation (persevere, pivot, kill)?
- What remains untested and what is the next experiment?

Sources consulted: Bland, D. and Osterwalder, A., Testing Business Ideas (Wiley, 2020).
