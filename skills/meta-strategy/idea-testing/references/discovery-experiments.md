# Discovery Experiments: Learning Whether the Problem Is Real

Use this reference at the start of idea testing, when there is an idea but no proof that real people have the problem. Discovery experiments are cheap and fast and produce directional evidence, not proof. Run several before concluding anything, then escalate to validation-experiments.md. Record each with the cards in test-learn-decide.md.

Goals: expose the riskiest desirability assumptions; understand customer jobs, pains and gains; shape the value proposition before investing in delivery. The catalogue draws on the experiment-library approach of Bland and Osterwalder (Testing Business Ideas, 2020), reorganised here by the job each technique does.

Evidence strength used below: weak (opinions, few people), moderate (observed behaviour or action at scale), strong (payment or commitment; see validation-experiments.md).

## 1. Choosing a technique

| Question you need answered | Techniques |
|---|---|
| What jobs, pains and gains do customers have? | Customer interviews, observation, forums, card sorting, speed boat |
| Can partners and suppliers deliver? | Partner and supplier interviews |
| What does the market already know and do? | Expert interviews, search trends, web traffic, sales-team and support analysis |
| How many people share the problem and how do they rank it? | Discovery survey, card sorting |
| Does the message attract attention? | Online ad, link tracking, email or messaging broadcast, social campaign, explainer video, brochure |
| Will customers recommend it? | Referral programme test |
| Which features matter most? | Product box, buy-a-feature, storyboard, paper prototype |

## 2. Qualitative exploration

### Customer interviews
Purpose: learn jobs, pains and gains in depth. Evidence: weak to moderate.
1. Recruit about 15 to 20 target customers (not friends or family).
2. Write an open script about behaviour, not your product.
3. Hold 30 to 45-minute sessions; one person asks, one records verbatim notes.
4. Debrief straight after each session.
5. After the set, affinity-sort responses by theme and rank the top three jobs, pains and gains by frequency.
6. Update the value proposition canvas.
Useful prompts: "Walk me through the last time you dealt with [problem]." "What is the most frustrating part?" "What do you do now and how is it working?" "What would have to be true for you to switch?"
Do not pitch; you are learning. Local adaptation: in-person visits to workshops, stalls and offices are expected; warm introductions raise participation. Metrics: number of interviews; share mentioning each pain unprompted; emotional strength.

### Partner and supplier interviews
Purpose: test feasibility. Ask whether they can supply the assumed volume and quality, their real costs and lead times, minimum orders and conditions, and who else competes for their capacity. Identify key input suppliers, agencies and distribution partners early; their constraints often define feasible models.

### Expert interviews
Purpose: quick domain knowledge on regulation, market structure, competition and technical needs. Recruit five to ten experts (veterans, officials, academics, successful competitors). Prompts: "What do you wish you had known at the start?" "Who succeeds here and why?" "Why do businesses in this sector fail?" Local sources: sector associations, standards-body technical officers, licensing staff.

### Day-in-the-life observation
Purpose: see what customers do rather than say; uncover workarounds and hidden jobs. Evidence: moderate.
1. Get permission to shadow a customer through a working day.
2. Observe without intervening; note behaviour, workarounds, inefficiencies and frustrations.
3. Ask clarifying "why" questions afterwards.
4. Look for manual workarounds that could be automated, processes that differ from what was described, and improvised resources.
Especially valuable in informal businesses, where owners often cannot state their pain until observed.

### Discovery survey
Purpose: directional quantification of a problem. Evidence: weak to moderate.
1. Use open or rank-order questions, never leading ones.
2. Ask about current behaviour, not hypothetical future behaviour.
3. Recruit at least about 50 respondents from the segment.
4. Count response frequency to find the top jobs, pains and gains.
5. Use it to prioritise, not to validate.
Ask: what tools or methods do you use for [job]? rank these pains; how often does the problem occur and what does it cost in time? Avoid "would you use" and "would you pay", which overstate willingness. A messaging-app poll of five to seven questions often reaches small-business owners better than an online form.

### Discussion forums
Purpose: read candid complaints without being in the room. Evidence: moderate (authentic and unprompted).
Sources: social-network groups, messaging-group administrators, professional-network groups, sector communities, product reviews on marketplaces.
Procedure: identify three to five communities; search pain-related terms; note common complaints, the customers' language, the solutions they compare and what they wish existed. Observe; do not pitch.

## 3. Data analysis

### Search trend analysis
Purpose: gauge demand and vocabulary. Evidence: moderate.
1. Use a trends tool for relative volume and trajectory of problem keywords.
2. Use a keyword planner for volume and competition.
3. Restrict to the country or region.
4. Look for growing searches, high volume unserved by local suppliers and seasonality.
Use for market-size estimates, proof the problem is actively felt, and copy vocabulary. Local search volumes are lower than in large markets; a few hundred monthly searches may be a significant market. Search local-language variants too.

### Web traffic analysis
For an existing site: install analytics; find top landing pages, search queries and exit pages; compare time on page across value-proposition wordings; track visitors, pages per session, bounce and conversion events. Only useful with meaningful traffic.

### Sales-team feedback
For firms with a sales team: mine near-misses (why they almost bought and did not), top three feature requests, common objections and quotes about what nearly stopped a purchase. Second-hand and weak but efficient; suits new lines or segments.

### Customer support analysis
Analyse tickets, complaints and feedback for repeated complaints, feature requests clustering around one job, and customer language about needs. Weak to moderate.

## 4. Testing interest at scale

### Online ad test
Purpose: test whether a value proposition attracts attention; test messaging, do not sell. Evidence: moderate (interest, not intent).
Metric: click-through rate; a working early-stage bar is roughly 1 to 2 percent, but benchmark your sector.
1. Define the audience on a social or search ad platform.
2. Create two or three variants with different value statements.
3. Send clicks to a simple landing page.
4. Run seven to fourteen days on a small budget.
5. Compare variants, kill weak ones, iterate on the winner.
Social ad platforms dominate local digital advertising; target by location, interest and job title.

### Link tracking
Track clicks on a specific link in context to test call-to-action copy, subject lines or button placement. Use tagged links and a link-shortener or messaging-link tracker; tag every link shared in broadcasts and compare framings.

### Email or messaging broadcast
Purpose: test whether a value message drives action from a warm audience. Evidence: moderate (opens and clicks), moderate to strong (conversions).
Typical targets for a warm list (verify): opens about 20 to 30 percent, clicks about 2 to 5 percent of opens, conversion about 1 to 5 percent of sends.
1. Build a warm list.
2. Write a subject line stating value, not features.
3. One call to action per message.
4. Send and measure opens, clicks, conversions.
5. Iterate on the weakest element.
Messaging-app broadcasts often outperform email locally; keep messages short, send mid-morning on weekdays, and do not broadcast more than weekly without consent. A community-list test of a new product idea can confirm interest with a few hundred subscribers in weeks.

### Social media campaign
Purpose: build awareness and test what content resonates. Evidence: engagement weak, conversions moderate to strong.
Metrics: views, reach, shares and comments (read the comments), link click-through, conversion.
1. Set the goal (awareness, leads or sales).
2. Calendar at least two to three posts a week.
3. Post for four to eight weeks before concluding.
4. Compare content by shares, clicks and conversions.
5. Double down on the best format.
Channel fit: the leading social network for most consumers and small businesses, short-video platforms for younger people, the professional network for services, visual platforms for fashion, food and hospitality.

### Referral programme
Proxy for product-market fit. Metrics and starting targets (verify): advocate share rate about 15 to 20 percent; friend click-through about 50 to 80 percent; friend conversion about 5 to 15 percent. Word of mouth already drives most growth locally, so trackable referral codes shared by messaging can quantify it.

## 5. Discussion prototypes and workshops

### Paper prototype
Sketch screens or steps; simulate the experience with five to ten target customers; one guides, one records; observe confusion and excitement; do not explain or defend. Near-zero cost; a few hours. Evidence: weak to moderate.

### Storyboard
Each team member sketches eight to twelve alternative scenarios of the customer experiencing the value proposition; show them to 15 to 20 customers; note which excite; update the canvas.

### Explainer video
A 60 to 90-second video; measure completion, shares and click-through to sign-up. Voice-over in a local language often lifts engagement with non-English-dominant audiences.

### Product box
Give groups of 15 to 20 customers cardboard boxes and markers; ask them to design the box for the product they wish existed (messages, features, benefits); each group pitches it to you as a sceptical customer. Output: top three jobs, pains, gains and message ideas. Works as a community workshop (associations, cooperatives, church groups), framed as a business development session.

### Speed boat
For an existing product or service: draw a boat; 15 to 20 customers write "anchors" (things slowing them down) on cards under the boat, deeper meaning more severe; cluster anchors, rate severity and urgency; prioritise experiments on the most severe and frequent anchors.

### Card sorting
Cards for each known job, pain and gain plus blank cards; 15 to 20 customers sort and rank from most to least important; cluster to find the top three of each.

### Buy a feature
List 15 to 30 features with imaginary prices (higher for complex); give each customer limited play money; they may pool money for expensive features; count investment per feature. Output: a prioritised feature list grounded in real trade-offs.

### Brochure
A printed or digital brochure testing whether the proposition is communicable; use A5 or A4 flyers at trade fairs, association meetings and markets; compare versions by inbound enquiries. Evidence: weak.

## 6. Summary of evidence and sequencing

- Interviews, surveys, workshops: weak to moderate.
- Observation, forums, search and traffic data, ad and broadcast clicks: moderate.
- Anything involving payment or signed commitment belongs to validation-experiments.md.
Sequence: interviews and observation, then survey and data analysis, then interest tests, then move to validation once the top jobs, pains and gains are stable.

Sources consulted: Bland, D. and Osterwalder, A., Testing Business Ideas (Wiley, 2020), and general customer-development practice. Local applications are the engine's own; thresholds are planning assumptions.
