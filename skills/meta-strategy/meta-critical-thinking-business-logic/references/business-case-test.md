When to read: whenever a plan recommends a material initiative — a new digital platform, ERP or CRM rollout, automation or AI programme, factory or capacity expansion, market entry, or product launch — or a funding ask depends on one.

# Business-Case Test

A business case is a disciplined justification for an investment, not a slogan or a foregone conclusion. This test paraphrases methods from Haines, S. (2022) *How to Create a Business Case* (publisher not recorded at ingestion; confirm before citing externally). It is used by the financial-projections, funding-request, digital-transformation, strategic-optionality, living-plan, and ICT skills.

## 1. Start with the problem and the decision

Before any solution, state:

- the problem or opportunity, with evidence;
- why it matters now (the timing trigger);
- how it fits the strategy;
- the exact funding or approval requested.

Stop if the draft opens with the solution and cannot state the problem independently of it.

## 2. Compare options, including doing nothing

| Option | Required content |
|---|---|
| Do nothing | Cost, risk, and revenue trajectory if the status quo continues |
| Low-cost, low-risk option | Smallest credible step, often a pilot or manual process |
| Preferred option | The recommendation |
| High-ambition option (when relevant) | What more investment would buy, and why it is not chosen now |

The do-nothing case guards against technology theatre and premature investment in systems or facilities before demand justifies them.

## 3. Use incremental economics

For each option, show only the change caused by the initiative:

- incremental revenue;
- incremental operating costs;
- capital expenditure;
- cash-flow timing by month or quarter;
- payback period;
- break-even point;
- net present value or equivalent return measure when the size and time span justify it.

Link every line of the use-of-funds table to an incremental benefit and a date. Where collections are slow or the currency is volatile, show the effect of delayed receipts and exchange-rate movement on timing.

## 4. Test sensitivity and risk

- **One-variable sensitivity:** change one assumption at a time (volume, price, cost, timing, adoption) and record the effect on payback and cash.
- **Risk analysis:** test milestone slippage, technology failure, vendor or support failure, and timing risk. Name the assumption that would reverse the recommendation.

## 5. Build the case with the people who will run it

- Gather cross-functional input (finance, operations, sales, technology).
- Write a clear storyline from problem to recommendation.
- Align key stakeholders early.
- Document every assumption and its source.
- Keep version control; record what changed between versions and why.

## 6. Plan the look-back

For high-capital or high-technology initiatives, schedule a post-implementation review:

| Question | Record |
|---|---|
| What was assumed? | Original assumptions and numbers |
| What happened? | Actual results for the same measures |
| What variance emerged? | Difference and its cause |
| What corrective action is needed? | Owner, date, decision |

Feed the look-back into the quarterly game plan, monitoring framework, and living-plan decision log.

## 7. Pass criteria

- [ ] Problem, timing trigger, strategic fit, and ask stated before the solution.
- [ ] Do-nothing option and at least one credible alternative compared.
- [ ] Incremental revenue, cost, capex, cash timing, payback, and break-even shown.
- [ ] Use of funds tied to benefits and dates.
- [ ] One-variable sensitivity and risk tests completed.
- [ ] Assumptions documented and versioned.
- [ ] Post-implementation review scheduled for high-capex or high-technology bets.

A plan that fails any item cannot describe the initiative as justified; record the gap as a blocker or a qualified finding.
