# Structured Problem Solving for Plan Analysis

Use this reference to structure the analysis behind a plan, validate market assumptions, decide what to research, and communicate conclusions briefly. The methods are the widely taught consulting toolkit (MECE structuring, issue trees, hypothesis-driven work, the 80/20 rule, key drivers, fact-based analysis, prewiring, the elevator test), summarised in Rasiel's The McKinsey Way; the procedures and prompts below are this engine's own.

## 1. MECE structuring

Every list of issues, options, risks or sections must be mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (nothing relevant missing). It is the main test of clear, complete thinking.
Check procedure:
1. Take the list.
2. For each pair of items ask whether they overlap; if so, merge or restructure.
3. Ask whether any aspect of the problem falls under none of the items; if so, add or refine a category.
4. Keep the top line to two to five items (three is ideal); place detail below.
Illustration: for "increase sales of a product", a list of change how we sell, improve marketing, cut unit cost and re-engineer production is not MECE because the last item belongs under cutting unit cost. Each sub-issue must sit under exactly one parent.
Safety valve: two or three important points that fit nowhere may go under "other issues" at sub-level, never as a top-line item in a formal presentation; it is a compliance device, not a substitute for thinking.

## 2. Issue trees

An issue tree turns the hypothesis into a map of what must be proved.
- Top: the problem statement.
- First level: the top-line MECE issue list, phrased as actionable hypotheses, not topics.
- Next levels: sub-issues (the questions to answer).
- Leaves: the analyses (data and calculations) that prove or disprove each sub-issue.
Each branch is MECE with its siblings; drill down only as far as needed to name the analysis.
Use: for each top-line issue ask "if this is right, what sub-questions does it raise?"; for each sub-question ask "what analysis would test it?"; treat the finished tree as the workplan and, if the hypothesis holds, as the outline of the final section. Sales-growth example structure: change sales strategy (organisation, skills, promotion); improve marketing (quality, packaging, consumer advertising); reduce unit cost (materials sourcing, production process, distribution).

## 3. Hypothesis-driven analysis

Principle: work out the probable answer before extensive research, then prove or disprove it. It focuses effort, avoids gathering everything and hunting for meaning, and speeds work.
Generating the initial hypothesis:
1. Absorb enough facts for an overview (trade publications, industry contacts, internal documents).
2. Identify the two or three key drivers (section 5).
3. Turn each into an actionable recommendation ("reduce our exposure to weather", not "weather affects profit").
4. Assemble the recommendations into a MECE top-line list; that is the hypothesis.
5. Build the issue tree.
Test it as a team before analysing: is it the best available given current knowledge; are all key drivers covered; is every recommendation actionable and provable; does every branch lead to a real analysis? Teams give stronger hypotheses because members challenge assumptions; the leader proposes and invites challenge.
Discipline: if the facts contradict the hypothesis, change the hypothesis, never the facts. Run a weekly reality check: what have I learned and how does it change the hypothesis?

## 4. The 80/20 rule as a diagnostic

Typically a minority of items (about a fifth) produces the majority of the result (about four fifths): sales from a few sellers, orders from a few customers, profit from a few products.
Procedure:
1. Assemble data: sales, margin and volume by product, customer, channel, team member and geography.
2. Sort each way.
3. Find the clumps.
4. Focus analysis on them.
5. Look for opportunity as well as waste: the rule shows where potential concentrates.
Opening question for any profitability problem: where do the profits come from? Reallocating scarce people to the largest accounts can raise total sales, not just shift them.
Corollary, do not boil the ocean: gather enough facts to prove or disprove the hypothesis and stop.

## 5. Key drivers

Of many factors, two or three drive most of the outcome. Name them first and ignore the rest at first pass; complexity multiplies work faster than the number of components, so simplify as physicists do by keeping only dominant forces.
Typical drivers by plan section:
| Section | Drivers |
|---|---|
| Revenue projection | Price, volume and one dominant channel |
| Cost structure | One or two dominant cost lines (often labour plus one input) |
| Market size | Segment population x penetration x average spend |
| Competitive position | One or two factors that decide customer choice |
| Risk analysis | The two or three risks with highest probability x impact |
Run sensitivity analysis on the drivers in the financial model.

## 6. Fact-based analysis

Every statement needs a fact, not an opinion. Facts offset the lack of industry instinct and give credibility with senior decision-makers, banks and investors.
Acceptable facts: published data (industry reports, statistics, accounts); primary research (interviews, surveys, field visits); reasoned estimates with explicit assumptions, flagged as estimates.
Estimating from components when data is missing (never accept "no idea"):
1. Break the unknown into observable parts (for example a competitor's publication cost = estimated cost per page x pages x circulation; plus staff = headcount x salary benchmark; plus advertising from market observation).
2. State each assumption.
3. Build the total and flag the error margin.
4. Check whether the decision is sensitive to the error; a figure within a factor of two is often accurate enough to decide. Use this for market sizing.

## 7. Storyline and presentation

Structure shows thinking; sloppy structure signals sloppy analysis.
1. MECE structure; one issue per page.
2. One message per chart: the caption states the conclusion; the chart illustrates it.
3. Cite the source on every chart.
4. Lead the reader from problem to evidence to conclusion.
Simplest structure: say what you will say (summary or hypothesis), say it (evidence), say what you said (conclusion and recommendation).
Prewiring: before a formal presentation, walk each key stakeholder through the findings one by one to remove surprises, catch factual errors and build consensus, so the meeting confirms rather than confronts. For a plan, walk the relationship manager or lead investor through the key numbers and assumptions before submission.
Chart practice: keep charts simple; prefer plain colours that do not overpower the message; avoid three-dimensional graphics; use the fewest charts that make the point. A waterfall chart (start value on the left, net result on the right, rises and falls between) suits the income statement, cash-flow bridge and revenue-to-profit story.

## 8. The elevator test

Can you state the conclusion and sell it to a senior decision-maker in about 30 seconds?
1. Name the two or three issues addressed.
2. State the recommendation for each in a sentence.
3. State the payoff (the "so what").
4. Drop supporting data.
Template: "We think you can achieve [outcome] within [period] if you [action]; details later."
Apply to: the executive summary's opening paragraph; the funding request (ask, use of funds, repayment case in three sentences); competitive positioning (one-sentence value proposition); the risk summary (top risks and mitigations).

## 9. Mistakes to avoid

| Mistake | Remedy |
|---|---|
| Accepting the problem as stated | Confirm the real problem early; clients often present symptoms or the wrong problem (spend roughly the first tenth of the time on this) |
| Fitting facts to the hypothesis | Change the hypothesis |
| Boiling the ocean | Stop when the hypothesis is proved or disproved |
| Cookie-cutter answers | Validate instinct with fact-based analysis each time |
| Recommending the theoretical optimum | Fit solutions to what the organisation can implement |
| Accepting "no idea" | Estimate from components |
| Hiding problems upward | Keep information flowing; early disclosure costs less than surprises |
| Overloading a presentation | Use the minimum pages |
| Bluffing | Say "I do not know", then find out |
| Ignoring politics | Understand incentives and build consensus |

## 10. Forces scan

At the start, scan four external categories and internal change to produce a current snapshot and a view of coming shifts:
1. Suppliers: what is changing and with what effect?
2. Customers: changes in behaviour, power or needs.
3. Competitors: what moves are they making?
4. Substitutes: what could replace the offer?
5. Internal: developments in the business and industry that change how it designs, makes, distributes, sells and services.
Use for the market and competitive sections; it is the basis for positioning.
