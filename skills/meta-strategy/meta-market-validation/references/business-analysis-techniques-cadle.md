# Business Analysis Toolkit for Business Plans

Use this reference to choose an analytical technique for a plan section. Many analysis techniques are planning tools used in reverse: an analyst studies an existing organisation to design a future state, while a plan author studies an imagined future state to prove it is feasible. The same rigour applies both ways. The techniques are standard business-analysis practice (a catalogue popularised by Cadle, Paul and Turner); grouping, prompts and applications are this engine's own.

## 2. Scan the environment

- PESTLE: scan political, economic, socio-cultural, technological, legal and environmental forces, each as constraint or opportunity; repeat regularly. Use with the five forces for a complete picture. In low-data markets supplement published sources (revenue-authority briefings, city licence registers, national statistics) with field observation and community knowledge; social factors need primary research.
- Five forces: rivals, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power, supplier power. Define the industry boundary carefully (too narrow misses substitutes; too broad hides real rivals). The output is structural profitability, not a list of competitors. In fragmented informal markets identify rivals by physical market survey; buyer power often sits with a few well-connected traders or chains; supplier power is understated where inputs are imported (currency risk compounds it). See `market-strategy-frameworks.md` for rating methodology.

## 3. Audit internal capability and set strategy

- Mission-objectives-strategy-tactics check (with vision above): confirm tactics serve strategy, strategy serves objectives and objectives serve the mission; misalignment at any level is a strategic failure. Founder-led firms often have unwritten missions; writing them down exposes drift in growing businesses. Complement with `../02-company-overview/references/mission-vision-why.md`.
- Resource audit: catalogue physical (land, buildings, equipment; note leased versus owned, which matters for collateral), financial (profit on sales, liquidity, gearing), human (headcount, skills, motivation; count informal trade skills), reputation (brand, awards, community and referral standing, often the most valuable unreported asset) and know-how (systems, processes, intellectual capital).
- Portfolio matrix: plot products or units by market growth and relative share into stars (invest), cash cows (harvest), question marks (decide) and dogs (divest).
- SWOT and TOWS: SWOT combines audit (strengths, weaknesses) and scan (opportunities, threats); TOWS then generates options: use strengths to take opportunities; use strengths to counter threats; fix weaknesses to take opportunities; minimise weaknesses to avoid threats.
- Growth matrix: market penetration (existing product, existing market, lowest risk); market development; product development; diversification (highest risk).

## 4. Test implementation readiness

- Seven-element model: strategy, structure, systems, shared values (the anchor), staff, skills, style; changing one forces changes in the others, so check all seven.
- Four-view model: customer (outside-in value), business process, organisation (who does what) and technology views, so no perspective is omitted.

## 5. Measure performance

- Critical success factors: the few things the organisation must do well, either industry-wide (table stakes) or specific (differentiators), derived from mission and position.
- KPIs: measurable indicators subordinate to critical success factors (for example factor "excellent service" gives complaint volume per month and share of customers rating service very good or excellent). KPIs without success factors are metrics without context.
- Balanced scorecard (Kaplan and Norton): financial, customer, internal process, and learning and growth perspectives, to avoid short-term financial bias.

## 6. Investigate the situation

| Technique | Use | Points |
|---|---|---|
| Interviewing | Tacit knowledge, politics, individual views | Prepare objectives and questions; move from open to probing to closed; listen; manage silence; issue notes promptly |
| Facilitated workshops | Reconcile several perspectives quickly | Scope and objectives; preparation; facilitation (focus, participation, record); output review; follow-up. Unfacilitated workshops produce noise |
| Observation (structured, shadowing, ethnographic) | Gap between stated and actual practice | Time-consuming; reveals tacit practice |
| Questionnaires | Large populations | Introduction (purpose, confidentiality, return date), instructions, questions (open, closed, scaled, ranked), return instructions. Watch response rate, ambiguity, self-selection; pair with interviews |
| Sampling (activity or record) | Distribution of time or work without continuous observation | Random-interval observation; size the sample for confidence |
| Special-purpose records | Data not otherwise available | Custom forms to track specific events |
| Document analysis | Current-state understanding early on | Specify each key document: purpose, volume, frequency, data items, users |

Documenting results:
- Rich pictures: free-form drawings of people, processes, concerns, conflicts and relationships; informal by design; suited to complex or political situations.
- Mind maps: radial structure for issues and investigation scope; good for a first pass at each plan section.
- Context diagram: the system in the centre with every external entity and the information flows between; fixes scope.

## 7. Stakeholders

1. Nominate: prompt with customers, users, suppliers, regulators, competitors, partners, employees and owners.
2. Research the background from secondary sources (annual reports, sector publications, filings, competitor sites, news) before primary work.
3. Stakeholder wheel: concentric rings, inner for directly involved and outer for affected, to show proximity and influence.
4. Power-interest grid: high power and high interest, manage closely; high power and low interest, keep satisfied; low power and high interest, keep informed; low power and low interest, monitor. Extend with current versus desired position. Reflect actual power, not formal authority: elders, religious leaders, market associations and political connections often outrank formal roles.
5. CATWOE (Checkland): customer, actor, transformation, worldview (the belief that explains why the system exists; the key insight, since it exposes hidden agendas and value conflicts), owner, environment. Important where owners, workers and communities hold different views of what the business is for.
6. Activity model: the conceptual activities a system must perform for a given worldview, without saying how; derived from the CATWOE root definition.
7. Responsibility matrix (RASCI or RACI): responsible, accountable, supportive, consulted, informed for every activity; prevents accountability gaps.
8. Stakeholder management plan: power and interest, concerns, attitude (champion, supporter, neutral, critic, opponent, blocker), desired support, planned actions; keep it alive through the project.
9. Conflict modes (Thomas-Kilmann): competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating, by assertiveness against cooperativeness; choose the mode deliberately.
10. Principled negotiation (Fisher and Ury): separate people from the problem; focus on interests, not positions; invent options for mutual gain; insist on objective criteria; know the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, which decides when to walk away.

## 8. Analyse needs and model the organisation

- Value proposition analysis: from the customer's view, classify propositions as operational excellence (lowest cost or best convenience), product leadership, customer intimacy (best total solution) or regulatory and societal (compliance or mission); clarify functional versus emotional benefit.
- Value chain (Porter): primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service) and support activities (infrastructure, people, technology, procurement); find cost and differentiation opportunities per activity.
- Organisation diagram: the organisation with customers, suppliers, partners, regulators and the high-level processes linking them (not an internal hierarchy chart).
- Business event analysis: external events (customers, suppliers, regulators), internal events (management decisions) and time-based events each trigger processes with desired outcomes; basis for systematic process models.
- Process modelling with swimlanes: tasks, decisions and actors, one lane per role so handoffs are visible; check for bottlenecks, duplicate steps and loops; as-is models show improvement opportunities and to-be models define the target. Most small-firm operations live as tacit knowledge in the owner's head; a documented process map shows lenders and investors the business is systemised and can run without the founder. See `business-process-management.md` for the process lifecycle.
- Business rules: structural rules (a customer must have an account before ordering) and operational rules (discounts only above a stated order value); they govern eligibility, pricing and exceptions.
- Decision tables (all condition combinations mapped to actions; efficient for many conditions) and decision trees (sequential decisions); both prevent untested conditions.
- Gap analysis: gap between as-is and to-be; needs a thorough current-state analysis and a clear vision. At plan level, the as-is is the current business or market status quo and the to-be is the venture at full scale; the gap is the implementation challenge Section 13 must address, and unacknowledged gaps will be found in due diligence.

## 9. Evaluate options

1. Identify options creatively (brainstorm, mind map) to avoid early narrowing; use an incremental ladder: do nothing, minimum change, phased improvement, full transformation.
2. Feasibility: business (fits strategy and objectives), technical (capabilities exist), financial (fundable with positive return); use as gates.
3. Force-field analysis: list driving forces and restraining forces; the net force indicates likelihood of success; weakening a restraint is often more effective than strengthening a driver.
4. Cost-benefit analysis: one-off, ongoing and intangible costs; tangible and intangible benefits; make intangibles concrete and link them to measurable proxies (community relations, environmental stewardship and staff morale are often under-stated and matter to grant funders and impact investors).
5. Impact analysis: effects on processes, technology, structure, departmental relations, working practices, management style and recruitment; prevents underestimating change.
6. Risk analysis: register, rate by likelihood and impact, assign owners, define mitigation, contingency and tolerance; feeds the recommendation.
7. Investment appraisal: payback or break-even (speed of recovery); discounted cash flow and net present value (worth in today's money); internal rate of return (accept if above the cost of capital). NPV is theoretically strongest; payback is most used. Use a current, dated local lending rate as the discount rate unless a concessional rate applies (see `business-valuation-methods.md` for rate building).
8. Business case report: executive summary (findings, options considered, recommendation; the most-read part), current situation, options analysis (with cost-benefit, risk and impact for each), recommendation, appendices.
9. Business case presentation: know the audience, structure for decision not completeness, answer first then evidence, anticipate objections, end with a clear ask.

## 10. Define requirements (product and service scoping)

- Elicit: scenarios (normal and exception narratives; check that all user types, environments and frequencies appear); storyboards (visual step sequences); prototypes (throwaway, evolutionary, incremental; show, do not tell); hothousing (two to three day competitive prototyping workshop); timeboxing (fixed period; defer lower-priority items).
- Prioritise: MoSCoW (must, should, could, will not this time).
- Organise: functional (by area, process or use case), non-functional (performance, security, availability, backup), general (constraints, legal, look and feel), technical (hardware, software, telecoms).
- Document: a catalogue with unique identifier, name, source, description, priority, status, owner and linked models.
- Acceptance criteria: define before development, as the basis for testing.
- Validate: correct, complete, consistent, unambiguous, verifiable, necessary; use desk checks, peer reviews, walkthroughs, technical reviews or formal inspections.
- Manage change: request; impact assessment; authority review; decision (approve, reject, defer); update catalogue, traceability and models.
- Traceability matrix: link requirements to objectives, tests and design; every requirement traces to an objective and every test to a requirement.
- Use cases: actors and system functions with pre-conditions, normal, alternative and exception flows, post-conditions; bounds a proposed service.
- Data models: entity-relationship (entities, relationships, cardinality) or class models (data and behaviour, inheritance); a CRUD matrix cross-references entities and processes to find orphan data and unused processes.

## 11. Manage change and benefits

- Cultural web (Johnson and Scholes): stories, symbols, power structures, organisational structures, control systems, rituals and routines; diagnose culture before designing change.
- Lewin's three stages: unfreeze (create motivation), change, refreeze (embed). The common failure is refreezing too early. Moving from informal to formal operations (business and tax registration, electronic invoicing, social-security compliance, formal HR) is a typical case; unfreezing needs concrete examples of contracts lost or loans blocked by informality.
- SARAH curve: shock, anger, rejection, acceptance, hope; match the intervention to each person's stage and do not force the pace.
- Learning: Kolb's cycle (experience, reflection, conceptualisation, experimentation) and Honey and Mumford's styles (activist, reflector, theorist, pragmatist); design training for all four.
- Conscious competence: unconscious incompetence, conscious incompetence, conscious competence, unconscious competence; identify each person's stage.
- Benefits management: expected benefits, named benefit owners, tracking and review; plan contents: vision, benefits map, financial analysis, dependencies, tracking, accountabilities. Project management delivers outputs; benefits management secures outcomes.
- Benefits realisation: post-implementation review; handle timing (leading versus lagging indicators), attribution and the reluctance to audit failures; use the benefits map's enabling-change logic.

## 12. Essential starter set for a plan

Eleven techniques to reach for first: CATWOE (hidden worldviews in the founding team); swimlane process models (operations plan); use cases (scope a service); workshops (intake and working sessions); mind maps (brainstorm each section); interviews (customer discovery); organisation diagram (system boundary); scenarios (customer journeys); cost-benefit analysis (investment case); SWOT (synthesis); MoSCoW (implementation priorities).

## 13. Plan-section prompts

- Which techniques were used for each section, and what evidence did they produce?
- What is the as-is, the to-be and the gap, and where is the gap closed in Section 13?
- Which stakeholders hold real power and what do they want?
- What are the options considered, their costs, benefits, risks and the appraisal result?
- Who owns each benefit and how is it tracked?
