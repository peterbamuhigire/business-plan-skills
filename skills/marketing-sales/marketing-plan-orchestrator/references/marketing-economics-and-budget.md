# Marketing economics, budget and the demand-to-cash bridge

Parent: [Marketing Plan Orchestrator](../SKILL.md).

When to read: when setting acquisition cost ceilings, judging whether a
direct-response campaign pays, building the marketing budget, reconciling the
plan with the P&L, or writing the retention and customer-value section. These
are planning calculations. Accounting treatment, tax and statutory cost lines
belong to the Chwezi finance doctrine; record its gate in the plan's evidence
bundle. All figures below are illustrative.

## 1. Customer lifetime value (CLV)

Contribution CLV = average order value × contribution margin % × orders per
period × expected customer life (in the same periods). Expected life can be
estimated as 1 ÷ periodic churn. Discount long lives to present value when the
life exceeds about two years. Keep revenue CLV and contribution CLV separate;
only contribution CLV can justify spending.

Example: average monthly spend UGX 110,000, contribution 35%, monthly churn 7%
→ life ≈ 14 months → contribution CLV ≈ UGX 110,000 × 0.35 × 14 ≈ UGX 539,000.

Growing spend per existing customer usually moves CLV more than cutting
acquisition cost; test both in the model before choosing (Hunter, V. L. with
Tietyen, D. (1997) *Business to Business Marketing*, NTC Business Books).
Report the change in total customer-base CLV year on year as an asset measure
alongside sales.

## 2. Acquisition cost ceilings

**Customer acquisition cost (CAC)** = acquisition spend in a period ÷ new
customers won in that period (same definitions and window).

**Time to customer break-even** = CAC ÷ monthly contribution per customer.
Example: cost per enquiry UGX 30,000 at 30% conversion → CAC ≈ UGX 100,000;
monthly contribution UGX 38,500 → break-even in about 2.6 months (Croll, A.
and Yoskovitz, B. (2013) *Lean Analytics*, O'Reilly Media).

**Rule of thumb:** keep CAC at or below about one-third of contribution CLV
unless there is a stated reason (Croll and Yoskovitz report it as widely cited;
treat it as a heuristic, not a standard). Remember payment, platform and
delivery fees.

**Referral effect:** with a viral coefficient K below 1, each paid customer
brings about 1 ÷ (1 − K) customers in total, so effective CAC ≈ paid spend ÷
(paid customers ÷ (1 − K)). Present referral as a CAC reducer, never as
"going viral".

**Maximum affordable cost per lead (waterfall).** Work backwards from what a
customer is worth (Hunter and Tietyen (1997), adapted):

| Step | Calculation | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lifetime contribution of a look-alike customer | UGX 3,600,000 |
| 2 | × share the business will invest in acquisition | × 40% = UGX 1,440,000 |
| 3 | − cost of closing (visits, proposals) | − 2 visits × UGX 150,000 = UGX 1,140,000 |
| 4 | × qualified-lead-to-customer rate | × 25% = UGX 285,000 per qualified lead |
| 5 | × raw-enquiry-to-qualified rate | × 40% = UGX 114,000 per raw enquiry |
| 6 | − qualification cost per enquiry | − UGX 14,000 = **UGX 100,000 maximum media cost per raw enquiry** |

Any channel test that brings enquiries above the ceiling fails unless its
qualification or close rate is shown to be higher.

## 3. Direct-response campaign economics

Build a pro-forma P&L per offer to find the **net profit per order** and the
**orders per thousand contacts needed to break even** (Stockwell, J. and Shaw,
H. M. (1994) *Direct Marketing Checklists*, NTC Business Books):

| Row | Item | Example (school-bag offer) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Selling price | 90,000 |
| 2 | Cost of sales: goods 35,000 + handling 3,000 + delivery 7,000 + premium 2,000 | 47,000 |
| 3 | Overhead allocation (10% of price) | 9,000 |
| 4 | Expected returns | 4% |
| 5 | Return cost per returned order: handling + delivery (10,000) + refurbishing (12.5% of goods, 4,375) | 14,375 |
| 6 | Chargeable return cost = row 4 × row 5 | 575 |
| 7 | Bad debt or failed collection rate | 2% |
| 8 | Chargeable bad debt = row 1 × row 7 | 1,800 |
| 9 | Total variable cost = rows 2 + 3 + 6 + 8 | 58,375 |
| 10 | Unit profit after variable cost = row 1 − row 9 | 31,625 |
| 11 | Return factor = 100% − row 4 | 96% |
| 12 | Unit profit per order = row 10 × row 11 | 30,360 |
| 13 | Credit for returned goods = row 4 × goods cost | 1,400 |
| 14 | **Net profit per order** = row 12 + row 13 | **31,760** |
| 15 | Marketing cost per thousand contacts | depends on medium |
| 16 | **Orders per thousand to break even** = row 15 ÷ row 14 | see below |

- Printed flyer door-drop at UGX 900 a piece → UGX 900,000 per thousand →
  about 28 orders per thousand (2.8% response) to break even.
- SMS to an opted-in list at UGX 40 a message → UGX 40,000 per thousand →
  about 1.3 orders per thousand (0.13%).

Cheap media lower the line dramatically, but list quality and consent decide
whether any response comes. Returns and failed mobile-money or cash-on-delivery
collections belong in the model, not in a footnote.

**Test ladder:** smallest readable test → read at the planned horizon →
**retest** at the same size → **extend** → **roll out**. Stop at any stage when
cost per order or selling cost as a percentage of revenue breaches the P&L.

**Break-even units** for a launch = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per
unit). Compare the break-even volume with the reachable market: if break-even
needs an implausible share of the target market, change the offer before
spending.

## 4. Echo effect

Direct campaigns also lift sales in untracked channels (walk-ins, phone orders,
retail). Measure it: record a baseline of untracked sales for several months
before the campaign; compare during the campaign; echo % = incremental
untracked sales ÷ tracked campaign sales. Report total campaign effect with
the echo stated separately and with a seasonality caveat.

## 5. Lead requirement from sales capacity

Work from the sales team back to the media plan: required qualified leads =
sellers × meetings per day × selling days × share of meetings needing a new
lead. Divide by the qualification rate to get raw enquiries, and multiply by
cost per raw enquiry to get the lead budget. Example: 4 field sellers × 3
visits × 22 days = 264 visits a month; if half need a new lead, 132 qualified
leads; at 30% qualification, 440 raw enquiries; at UGX 9,000 each, UGX 3.96m a
month. If sales capacity is the bottleneck, add qualifying friction rather
than more leads.

## 6. Retention economics

- Define an **active customer** with time and money (for example, spent at
  least UGX [x] in the last 12 months and ordered at least once every three
  months) and classify the base as active, at risk, lapsed or former.
- **Market at risk:** share of customers reporting a problem × share of
  problems of each type × share saying they will not buy again (minimum risk)
  or will not or might not (maximum risk). Multiply customers at risk by the
  value of a lost customer to justify the fix budget; rank problems by money at
  risk, not frequency.
- **Early-warning triggers** from purchase rhythm (no order in the usual
  interval → reminder; still none → call; falling spend → review).
- **Assimilation:** the first contact after the first purchase (thank, check
  problems, profile, grade, prompt the second purchase) is the highest-return
  retention action; prove it with a test and control group.

## 7. Budget structure

1. Phase 1 by activity (media, production, research and testing,
   contingency, fees and tools) and Phase 2 by segment, geography and month:
   see [advertising and media plan](advertising-and-media-plan.md).
2. Monthly budget by vehicle, with sub-lines (printing, distribution, list or
   data costs, event fees, travel, staff time where included).
3. A separate line for retention and one for acquisition, because their
   costs and returns differ.
4. Test budget held separately from scale budget until tests read out.

## 8. Demand-to-cash bridge and P&L reconciliation

Build the bridge by month: reachable qualified audience → enquiries → qualified
opportunities → wins after the sales lag → deliverable volume (capacity check)
→ revenue recognised → cash collected (payment terms, deposits, failed
collections). Show retention and repeat purchases on separate rows. The
existing [decision workpapers](marketing-decision-workpapers.md) give the
bridge's governance rules.

Reconciliation checks before release (the flow-through principle in Abrams,
R. M. (1993) *The Successful Business Plan*, The Oasis Press: every cost in a
worksheet maps to a named statement line):

| Check | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Plan budget = P&L marketing and advertising line | Same total by month and year, same scenario |
| Sales staff and commissions in the sales plan = payroll and commission lines | Same headcount, timing and rates |
| Volumes in the bridge = revenue drivers in the model | Same units, prices and lags |
| Cash timing | Marketing spend precedes receipts; the cash-flow statement shows the gap and its funding |
| Scenario consistency | Base, downside and upside use the same definitions |

Any mismatch returns to the owning section; the plan is not released until it
reconciles. Finance-engine review is recorded; unavailable review is
NOT_ASSESSED.
