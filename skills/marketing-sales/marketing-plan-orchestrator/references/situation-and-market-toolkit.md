# Situation, market, competitor and positioning toolkit

Parent: [Marketing Plan Orchestrator](../SKILL.md).

When to read: while drafting sections 2–8 of a marketing plan (situation, market and customer, competitors, positioning and message, strategy), or the equivalent business-plan sections 04–07. Each tool states its procedure, its output and the failure it prevents. Use only the tools that change a decision.

This is the engine's own toolkit. It is organised by the questions a marketing plan must answer in order: what is happening around us, who buys and why, whom do they compare us with, what single idea do we own, and which strategic choices follow. Named frameworks (PESTEL, five forces, SWOT, segmentation and positioning classics) are standard tools; where a method comes from a particular book it is attributed briefly. The tests, tables and wording are this engine's.

## 1. What is happening around us

### 1.1 Frame the analysis

Look outward at the company's context, the consumer, the competition and the communication environment, and inward at operations, finance and people. Keep only facts that bear on this plan's decisions; move the rest to an appendix (framing in Kelley, L. D. and Sheehan, K. B. (c. 2021) *Advertising Management in a Digital Environment*, Routledge).

### 1.2 Wider environment reduced to key drivers

1. Scan political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal factors for the plan's place and horizon.
2. For each candidate factor record direction of change, evidence (source, date), the segment or cost line it touches, and whether it is a certainty, a trend or a forecast.
3. Keep only the three to six **key drivers** that could change market size, buyer behaviour or the basis of competition within the plan horizon. A list of forty bullets is a failure of analysis.
4. State each driver's implication for the plan ("mobile-money payment of school fees is routine in the target districts, so instalment offers can be collected without handling cash").

Current legal, tax and platform facts come from the currentness register or the Digital Research engine; the finance engine owns tax. (Method as presented in Johnson, G., Whittington, R., Scholes, K., Angwin, D. and Regnér, P. (2017) *Exploring Strategy*, 11th edn, Pearson.)

### 1.3 Industry pressure

Define the industry from the buyer's substitutes, not the firm's products. For rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power and supplier power (and complementors whose products raise or lower your value) list observed drivers, rate the pressure high, medium or low, and state the effect on price, margin or channel choice. "Low barriers to entry" is a threat, not a strength. Depth: `../../../pipeline/04-market-analysis/references/industry-structure-porter.md`.

### 1.4 Weighted factor tables

Use `meta-strategic-factor-analysis` for the procedure. The engine's single rating scale is the Wheelen–Hunger 1–5 response scale (5 = outstanding, 3 = average, 1 = poor); print it in every table header. Show an external table (opportunities and threats), an internal table (strengths and weaknesses) and a summary table of the strategic factors carried forward, each with weight (all weights sum to 1.00), rating, weighted score, an evidence comment and, in the summary, a time horizon. Weights reflect importance to success in this market, not the firm's pride; never mix scales.

### 1.5 From SWOT to strategies

A SWOT is a summary, not a strategy. Cross the summary factors into four cells (use strengths on opportunities; use strengths against threats; fix weaknesses to reach opportunities; defensive moves) and require at least one candidate strategy per cell, adopted or rejected with a reason in the strategy section. Reject items that are generic ("good customer service"), shared by every rival, or unevidenced; keep at most six per box.

## 2. Who buys, and why

### 2.1 Segmentation bases

| For consumers | For businesses |
|---|---|
| Need and benefit sought; occasion and frequency; life stage; income and payment method (cash, mobile money, card, instalments); place (district, town, estate, parish); language; media habits | Industry and sub-sector; size (staff, revenue, branches); buying process and approvers; purchase cycle (including public procurement rules); application of the product; relationship stage |

Segment on differences that change the offer, message, channel or price. Demographics describe; they rarely explain. Serve a segment only when the extra cost of tailoring to it is clearly below the extra sales it brings (the tailoring test in Stockwell, J. and Shaw, H. M. (1994) *Direct Marketing Checklists*, NTC Business Books). "SMEs" and "all parents" are not segments.

### 2.2 Four checks a target market must pass

The checks below were drawn up for this engine; the underlying idea that a target market must be specific, relevant, big enough and reachable is common to planning texts (see Abrams, R. M. (1993) *The Successful Business Plan*, 2nd edn, The Oasis Press).

| Check | Question | Evidence to show |
|---|---|---|
| Describable | Can we state the shared characteristics as conditions someone could verify? | Each defining attribute written as a checkable condition |
| Relevant to buying | Does each attribute affect the decision to buy? | A "why this matters to purchase" column |
| Large enough | Would winning a realistic share of it meet the objectives profitably? | Count, spend and share needed, with source and date |
| Reachable | Can we contact it affordably through channels it uses? | Named channels, evidence of use, cost estimate |

Two further rules: if an intermediary pays you, define the intermediary and the end user separately; and always name who pays, who uses, who approves and who can block.

### 2.3 Describing the segment

Describe the segment through who they are (demographic or firm profile), where they are, how they live or operate, what they value, how they buy (first-purchase occasion, frequency, interval, quantity, decision time, where they learn and buy, payment method) and what they are sensitive to (price, quality, brand, features, salesperson, offers, convenience, location, service, credit, warranty; rate each high, medium, low or none). The sensitivity ratings drive the message and the offer. East African fields to add: mobile-money use, WhatsApp and radio habits, church, SACCO and association membership, informal-market buying, commuting (taxi, boda, on foot) and language preference.

### 2.4 Buying situations

Record for each segment whether purchases are routine repeats, modified repeats or first-time decisions, and what triggers each. First-time decisions need education and proof; routine repeats need availability and reminders. Note when the buyer is most open to a message (pay day, school term, harvest, month-end close, festive season).

### 2.5 Customer insights report

Before creative or media spend, produce a report from three kinds of evidence: why people buy or drop out (research), how and where they behave (transaction and behavioural data), and who they are (life-stage and demographic data, used to interpret rather than define). Contents: segment map; top three motivators and repellents per segment; where each segment spends media time; purchase triggers and objections; what the top three competitors all say; the difference that matters to buyers; changes since the last report; sample, method and date.

An SME without data brokers can combine 150–400 structured survey responses (WhatsApp or SMS links, a small airtime incentive), 8–12 depth interviews and a CRM or sales-book analysis. Record method and sample; never fabricate respondents or treat a synthetic persona as data. Consent and data-protection rules apply to recruitment (see [location and scope calibration](location-and-scope-calibration.md)). The research-first sequencing follows Stutts, P. (2021) *The Undefeated Marketing System*.

## 3. Whom they compare us with

### 3.1 Who counts

Include competitors aiming at the same customer at the same time, and category alternatives: informal traders, imports, NGO or free provision, in-house teams and doing nothing. "We have no competition" signals an unexamined market.

### 3.2 Two weighted scoring grids

1. **Buyer-view grid:** features, price, indirect costs, quality, durability, image, perceived value, recognition, relationships, location, delivery time, convenience, credit terms, service, social image. Weight each 1–10 by importance **to the target segment** (from interviews, not team opinion); score the firm and each competitor within the weights; allow negative scores where an attribute repels the segment.
2. **Capability grid:** finance, marketing budget, technology, distribution access, supplier access, scale, efficiency, sales capability, range, partnerships, people, certification and intellectual property.
3. Add market-share distribution (by revenue and volume, with trend) and a barriers table rating each barrier's strength **and how long it will last**.

(Two-grid idea adapted from Abrams (1993).)

### 3.3 Finding what really drives choice

List every product, service, operational and commercial attribute; score competitors on the same list; strike out attributes everyone offers; mark where you are uniquely better; then ask customers to rank the shortlist and rate how much each matters to buying. An attribute ranked first but unimportant to buying is not a driver. Interview recent defectors and classify them (price, product, service, market exit, substitute technology, internal politics); do not spend on winning back the unsalvageable kinds (approach from Hunter, V. L. with Tietyen, D. (1997) *Business to Business Marketing*, NTC Business Books).

## 4. The one idea we own

### 4.1 Positioning statement

Template: "For [target and mindset], [brand] is the [frame of reference] that [single promise], because [checkable reason to believe]." The reason must be a verifiable fact (certification, process, partnership, data), never an adjective. Test it: does it differentiate, match a customer truth, state clear value, stay memorable, work across every touchpoint, prove out and stay ownable? Reject four failures: blurred, too narrow for the awareness you have, contradictory, and too close to a rival to be believed. A brand owns one idea in the buyer's mind (positioning checks as presented in Kelley and Sheehan, c. 2021).

### 4.2 Five buyer-benefit questions

Answer each for the chosen segment: **What job does it do?** **What does it do to the buyer's whole money position** (including cash-flow fit such as instalments or smaller packs)? **What time and worry does it save?** **How does it make the buyer feel about themselves?** **What support and security does it give over time** (including presence upcountry)? Choose the one or two answers that matter most to the segment and that no rival owns, and express them at every contact point: name, slogan, key words, product design, logo, packaging, premises, staff dress and scripts. Audit for contradictions. (The five-question habit is adapted from Abrams (1993), which frames the same ground with its own five headings.)

### 4.3 Two or three message themes

List every claim the client wants to make. Score each on importance to buyers (from the insights report), proof available and distinctiveness against the top three competitors. Keep two or three that score high on all three; the rest become supporting copy or FAQ. Frame each theme for the segment (the policy for one group, the effect on the family for another). (Focus rule from Stutts (2021).)

### 4.4 Which strength to lead with

Lead with one of three strengths (operational excellence, product leadership or customer intimacy) and hold acceptable standards on the other two (a classic choice presented in Kelley and Sheehan, c. 2021, from Treacy and Wiersema).

## 5. Strategic choices that follow

- **Who to serve and how broadly:** concentrated, differentiated or undifferentiated coverage; state the order when capacity cannot serve every chosen segment at once.
- **Competitive position:** low price, hybrid, differentiation or focused differentiation, with why it is sustainable; use `meta-strategic-options-evaluation` for the full option assessment, including the response to a low-cost entrant (Johnson et al. (2017)).
- **Growth route and method:** penetration, market development, product development or diversification; organic, alliance or acquisition.
- **Push versus pull:** push through intermediaries (trade margins, sales force, dealer incentives) or pull through end-user demand (advertising, content, referrals); state the balance and channel-conflict rules.
- **Marketing mix:** product, price, place and promotion; for services add people, process and physical evidence. Each element ends in a decision, budget line and owner.
- **Market maturity:** match the goal to the stage (new: exposure and credibility; growing: differentiate and build share; stable: defend share; declining: take share from weakened rivals or exit).

## 6. Acceptance checks for sections 2–8

- Key drivers number six or fewer, each with an implication.
- Factor tables use one stated scale and weights sum to 1.00.
- Every strategy cell yields a candidate that is adopted or rejected with a reason.
- The target market passes all four checks with a justification column.
- Competitor grids include category alternatives, with weights sourced from customers.
- The positioning statement has a checkable reason to believe and passes the four-failure check.
- Two or three message themes, each with proof.

**Illustrative example.** A Masaka private clinic: key driver = mobile-money payment of fees; target = salaried households within 10 km who already use private clinics (describable, relevant, 6,000 households counted from an employer list, reachable via radio and church groups); competitors include the public health centre and a herbalist; positioning = "same-day results for working parents, because the lab opens at 6 a.m."; themes = same-day results, fixed prices posted at reception.
