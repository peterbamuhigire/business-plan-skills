# Capital-efficient SaaS: plan tests

This is the engine's set of tests for SaaS plans that will grow mainly from revenue rather than repeated venture rounds, which is the common situation in East Africa. It is organised as eight tests a reviewer runs on the plan, each with a pass condition. The capital-efficient SaaS literature informs it, especially Walling, R. (2023) *The SaaS Playbook*, which describes a staged path from small products to stand-alone SaaS, argues for largely bootstrapped funding, and discusses moats, pricing and growth accelerators. The tests, wording and examples are this engine's; the book's company examples, multiples and margins are not used.

## Test 1 — Founder readiness

Place the founding team on a readiness path:

| Stage | Evidence | What the plan may credibly ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Learning a channel | A small product (plugin, add-on, template, information product, marketplace listing) with first revenue from one predictable channel | Small, channel-learning budget |
| Replacing hourly income | Several such products or a productised service that frees the founder from selling hours | Budget to build a first stand-alone product |
| Stand-alone SaaS | Capital, channel skill and tolerance for a long ramp | Full product and go-to-market budget |

**Pass:** the plan states the stage and either justifies skipping a stage, funds the missing stage, or adds a co-founder who has done it. A first-time founder with no channel experience asking for a large stand-alone SaaS budget fails. Services firms often reach product this way (see `ict-services-firm-business-plan`).

## Test 2 — Funding mode

| Mode | Money comes from | Investor expects | Fits when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue-funded | Founders and customers | Nothing | Slow, controlled growth is acceptable |
| Funded from a previous business | Founders' own capital | Nothing | Founders have prior exits or profits |
| Mostly revenue-funded | Modest sums from family, angels or profit-seeking funds | Profitability, not further rounds | Most realistic outside major venture hubs |
| Venture-funded | Repeated large rounds | A very large exit | A winner-takes-most market and reachable venture investors |

**Pass:** "mostly revenue-funded" is the default unless the plan shows both a winner-takes-most market and access to venture investors. Burn plans that assume venture money without venture investors fail. Name funders only from a dated source register.

## Test 3 — Why SaaS for this business

Use only arguments evidenced for this business: recurring revenue that compounds; steadier income in downturns than one-off sales; a defined business problem rather than dependence on consumer fashion; no need to build two sides of a marketplace at once; outside funding optional; margins that improve with scale. **Pass:** any valuation multiple quoted carries a current, dated source.

## Test 4 — Fit versus repeatable growth

Distinguish **customers want and pay for it** (paying customers; many users who would be very disappointed to lose it; a retention curve that flattens; organic referrals) from **the business can find more customers every week through repeatable channels**. **Pass:** the narrative and funding ask say which stage applies; funds for scaling channels are not requested before the first is proven. Signals and tests: `saas-mvp-to-product-market-fit-roadmap.md`.

## Test 5 — Continuous customer learning

Interview four groups every month: prospects, customers, people who chose not to buy, and people who cancelled. Set a monthly quota, an owner and a log; review quarterly into roadmap, positioning, pricing and customer profile. Sort feature requests into clearly no, clearly yes, and needs judgement, asking: what is the use case, what share of customers would use it, does it fit the product direction? **Pass:** cadence and owner appear in the operating plan and board pack.

## Test 6 — Position against incumbents and prove a moat

Established categories prove willingness to pay and often hide underserved niches. Choose one or more wedges:

- **Price:** a temporary entry wedge (much of the incumbent's value at a clearly lower price), raised as the brand grows; local-currency pricing against foreign-currency incumbents can create it; test it.
- **Buying model:** self-serve sign-up, published prices and trials against incumbents that demand set-up fees and demonstrations.
- **Product:** mobile-first, integration-first design against older systems.

Turn the incumbent's size into a challenger message. Watch rivals' strategic moves and deals actually lost to them, not their polish or funding news.

Durable moats: integrations (especially private ones), brand, owned traffic (search ranking, app-store and marketplace position, owned communities), switching costs (accumulated data, deep workflow use, organisation-wide adoption). A unique feature is not a moat; it can be copied within months. **Pass:** one real moat is named with its decay risk in the risk section and an annual review. Scoring: `../../../pipeline/06-competitive-analysis/references/saas-moats-and-defensibility.md`.

## Test 7 — Focus and pricing discipline

Distractions to reject unless justified: early translation into many languages (support, marketing and community in each language are the real cost; justified mainly when users and buyers speak different languages); white-labelling for partners without distribution (only with substantial up-front payment); new verticals before the first is won; under-pricing from founder nerves.

Pricing rules: do not compete on price for long, because pricing power follows brand; price on a measure that grows with the customer's success (users, records, usage) so expansion is built in; offer annual prepayment with a discount; use freemium only with product-led distribution; consider taking payment details at trial start to filter low-intent sign-ups; raise prices regularly for new customers and, with notice or added value, for existing ones; use three tiers to anchor the middle. Architecture: `saas-pricing-and-packaging-strategy`.

## Test 8 — Growth accelerators and dashboard

Name at least one accelerator with a start date and evidence: **expansion revenue** from existing customers; **referral or collaborative spread** (strongest in shared-use products); **net negative churn** (expansion exceeds lost revenue); **two funnels** (self-serve and sales-led feeding each other).

Dashboard: MRR and ARR growth, gross and net churn, LTV:CAC, CAC payback, trial-to-paid conversion, quick ratio ((new + expansion MRR) ÷ (contraction + churned MRR)), burn multiple, Rule of 40, with thresholds from current sources. Hire for the stage. Budget for founder support (peer groups, masterminds) where founder density is low.

**Illustrative example.** A Nairobi payroll SaaS founded by a two-person services firm: stage "replacing hourly income" (three payroll templates selling on a marketplace); funding mostly revenue-funded with one angel; wedge = KES pricing, self-serve sign-up and statutory-return integration; moat = private integration with a bank's bulk-payment channel; accelerator = expansion as client headcount grows.

## Release checks

- Readiness stage stated; no stand-alone SaaS outcomes projected for a channel-learning founder.
- Mostly revenue-funded unless venture conditions are evidenced.
- Fit and repeatable growth distinguished in narrative and funding.
- One real moat and its decay risk named.
- At least one accelerator with a timeline.
- Customer-learning cadence in the operating plan and board pack.
- "Raise money and hire a sales leader" rejected as a marketing strategy without channel evidence.
