# SaaS scaling decisions: rules for the plan

This is the engine's register of scaling decisions a SaaS plan must make explicitly, each with a rule and the evidence a reviewer should see. It draws on operator writing about scaling SaaS companies, including Cotton, B. (2019) *How to Run a SaaS Business*, and on negotiation discipline in van der Kooij, J. et al. (2018) *The SaaS Sales Method for Account Executives*, Winning by Design. The rules, grouping and examples are this engine's; company examples and statistics from the sources are not used.

## Decision 1 — When and how to discount

**Before any price concession is discussed, confirm:** the buyer accepts the problem must be solved now; the buyer prefers this solution to the alternatives; the start date and procurement route are known; and the person who signs is involved. If any is missing, the conversation is about value, not price.

**Approval:** set a maximum discount per role (seller, manager, director) and log every approval with its reason.

**Trade every concession for something the business values:** longer term; cash paid in advance; a logo or case study; reference calls; a joint webinar or article; more users or modules; signature by an agreed date. Vary the timing and reason for promotions so buyers do not learn to wait.

## Decision 2 — Whether to offer a free tier

Approve a free tier only when all four hold and are evidenced: the free tier gives real value; the paid tier has clear upgrade triggers; free users cost little to host and support; and the company can attract a large free base. If approved, project free-to-paid conversion with a stated source and a test.

## Decision 3 — Where each product line sits in its life

Every product line rises, matures and declines. Show each line's position and fund the next line before the current one flattens. A three-horizon view helps (widely attributed to McKinsey): today's earning products, emerging lines expected to earn soon, and longer-range pilots and bets. Sections 03 and 13 should show the portfolio, not one roadmap.

## Decision 4 — How the organisation changes with size

Ways of working break as headcount grows, roughly at each step of a few times in size (founders only; a first team; several teams; departments; divisions). At each step expect new processes, roles, playbooks and management layers. Show the organisation today, at the next step and the one after, using `skills/pipeline/09-management-team/references/saas-talent-and-org-design-template.md` and `skills/pipeline/13-implementation-timeline/references/saas-arr-milestone-timeline.md`.

## Decision 5 — Whom to hire, and when

Justify each senior hire by the stage the company will be at when they start, not by their previous employer's size. Leaders from large companies often expect support teams, manage rather than do, and optimise for politics a small firm does not have. Where senior talent is scarce, budget for developing operators (coaching, secondments, fellowships). Hire sales representatives at least in pairs so results separate the person from the model.

## Decision 6 — What the company will not do this year

Keep a one-page annual focus document: purpose; the year's strategy; four or five major projects; an explicit list of what is **not** funded; and the numbers that track progress. Cotton describes such a document under the name "MSPOT"; the omissions list is the part that protects focus. Template and cadence: `skills/meta-strategy/meta-living-plan-governance/references/living-business-plan-operating-model.md`.

## Decision 7 — Brand and channel mix

- Keep a brand-building budget separate from performance marketing, with its share and reason (pricing power, faster sales cycles, recruiting). Where category awareness is low, early brand spending may pay more; test before claiming.
- Look for a channel whose acquisition cost sits below the ceiling justified by CLV; fund it while the gap lasts and name the signal that it is closing.
- Run at least three acquisition channels so one failure does not halt growth.

## Decision 8 — Growth before profit, or not

Spending ahead of profit is defensible only with a healthy LTV:CAC and a genuinely large, open market. Most plans outside venture-scale markets should aim for Rule-of-40 discipline from about year two. State the choice and the evidence.

**Illustrative example.** A Dar es Salaam logistics SaaS: discounts only after the four confirmations, with sellers capped at 10% and larger cuts traded for annual prepayment; no free tier (hosting route-optimisation for free users is costly); the annual focus document lists "no new country before 40 paying fleets in Tanzania" among its omissions.

## Release checks

- Discount confirmations, approval matrix and trade list stated.
- Free-tier decision recorded with evidence.
- Portfolio position and next-line funding shown.
- Organisation shown now and at the next two size steps.
- Senior hires justified by stage.
- Annual focus document with an omissions list.
- Brand budget, channel count and growth-versus-profit choice stated.
