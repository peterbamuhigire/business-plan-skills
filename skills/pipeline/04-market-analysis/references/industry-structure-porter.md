# Industry Structure Analysis: Strategic Groups, Mobility Barriers and Evolution

Use this reference to describe how firms in an industry differ, why their performance persists, how the competitive rules are changing, and which segment is structurally best. It applies Michael Porter's structural-analysis concepts (strategic groups, mobility barriers, the critique of the product life cycle, driving forces of evolution; Competitive Strategy, 1980) in the engine's own wording. It complements the five-forces work in competitive-strategy-porter.md: five forces explain why an industry is attractive in aggregate; strategic groups explain why performance differs within it; evolution explains how the rules change over time.

## 1. Strategic dimensions: how firms compete

Characterise each competitor on the dimensions that matter in the industry. Typical dimensions:
- Scope and specialisation: product-line width, segments and geography served.
- Brand: brand building versus price competition.
- Demand-building route: pull (end-consumer promotion) versus push (through channel partners).
- Channel choice: company-owned, specialist or broad-line outlets.
- Quality: materials, specification, features.
- Technology position: leader, fast follower, imitator.
- Vertical integration: how much value is added in-house, backwards into inputs or forwards into distribution.
- Cost position: pursuit of low cost through scale and efficiency.
- Service: engineering support, service network, credit.
- Price position relative to the market.
- Financial and operating leverage.
- Relationship to a parent group (resources, objectives, shared operations).
Not every dimension matters in every industry: in a commodity industry brand is near zero but integration, service and cost position vary widely.

## 2. Strategic groups

A strategic group is a set of firms following the same or a similar strategy on the chosen dimensions; it is the intermediate unit between the industry and the single firm.
- Within a group, rivalry is most intense (same customers, same way).
- Between groups, competition is weaker and moderated by mobility barriers.
- Profitability differs by group because some positions face structurally weaker forces.

### Procedure to build the map
1. Choose the two or three dimensions that best separate firms.
2. Plot each significant competitor on a two-axis map (for example price position against breadth or service level).
3. Circle clusters of similar firms; these are the groups.
4. Size each circle by group revenue or share.

Illustration of contrasting groups (appliance-type industry): broad-line, heavily advertised, integrated firms with captive distribution and service; specialist premium producers with selective distribution; low-cost producers of unadvertised private-label products. Each group's main worries differ (technology and import costs; quality dilution; retailer price pressure).

### Questions for the plan
- Which group does the business belong to, and who are its direct rivals?
- How does rivalry behave inside the group (pricing, launches, distribution battles)?
- Which map positions are empty, and is each a real gap or an unviable position?
- Is the target group structurally more or less attractive than the others?

## 3. Mobility barriers

Mobility barriers stop firms moving between groups. They resemble entry barriers but operate inside the industry, and they explain why performance differences persist.

Typical sources of mobility barriers:
- Economies of scale that need years of investment.
- Differentiation: established brands, loyalties and reputations.
- Capital requirements (facilities, research, advertising, distribution).
- Customer switching costs.
- Access to distribution tied up by incumbents.
- Proprietary technology.
- Vertical integration that forces entry at several stages at once.
- Experience and learning effects.

Implication: a group with high barriers on all sides is protected; a group with low barriers on the entry side is exposed to fast imitation. State (1) the group, (2) the barriers protecting it and (3) the barriers the business must overcome if it plans to move groups.

## 4. Industry evolution and the product life cycle

Industries change: forces, groups and attractiveness shift, and a firm that sees the direction early can position first.

Limits of the standard life cycle (introduction, growth, maturity, decline):
1. Duration varies widely; some industries skip stages or revive after apparent decline.
2. Concentration patterns differ; some industries stay concentrated, others fragment or consolidate.
3. Firms can reshape the curve through innovation and repositioning.
4. It describes but does not explain when or why.
Use it only as a rough baseline, then use driving forces to predict how the industry will depart from it.

### Life cycle orientation table

| Dimension | Introduction | Growth | Maturity | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buyers | Higher-income, hesitant, must be convinced | Widening group tolerant of uneven quality | Mass market, repeat buying, brand choice | Sophisticated, switching, low loyalty |
| Products | Poor quality, many variants, no standards | Differentiation, reliability matters | Standardised, superior quality | Little differentiation |
| Marketing | Heavy promotion, high prices | Promotion continues, distribution key | Segmentation, wider lines, service and deals | Minimal spend |
| Operations | Overcapacity, short runs, skilled labour | Undercapacity, move to mass production | Optimum capacity, stable processes | Overcapacity |
| Competition | Few rivals | Many entrants, mergers | Price competition, shakeout, private labels | Exits, few survivors |
| Profitability | High prices, low profit | Highest profit | Falling prices and profit | Low margins |
| Strategic focus | Research, engineering, share | Marketing, cost management | Cost control and stable share | Cost cutting, manage for cash |

## 5. Driving forces of evolution

Test each candidate force: does it change the strength of one or more of the five forces?

| Force | Structural effect |
|---|---|
| Long-run slowdown in growth | Shifts rivalry from expansion to share fights |
| Changes in buyer segments | New groups arrive, or existing groups change in income, sophistication and needs |
| Product innovation | Redefines expectations, creates substitutes, moves entry barriers |
| Process innovation | Changes cost structure, may favour purpose-built newcomers over legacy incumbents |
| Marketing innovation | New channels bypass existing networks; new media shift dynamics |
| Entry or exit of major firms | Reshuffles structure; imports capabilities from other industries |
| Input cost changes | Shifts relative cost positions (local producers versus importers) |
| Regulatory change | Creates or removes barriers, changes costs, opens segments |
| Reduced uncertainty | Risk-averse firms enter as information grows; pioneer advantages erode |
| Complementary products spreading | Changes demand patterns |

Apply: (1) pick the two or three most active forces; (2) state the structural change each will cause and which force it strengthens or weakens; (3) estimate the time horizon; (4) name which firms benefit and which are threatened.

## 6. Industry segmentation

Different segments can have very different structures. Segment by product variety, buyer type (industrial or consumer, large or small, price-sensitive or quality-seeking), geography (local to international) and distribution channel (wholesale, direct, retail, e-commerce). A business may sit in an unattractive segment while an adjacent one is structurally better; focused strategies exploit exactly that.

## 7. Applying it in data-poor markets (East Africa)

Use proxies when formal data is scarce:

| Dimension | How to observe |
|---|---|
| Price position | Physical price surveys, stall audits, buyer interviews |
| Quality and certification | Standards-body marks, ISO certification, export licences, regional origin certificates |
| Distribution | Urban versus rural coverage, supermarket versus market presence, e-commerce presence |
| Vertical integration | Own transport, farm or factory, own brand |
| Brand | Radio and billboard presence, awareness surveys |
| Service | Warranty, after-sales support, credit terms |

Example grouping (dairy): branded processors (pasteurised, packaged, supermarket-distributed, higher price); cooperative processors (semi-processed, wholesale and institutional buyers, medium price); informal traders (raw milk, farm-gate and hawkers, lowest price, no brand). A new entrant chooses a group or creates a new one.

Forces to test for the region (verify each with a dated source): rapid urbanisation; mobile-money reshaping payments, credit and distribution; continental trade liberalisation increasing competition; currency and fuel volatility moving cost positions; digital tax invoicing, national identity and business-registration gateways and regional standards harmonisation changing compliance costs; a very young population with different preferences. Evolution can be compressed (mobile money leapfrogging) or stalled by infrastructure.

## 8. Plan-section prompts (competitive analysis)

1. Map the three to five most significant competitors into groups by price, quality and coverage.
2. State which group the business occupies or intends to join.
3. Assess the mobility barriers around each group.
4. Identify any empty position and explain whether it is an opportunity or structurally impossible.
5. Name the two or three driving forces and their timing.
