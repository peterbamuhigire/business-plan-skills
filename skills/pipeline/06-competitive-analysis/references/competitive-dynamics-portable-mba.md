# Competitive Dynamics: Advantage, Architecture and Complementors

Use in the competitive-analysis section to test whether claimed advantages are real, sustainable and supported by operations.

## 1. Layered competitive advantage
Advantage exists in layers (price, delivery, quality, technology, design, service, brand); the more layers, the more durable the position. Apply five principles:
1. Think relatively: what counts is superiority as perceived against named rivals, not absolute improvement (a firm can improve and still fall behind if rivals improve faster).
2. State disadvantages as well as advantages: every business has dimensions of advantage, parity and disadvantage; ignoring the last overstates position and hides improvement priorities.
3. Seek multiple sources: cost control and differentiation are not alternatives; even premium brands control cost to reinvest in the product.
4. Align onstage and backstage (below).
5. Expect the terms of competition to shift: once a differentiator is universal (accuracy, reliability) it becomes a commodity and the battleground moves (design, features, digital access).

## 2. Onstage and backstage alignment
- Onstage advantage: what customers compare (price, quality, delivery, brand, service). Backstage advantage: the resources and capabilities behind it (cost structure, processes, logistics, organisation, talent).
- Rule: onstage advantages cannot be sustained without backstage support. Before attacking a rival, verify who is backstage-advantaged; choose battlefields where the rival is backstage-constrained (for example, a rival with a lower cost structure will win any price, margin or advertising war, so attack on a dimension it cannot match without alienating its own customers).
- Mapping template: for each onstage criterion (price, quality, delivery speed, brand or trust, after-sale service) record our position, competitor position, backstage driver and whether backstage support is yes, partial or no. Any partial or no row is a vulnerability to address in the plan.

## 3. Purchase and consumption cycle (13 steps)
Need recognition; needs definition; alternatives identification; vendor choice; order placement; payment; enquiry follow-up; delivery; storage; use; maintenance or upgrade; replacement decision; disposal. Each step is a possible differentiation point (triggers and reminders; consultative selling; discovery and referrals; social proof; easy ordering; payment flexibility including mobile money and credit; proactive status updates; delivery reliability; packaging; user support and training; after-sale service; refresh prompts; take-back and recycling).
- Most firms compete only on identification, choice and ordering; those differentiating on early triggers or post-sale steps face less competition and build deeper relationships.
- Audit question: which steps does this business address that direct competitors do not?

## 4. Value chain architecture
- Architecture is the basic design of value creation: which activities are done where and by whom. Disruptive redesigns typically change the architecture (new process technology, customer-performed assembly, direct-to-customer assembly and sale) rather than the product alone.
- Three criteria for choosing an architecture: serves target customers effectively and efficiently; keeps flexibility to scale, contract or switch; is hard for competitors to imitate.
- Plan questions: where does the business add distinctive value versus generic activity? What is done in-house or outsourced? Is the architecture different from incumbents' or a replica? What would a disruptive entrant's architecture look like and how exposed is the model?

## 5. Outsourcing risk taxonomy
- Non-performance: supplier fails on quality, quantity or time. Mitigate with supplier qualification, dual sourcing, service-level contracts.
- Disproportionate value expropriation: a supplier of a critical component captures more industry value than the integrator; risk rises when the activity is on the customer-value critical path, the supplier builds exclusive customer relationships, its capability gets harder to replicate, or the integrator loses the ability to evaluate or switch. Mitigate with intellectual-property ownership, multiple sources and retained capability.
- Elimination: the supplier integrates forward and competes, using knowledge of your costs and customers. Retain core capability.
- Risk register: activity, risk type, probability, impact, mitigation (for example manufacturing: non-performance, dual suppliers and SLA; software: expropriation, IP ownership clauses; logistics: multiple carriers; core platform: retain in-house).
- Every significant outsourcing in the plan must state which risk applies and its mitigation; carry it into the operations plan.

## 6. The value net and complementors
- Porter's five forces omit cooperative relationships. The value net adds complementors: firms whose products raise buyers' willingness to pay for yours, or lower suppliers' required price. Thinking in complements aims to make the pie bigger rather than fighting over a fixed one.
- Five determinants of complementor power: concentration, switching costs, ease of unbundling, pull-through capacity (demand they drive for your product) and asymmetry of integration threat.
- Map each player: competitors, suppliers, customers, complementors (who they are, how they affect us, strategy: differentiate, dual-source, build switching costs, partner or co-market or invest). Ask whether any complementor could become a competitor.

## 7. Integrated workflow
1. Map the arena (value net). 2. Score relative advantage on each customer-valued dimension against the top two or three rivals. 3. Verify backstage alignment for each claimed advantage. 4. Scan the full purchase cycle for uncontested steps. 5. Stress-test the architecture on the three criteria. 6. Audit outsourcing risk. 7. Map complementors and their power.

## 8. Uganda and East Africa applications
- Relative advantage: benchmark against competitors, not absolute standards.
- Onstage and backstage: agri-processors often claim quality and timeliness without certified processing or a logistics system; close the gap before investors find it.
- Purchase cycle: payment (mobile money, credit terms) and disposal (packaging take-back) often build more loyalty than product quality alone.
- Architecture: informal furniture makers delivering within a day of a messaging-app order at a fraction of formal retail prices show a disruptive local architecture.
- Outsourcing: non-performance is the common failure; expropriation is rising in technology outsourcing, so specify IP ownership. Under Ugandan law contractor-created IP needs written assignment to the commissioning company (copyright statute; verify with counsel and the local registration bureau).
- Complementors: mobile money providers (for e-commerce), transport firms (for delivery), power utilities (for cold chain). Success may depend on their reliability more than on rivals.
- Shifting terms: in banking, branch proximity gave way to digital access; any sector in digital transition must state its new battleground.

## 9. Diagnostic checklist for weak competitive sections
Only direct competitors listed (add complementors and substitutes); advantages without disadvantages (add a relative-disadvantage column); onstage claims without backstage evidence (audit capability); analysis stopping at point of sale (map all 13 steps); outsourcing without risk discussion; value chain described but not compared with incumbents; complementors treated as neutral; advantages stated in absolute terms (restate against named rivals).
