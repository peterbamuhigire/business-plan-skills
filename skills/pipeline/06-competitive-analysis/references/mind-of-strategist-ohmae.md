# Strategic Thinking Toolkit (3C, Key Factors, Four Routes)

Use to frame the competitive-analysis section and to turn analysis into distinctive strategy. The approach (Ohmae): analyse rigorously, then reassemble the parts in a new configuration. Neither mechanical step-by-step analysis (marginal gains) nor pure intuition (fails in novel conditions) is sufficient.

## 1. Frame the critical issue
- State problems as solution-oriented questions, not symptoms: "How do we reduce overtime?" becomes "Is the workforce large enough for demand?"; "How do we cut costs?" becomes "Which cost elements are structurally above benchmark?"; "How do we improve sales?" becomes "Do we have the right product for the customers targeted?" A yes or no question points to a category of action and changes what data you collect.
- Issue diagram: (1) state the framed question; (2) identify the major mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive branches; (3) repeat at each branch until terminal items are manageable and measurable; (4) give each terminal item an owner and a test. Example decomposition for a costly product: design too expensive (materials above need, too many parts), fixed costs too high (low utilisation, overhead allocation), variable costs too high (purchasing, yield, scrap). Without it, managers attack the most visible cost (often labour) and miss the structural driver.

## 2. The 3C strategic triangle
Strategy is how a firm differentiates itself positively from competitors, using relative strengths to better satisfy customer needs. Analyse three living parties together:
- Corporation: strengths, functions, cost structure, technology, brand, distribution and finance, always relative to the competitor in the segment.
- Customer: needs, purchasing criteria, decision process, price sensitivity, segments; customers want outcomes, not products, so find the underlying objective they are trying to satisfy.
- Competition: strategy, assumptions, resources, cost structure and corporate context (a division of a conglomerate can cross-subsidise and absorb losses).
- If the firm's approach matches rivals', price becomes the only variable and margins that fund innovation vanish; a gap between how you and rivals satisfy the customer is essential.

## 3. Strategic planning unit (SPU) tests
Define the unit at which strategy is set using three tests: Customer (can it address the total relevant market, not a narrow slice?), Functional (does it control all critical functions: procurement, design, manufacturing, distribution, sales, after-sales, financing?), Competitor (can it see the competitor's whole business, including shared resources?). Too narrow an SPU optimises the wrong contest, for example a product that meets technical specifications but is too heavy for installers because distribution and installation were excluded from design; or a firm with a weak service network that redirected engineering to make a product needing little servicing rather than trying to out-build rivals' service moat.

## 4. Key factors for success (KFS)
Every industry has a few factors that determine success; managers often mistake secondary factors for primary ones.
- Identify by hypothesis: ask experienced operators for the "secret of success"; form candidate hypotheses; test each (firms that succeeded without it? firms that had it and failed?); for validated KFS identify controllable sub-variables; concentrate resources there.
- KFS differ by industry and must not be assumed: for example banking (cost of funds and lending returns), heavy manufacturing (scale and capacity utilisation), beer (scale in distribution rather than brewing), ice cream (seasonal demand management and economical refrigeration through the chain), forestry (land ownership and yield per hectare, which depends on growth rate and hence on the binding input, water or sunlight, so different constraints call for different investments).
- Focus: resources spread across all dimensions give mediocrity everywhere; concentrating on two or three validated KFS gives real superiority where it counts.

## 5. Four routes to strategic advantage
When you cannot match a rival on every dimension:
1. Focus on KFS: master one or two variables and accept deliberate weakness elsewhere; resist "fixing" non-KFS weaknesses. It is self-reinforcing.
2. Build on relative superiority: find where the rival is comparatively weak (region, segment, feature, time period) by studying its organisation and cost structure; concentrate resources where the gap is largest, not where you feel strongest. Method: list every dimension of competition (price, quality, reliability, delivery, service, customisation, brand, financing terms, coverage, compliance, after-sales); score both firms on a five-point scale; compute the gap per dimension; widen positive gaps that a meaningful segment values rather than closing negative gaps on non-KFS dimensions.
3. Pursue aggressive initiatives: challenge shared industry assumptions. Ideal-state method: ask what you would do if all constraints were removed; then ask what prevents reaching it; classify each constraint as legally required, economically required or merely assumed; redirect effort to removing assumed constraints. Zero-based production: design product and production system from scratch around the target customer's need and price tolerance. Value analysis and value engineering: dismantle the best competing product and compare construction, parts count, material grades, assembly and supply chain to find how its cost is lower, then re-engineer without new handicaps.
4. Exploit strategic degrees of freedom (SDF): the dimensions along which the customer's underlying objective can be satisfied more completely. Early competition focuses on obvious SDFs (function, reliability, price); as these saturate, new ones emerge (aesthetics, service, customisation, integration, community, environmental attributes). The pioneer of the next SDF defines the new competitive space.

## 6. Customer segmentation
- Segment by objectives (what outcome the customer seeks, what job the product is hired for) when customers differ functionally; segment by coverage (geography, demographics, channel, account size) when distribution economics or regulation differ by place.
- Objectives first: it reveals the true competitive set (a customer seeking rapid city transit chooses among boda-boda, taxi, public transport and walking, not among boda-boda operators). Then use coverage segmentation as the operational filter for efficient channels.

## 7. Competitor strategy
Inhabit the rival's planner: what perceptions and assumptions underlie its strategy, and what constraints limit its response? For rivals inside larger groups, account for shared resources (cost structure not visible at business-unit level), cross-subsidy capacity (sustaining price wars and investment), and technology inheritance (capabilities not yet visible in current products). A diversified rival can absorb losses that would sink a standalone firm.

## 8. Challenging constraints
When managers list what cannot be done: write each constraint explicitly; ask what alternatives exist if all were removed; take the answer as the ideal state; classify each constraint; then redirect effort to removing the assumed ones. Common rationalisations and challenges: "top management does not understand the front line" (define the missing information and supply it); "people quality has declined" (diagnose training, hiring or role design); "government regulation hamstrings us" (separate legal requirements from industry convention); "competitors have too much clout with distributors" (identify the mechanism: exclusivity, rebates, relationships, service; price the specific cure); "we cannot afford it" (capital or prioritisation? capital constraints blocking KFS investment are strategy problems).

## 9. Profit diagrams
Profit = (price minus cost) x volume. Diagnose each variable separately before choosing a remedy.
- Volume: are we losing existing customers (churn and its cause), failing to acquire (awareness, consideration, conversion), in the wrong channels, or could price stimulate volume enough to offset margin loss?
- Price: is the product truly differentiated, is the segment price- or value-sensitive, is there an unserved premium segment, do payment terms and minimum orders act as hidden discounts?
- Cost: specifications above need (value engineering), fixed cost per unit, variable cost above benchmark (purchasing, yield, scrap), and working-capital cost hidden in operating cost.

## 10. Avoiding tunnel vision
- Under attack, executives narrow to defending the attacked position (cut price, match features) instead of stepping aside to a dimension where the attacker has no advantage.
- Change the objective function: replace "win at all costs" with "avoid the worst outcome" to widen options; for firms in decline define the worst case precisely then find every route around it; for firms on track define the ideal end state and map obstacles.
- Perfectionism as paralysis: list every uncertainty stopping action, estimate probability and impact of a negative resolution, and if none individually changes the result materially, act decisively.

## 11. East African applications
- 3C: enumerate informal players, imported substitutes (invisible at retail, present at wholesale) and the "do nothing" option as competitors.
- KFS: interview the trader who has survived several economic cycles; in local retail the KFS are often supplier credit terms, location and foot traffic, and managed customer credit, with product quality ranking lower than expected.
- Issue diagram: split cost into materials, labour, premises and working capital; float cost from extending credit to customers is often the largest hidden cost in trading businesses and is rarely in informal accounts.
- Constraints: test claims such as "we cannot afford a website", "the market will not pay more than X", "banks do not lend to businesses like ours" for evidence that they are structural rather than assumed.
- SDF: for motorcycle taxis the objective is arriving safely on time; unexploited SDFs include guaranteed delivery windows via booking, parcel delivery, women-rider services for safety-sensitive customers, and commuter subscriptions.
- Relative superiority: local manufacturers rarely beat imports on cost but can win on delivery and restocking speed, after-sales and warranty handling, trade credit, customisation to local conditions and knowledge of local compliance.
- Zero-based design: design agri-processed products around what the local consumer needs at an affordable price with locally available inputs, not by copying imports.
- Segmentation by objective: urban and rural consumers may share one objective (affordable reliable protein, safe financial services); product may be the same while distribution and pack format differ.
- Profit diagram: many SMEs assume volume or cost problems and overlook underpricing; test price elasticity before choosing a remedy.
- Tunnel vision: answering a new entrant with an immediate price cut; instead find the dimension of the triangle the entrant does not address.

## 12. Three tools to use first
1. 3C audit at the start of any competitive analysis (relative strengths, customer objective, real competitors including informal and substitutes).
2. KFS identification before recommending strategy; test each factor against successes and failures.
3. Four-routes assessment for each gap; do not default to route one when routes two, three or four are less contested.
