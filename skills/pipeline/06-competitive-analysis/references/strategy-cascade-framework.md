# Strategy Choice Cascade: Plan-Section Reference

Use to turn a business plan's strategy into an integrated set of five choices. Strategy is not a vision, a plan or a list of best practices; it is a coordinated set of choices producing sustainable advantage (Lafley and Martin, Playing to Win).

## 1. The five choices (cascade)
1. Winning aspiration: the purpose and what winning looks like (specific and measurable: category position, revenue target, impact).
2. Where to play: geography, product type, consumer segment, channel, vertical stage.
3. How to win: cost leadership or differentiation on the chosen field.
4. Capabilities that must be in place: three to six that reinforce one another.
5. Management systems and measures that enable those capabilities.
- The cascade is iterative, not linear; where to play and how to win are tightly coupled and form the heart of strategy; larger firms nest cascades (company, division, product). A complete cascade fits on one page.
- Illustration to reuse as a pattern: a packaged-snacks processor sets an aspiration of category leadership in a defined region within a stated period; chooses geography (home market first, then neighbours), segments (health-conscious urban consumers, institutional buyers), channels (supermarkets, convenience, online), products and vertical stage (contract sourcing, processing, packaging, distribution but not retail); wins by differentiation (quality, local sourcing, distinctive brand, premium-but-accessible price); builds capabilities (farmer sourcing network, certified processing, brand insight, cold chain, innovation); and runs systems (HACCP, sales dashboards, farmer reviews, brand tracking, stage-gate). Each choice constrains and enables the others.

## 2. Five strategy traps
| Trap | Failure | Avoidance |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy as vision | No choices behind the statement | Convert to where-to-play and how-to-win |
| Strategy as plan | Activities that may not add to advantage | Base plan on integrated choices; ask if it creates a unique position |
| Strategy as emergent | Reactive; prey to strategic rivals | Make deliberate choices under uncertainty and revise them |
| Strategy as optimisation | Optimising the wrong activities | Ask whether activities are the right ones first |
| Strategy as best practice | Sameness, mediocrity | Seek distinctive choices |
Self-test: a vision on the wall without choices (trap 1); an annual budget with projects but no competitive logic (2); "wait and see" as habit (3); planning meetings about cost cutting only (4); "copy the leader but do it better" (5).

## 3. Where to play
- Dimensions and questions: geography (which regions, cities, districts), product type, consumer segment (mass, middle-class professionals, high-net-worth), distribution channel (direct, modern trade, market stalls, e-commerce, wholesale), vertical stage (sourcing, manufacturing, distribution, retail, after-sales).
- Choose where not to play, with reasons; "everywhere" is not a choice.
- Documentation template: for each of the five dimensions record where we will play; then where we will not play and why. Example pattern: a retailer serving the capital and the largest regional towns through own stores for middle-income households, excluding rural trading centres (insufficient purchasing power), wholesale (margin erosion) and financial services (outside competence).

## 4. How to win
- Cost leadership: lower unit cost than every competitor (only one true low-cost player exists); funds lower prices or reinvestment. Example driver: high-capacity milling with direct farmer sourcing giving a per-kilogram cost gap.
- Differentiation: customers choose you at a higher price because of something they value; the premium must exceed the cost of delivering it. Example driver: traceable single-origin coffee with distinctive packaging and story.
- Consider with where to play: premium differentiation needs customers with willingness to pay; cost leadership needs price-driven markets where the lowest cost is achievable.
- Industry structure is not fixed: a firm can reshape the context (for example giving a product away to win share and earning from services; a fintech offering zero-fee transfers to build scale then earning from merchant payments).

## 5. Activity system mapping
- Capabilities form a system of reinforcing activities; advantage comes from the fit, not any single capability. Steps: list three to six core capabilities; place each as a node; draw reinforcing links; identify the critical links that are hardest to copy.
- Three tests: feasibility (can we build and run it?), distinctiveness (different from rivals'?), defensibility (hard to replicate as a system?).
- Reverse-engineer competitors: identify their apparent where-to-play and how-to-win, map supporting capabilities and links, find gaps, then design your system around those gaps (for example a bottled-water entrant targeting the absence of subscription office delivery and digital ordering that a large-plant competitor lacks).
- Build at the lowest indivisible level (one coherent set of choices) and aggregate upwards.

## 6. Strategy logic flow (four dimensions, seven questions)
1. Industry analysis: (a) distinct segments? (b) structural attractiveness of each (five forces)? Favour low supplier and buyer power, moderate rivalry, high entry barriers, few substitutes.
2. Customer analysis: (c) what do channel customers value? (d) what do end consumers value, and what are their unmet needs and reasons for choice?
3. Relative position: (e) capabilities relative to competitors? (f) cost position relative to competitors?
4. Competitor reaction: (g) how will competitors respond to our choices; can we choose a field they will not follow onto?
Iterate; challenge accepted industry maps. Illustration pattern: a pharmacy chain reading segments (hospital, standalone, chain, online; urban versus rural; prescription versus over-the-counter), segment attractiveness (licensing barriers), customer value (availability, genuine medicines, advice, price), relative position (multiple sites, trained pharmacists, bulk buying versus higher fixed costs) and likely reactions of each rival type.

## 7. Reverse engineering a strategic choice (seven steps)
1. Frame the choice as at least two mutually exclusive options. 2. Generate possibilities, each as a positive narrative. 3. For each ask what would have to be true for it to be a great choice (sceptics state their doubts as conditions). 4. Identify the least likely conditions (barriers). 5. Design tests for the barriers only. 6. Run focused, time-bound tests (market research, financial modelling, pilots, expert interviews). 7. Choose the possibility whose conditions hold most robustly.
- Benefits: converts critics into contributors, separates logic from opinion, sets a standard of proof, and turns barriers into research questions.
- Pattern example: expand through physical stores in secondary towns versus launching e-commerce. Conditions for stores: enough middle-class population with discretionary spend, rents allowing break-even in a stated time, reliable upcountry supply, low competition. For e-commerce: customers comfortable ordering online, affordable last-mile delivery, adequate digital payment adoption, customer acquisition cost below store foot-traffic cost. Test the barrier conditions first.

## 8. Cascade to business plan mapping
| Choice | Plan sections | What to write |
|---|---|---|
| Aspiration | Executive summary, vision and mission, company overview | Measurable winning position |
| Where to play | Market analysis, target market, competitive analysis | Five dimensions plus exclusions; size the addressable market inside the boundaries |
| How to win | Competitive analysis, value proposition, marketing, pricing | Cost or differentiation; how choices reinforce; margin advantage |
| Capabilities | Operations, team, technology, partnerships | Three to six as an activity system; exist versus must build |
| Management systems | Operations, financial plan, milestones, KPIs | Quality systems, dashboards, review cadence, controls |

### Integration checklist
Aspiration specific; all five where-to-play dimensions explicit; exclusions documented; how-to-win is cost or differentiation (dual only if justified) and fits the field; capabilities presented as a reinforcing system passing the three tests; management systems enable them; competitor activity systems reverse-engineered; logic flow applied; financials consistent with the choices (a differentiation strategy shows premium pricing); barrier conditions and tests identified.

### Common plan mistakes
"We will serve everyone" (no where-to-play); "excellent customer service" (undefined how-to-win: define measurably and show the margin advantage); "no competitors" (at minimum the customer's do-nothing alternative); fifteen core capabilities (cut to three to six that reinforce); strategy disconnected from financials (align pricing, cost structure and investment).

### Strategy-on-a-page template
Company; winning aspiration (one or two sentences); where to play (geography, products, segments, channels, vertical stage); where we will not play; how to win (type, specific advantage, why rivals cannot easily copy it); core capabilities as a reinforcing system (three to six); management systems; key barrier conditions tested with results.

## 9. Warning signs and signs of a winning strategy
Warning patterns: do-it-all (no choices); Don Quixote (attacking the strongest rival head-on); Waterloo (fighting on many fronts); something-for-everyone; dreams-that-never-come-true (aspiration without choices); programme-of-the-month (generic industry strategies).
Winning signs: an activity system unlike any rival's; customers who adore you and non-customers who cannot see why anyone would buy; rivals who profit doing what they do (so they need not attack your core); more resources than rivals; rivals attacking one another instead of you; customers looking to you first for innovation.

Sources consulted: Lafley and Martin, Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works (Harvard Business Review Press, 2013); Porter on activity systems and five forces. Local illustrations are hypothetical patterns; replace with plan-specific evidence.
