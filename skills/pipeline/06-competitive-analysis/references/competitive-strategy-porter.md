# Industry Structure and Competitive Positioning (Porter Frameworks)

Use for the industry-attractiveness and positioning parts of the competitive analysis. Core argument (Porter, 1980): profitability depends on industry structure and on the position a firm chooses within it; strategy is choosing a position defensible against the competitive forces.

## 1. Five forces
Rate each force High, Medium or Low with the reasons.
1. Threat of new entrants. Barriers: economies of scale, product differentiation and brand loyalty, capital requirements, switching costs, access to distribution, cost advantages independent of scale (proprietary technology, location, learning curve, subsidies), government policy. Expected retaliation (history of retaliation, spare capacity, slow growth) also deters entry. Ask: capital to reach efficient scale, brand or switching-cost protection, whether channels are open, licences and standards needed, past incumbent retaliation.
2. Supplier power. Higher when suppliers are few and more concentrated than the industry, do not depend on it for revenue, sell differentiated products or impose switching costs, or can integrate forward. Ask: number of suppliers per key input, substitutability, share of supplier revenue, switching cost, forward integration ability.
3. Buyer power. Higher when buyers are concentrated or buy in volume, products are standard, the product is a large share of buyer cost, buyers earn low profits, can integrate backward, or are fully informed. Consumers are less price-sensitive on high-involvement or performance-critical products; institutional buyers behave as rational economic actors.
4. Substitutes. Threat is high when substitutes improve in price-performance or come from high-profit industries; they cap the price ceiling. Ask: what else meets the underlying need, price-performance trend, switching cost, substitute producers' resources.
5. Rivalry. Intensified by numerous or balanced rivals, slow growth, high fixed or storage costs, weak differentiation or low switching costs, capacity added in large increments, diverse rivals, high strategic stakes and high exit barriers. Ask: number of comparable rivals, growth, differentiation, ease of exit, recent price, advertising or product wars.

Summary format: force, intensity (H/M/L), key factors, overall industry attractiveness with rationale, and the strategic implication (which forces the strategy must address to earn above-average returns).

## 2. Generic strategies
Two sources of advantage (lower cost, differentiation) by scope (broad, narrow) give cost leadership, differentiation, cost focus and differentiation focus.
- Cost leadership: lowest cost structure (deployed as price leadership or higher margin). Requires efficient scale, experience-curve reductions, tight overhead control, avoiding marginal accounts, minimal spend on peripheral functions. Protects against buyers, suppliers, entrants and substitutes. Risks: technology change nullifying investment, imitation, missing product or marketing shifts, cost inflation. Choose when share is large, products commodity-like and buyers price-sensitive.
- Differentiation: uniqueness on attributes buyers value widely (design, service, network, technology, brand, delivery reliability) rewarded with a price premium. Builds loyalty, insulates from price rivalry, raises entry barriers, limits buyer power. Risks: cost gap too wide, buyers' need fades, imitation. Choose when preferences are diverse and the firm can sustain uniqueness.
- Focus: narrow buyer group, product segment or geography served better than broad rivals. Cost focus exploits segment cost differences; differentiation focus serves special needs broad players overlook. Risks: broad rivals target the segment, segment differences narrow, sub-segment focusers appear. Choose when the segment is different, underserved and large enough but unattractive to big rivals.
- Stuck in the middle: no advantage on cost, differentiation or focus means below-average returns.

## 3. Competitor analysis (four diagnostic components)
1. Future goals (financial, market, strategic, management): a share-seeker behaves differently from a profit-maximiser; distressed rivals make desperate moves.
2. Current strategy (generic strategy, pricing, R&D, advertising, service, alliances): reveals commitments and vulnerabilities.
3. Assumptions about itself and the industry: wrong assumptions are blind spots to exploit.
4. Capabilities (core strengths and weaknesses, ability to grow, ability to respond quickly).
Response profile questions: Is the rival satisfied with its position? What moves will it likely make? Where is it vulnerable? Which moves provoke the strongest retaliation? How can we avoid triggering a damaging response?
- Good versus bad competitors: good ones stabilise the industry (absorb demand swings, deter entry through niches, improve structure, avoid destructive pricing); bad ones destabilise (irrational pricing, over-expansion, survival-driven tactics). It can be rational to preserve good competitors while attacking bad ones.
- Competitor intelligence system: sources (filings, trade press, job advertisements, sales staff, suppliers and distributors, industry observers, reverse engineering), files with named owners, regular profile updates and sharing with strategy, sales and product teams.

## 4. Market signals
Signals are competitor actions or communications that reveal intentions: prior announcements, post-hoc explanations, statements on industry conditions, capacity announcements, and statements in industry forums. Compare words with deeds; weight signals costly to fake (irreversible commitments); watch for retaliation signals after your own moves. Make your own credible signals (capacity, long-term supplier contracts, public commitments) to deter unwanted moves cheaply.

## 5. Strategic groups
A strategic group is a cluster of firms following similar strategies. Define by dimensions (specialisation, brand versus commodity, technology stance, cost position, service level, channel, geographic scope, vertical integration); plot firms on two axes (bubble size for revenue). Mobility barriers (brand investment, scale, distribution access, proprietary technology) protect groups. Rivalry is strongest within a group; profitability differs by group; an empty map space may be an opportunity or a trap.

## 6. Competitive moves
- Moves less likely to provoke retaliation: quality improvements without price cuts, market-expanding moves, moves signalled in advance, moves letting rivals save face. More likely: direct price cuts in a powerful rival's core segment, threats to strategic objectives, perceived unprovoked aggression.
- Credible commitment (irreversible investment, long lease, dedicated facility, public statement) deters response.
- Focal points (natural boundaries, psychological price points, historical market shares) predict where battles settle.

## 7. Applying the frameworks in East Africa
- Poultry example of a five-force reading: entry high (low capital for small producers), supplier power medium (few hatcheries, feed dominates cost), buyer power low to medium (fragmented buyers, stronger supermarkets), substitutes medium (fish, beef, pork), rivalry high (many small producers, commodity product). Overall low attractiveness, so strategy should lean on cost discipline, vertical integration or differentiation (certified, branded, restaurant-direct). Re-score with current evidence.
- Private secondary education example: entry medium, supplier power low, buyer power low (switching costs for parents), substitutes low, rivalry medium (location-based; reputation sticky). Overall moderate to high attractiveness; build reputation and exam results.
- Cost leadership fits agricultural commodity processing, mass-market consumer goods facing imports and logistics; differentiation fits tourism and hospitality, professional services and premium food and beverage (coffee, specialty grains, certified organic); focus suits SMEs serving a district, buyer type (institutions, NGOs, hotels) or a premium niche in a commodity market. Stuck in the middle is the default SME failure mode: informal firms undercut on price and the firm has no brand, standard or service promise.
- Low-data adaptation of the four components: future goals from director interviews, events and new branch registrations; current strategy from price surveys, product audits, mystery shopping and supplier talks; assumptions from association meetings, local press and public statements versus actions; capabilities from job advertisements, trade-credit terms and visible capacity use. See the due-diligence OSINT reference for a layered method.

Sources consulted: Porter, Competitive Strategy (Free Press, 1980); Porter, Competitive Advantage (1985).
