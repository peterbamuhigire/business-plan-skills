# Value Chain, Core Competencies and Capability Analysis

Use this reference when Section 03 (Products and Services), Section 06 (Competitive Analysis) and Section 08 (Operations) must explain how the business delivers its offer, what makes it distinctive, which activities to keep in-house, and where capability gaps lie. The tools are standard strategy frameworks: value-chain design, core competence (Prahalad and Hamel), the resource-based view with VRIO (Barney), capability analysis, stakeholder mapping and capability-gap analysis. The worksheets are this engine's own; all examples are placeholders to be replaced with the plan's verified facts.

## 1. Value-chain design

The value chain is the set of linked activities, upstream to downstream, performed alone and with suppliers, customers and partners to turn inputs into outputs. Two design decisions: the basic architecture, and which activities to perform internally versus outsource.
Architecture criteria (they often conflict, so record the trade-off):
| Criterion | Question |
|---|---|
| Effectiveness and efficiency | Does it let us market, sell and deliver to target customers best? |
| Flexibility | Can we scale, change the product bundle and switch architecture? |
| Defensibility | Is it hard for competitors to copy and neutralise? |
Outsourcing rule: do not perform an activity that others do more effectively and efficiently. Outsourcing risks and safeguards:
| Risk | Safeguard |
|---|---|
| Non-performance (partner declines or shifts priorities) | Alternative suppliers; partial in-house capability; equity stake |
| Value expropriation (partner takes most of the profit) | Retain critical non-substitutable activities; keep upgrading your competences; take early equity |
| Elimination (partner squeezes you out) | Keep your activities critical, superior, non-substitutable and hard to imitate |
Managing the chain: excellence in each activity (understand its cost and value drivers); internal integration (design, manufacturing, marketing, service and distribution share insight, cutting cycle times); external integration with supplier and customer chains (electronic links, meetings, proximity).
Plan worksheet, per major activity: name (sourcing, production, distribution); in-house or outsourced and why; key cost drivers; contribution to customer value; integration with adjacent activities. Illustration: a coffee processor keeps field sourcing and export sales in-house, owns washing, and outsources dry milling and freight because the specialist's cost per kilogram beats the capital cost of building its own mill (compute the comparison from quotes).

## 2. Core competencies

A core competence combines multiple technologies (hard and soft), collective learning across levels and functions, and the capacity to share across business and geographic boundaries. Working formula: competence = technology x governance process x collective learning (multiplicative, so technology alone is under-used without governance and learning).
Composition: people-embodied knowledge (tacit and explicit) and capital-embodied knowledge (proprietary and vendor-based).
Capability versus core competence:
| | Capability | Core competence |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Often one function or unit | Permeates the organisation |
| Effect | Table stakes to play | Differential advantage to win |
| Imitability | Often acquired or copied | Hard to copy because it bundles technology, customer knowledge and learning |
Three tests: differentiation (distinct customer value), transcendence (spans current and new businesses), inimitability. Few firms sustain more than five or six.
Five management tasks to leverage competences: absorb new knowledge; integrate knowledge streams through cross-disciplinary teams; share across boundaries; learn to forget outdated dominant logic; deploy across units and align administrative systems.
Audit worksheet: competence name; technologies; knowledge and skills bundled; customer value created; products it underpins; can competitors imitate it and why not; investment to develop it further. Illustration: mobile-first financial product design for unbanked customers, bundling interface design, agent-network management, local-language localisation and informal-savings knowledge.

## 3. Capability analysis (resource-based view)

Tangible assets (equipment, property, cash, inventory) are necessary but rarely sufficient; intangible assets (tacit market knowledge, supplier relationships, efficient back-end processes, brand reputation) are usually the real building blocks and are harder to copy. A true capability is a repeatable, tacit set of elements that lets the firm perform consistently, not a one-off success.
Four steps:
1. Determine the value chain, working backwards from the customer; identify key players, shares and bargaining power at each stage.
2. Isolate core capabilities (usually three to six) from processes (the few driving most cost, time and quality), people (critical skill areas; focus on key positions, not just strong individuals) and systems or technologies.
3. Test alignment: internally (people, processes and systems reinforce each other) and externally (fit with the value proposition: product leadership, operational excellence or customer intimacy).
4. Test sustainability: imitability (limited by scarcity, social complexity, causal ambiguity) and durability (patent expiry, talent attrition, technology obsolescence).
Core versus non-core: table-stakes activities are candidates for outsourcing or partnering; protect and invest in the differentiating ones.
SWOT link: strengths are valuable, rare, hard-to-imitate capabilities; weaknesses are gaps against what the strategy needs.
Worksheet per capability: name; core processes; critical people and skills; systems; internal alignment (strong, moderate, weak); external alignment (yes, partial, no); imitability; durability in years; core or non-core; outsourcing candidate and partner. Illustration: cold-chain reliability on a cross-border corridor built from tracked refrigerated trucks, trained drivers and a temperature-monitoring protocol; record what it cost and how long it took to build.

## 4. Competitive advantage in products and services

Two levels: onstage (price, quality, delivery, features, image) and backstage (resources and competences producing it). Onstage advantage cannot last without backstage superiority.
Five principles: think relative to competitors; state advantages and disadvantages (net advantage matters); seek multiple sources; align onstage and backstage; expect terms to shift as customer priorities change and rivals reach parity.
Link capabilities to needs by analysing the whole purchase and consumption cycle: recognise need, choose, order, pay, receive, use, maintain, replace. Each stage is a differentiation point. Differentiation works even in apparent commodities and does not always raise cost; sources: performance, customisation, speed, service quality, brand, relationships, convenience. Multiple differentiators (speed plus service plus convenience) are harder to copy than a price cut.

## 5. Strategy for small firms

Vulnerabilities: little room for error; weak bargaining power with suppliers and channels; dependence on one product, outlet or customer group. Advantages: closeness to customers, flexible mind-set, agility.
SWOT for small firms: judge strengths and weaknesses relative to rivals (including emerging ones), not the firm's own history; include industry fragmentation in the opportunity and threat analysis (fragmented industries that large players cannot easily dominate suit small firms); ask how strengths become customer value and which customer needs are opportunities.
Focus or niche strategy usually suits small firms. Niche questions: which segment can we serve; why is it attractive to us; how do we use our strengths to win; how do we protect against current and future rivals. Warning: the closest competitors are usually similar small firms, so argue why you beat them, not why you beat large players.
Value-chain questions for small firms: key activities; cost of each; customer benefit of each; how to integrate to add value; how to manage resources to maximise value. Internet-based competitors may reshape any activity, so a small firm that is best at one activity can beat vertically integrated rivals.
Specialise when demand is highly segmented, small pockets avoid head-on competition, you can stay close to vendors, channels and customers, and limited resources would be spread thin by a broad approach. Illustration: a regional food processor targets a niche (for example school canteens) where proximity to farms lowers transport cost and local relationships and flavour adaptation give advantages national producers cannot match; quantify with measured transport costs.

## 6. Stakeholder value mapping

Create value for one stakeholder by shortchanging another is untenable; align interests.
1. Identify: primary stakeholders (customers, employees, financiers, suppliers; the business cannot sustain advantage without them) and secondary stakeholders (government, media, community groups, competitors, interest groups).
2. Analyse interests with a priorities matrix: rows are issues (product safety, job fulfilment, financial returns, environmental impact); columns are stakeholder groups; score importance from critical to unimportant.
3. Assess each strategic option's positive or negative impact on each group; use it to weigh options and find ways to align interests.
Plan output, a stakeholder value map: customers (specific value); employees (skills, fair pay, meaningful work); suppliers (reliable orders, fair terms); community (local jobs, stewardship); investors (return, growth); government (tax, compliance, sector development). Illustration: an off-grid solar firm maps reduced fuel spend for households, installer income and training, rural electrification targets, safer indoor air and study time, and recurring pay-as-you-go revenue for investors.

## 7. Capability-gap analysis

Four stages:
1. Envision 3 to 5 year scenarios, including plausible less likely ones; ask which success factors gain prominence.
2. Profile the ideal player: highest rating on each current and emerging success factor.
3. Stretch the ambition: good, strong or leader; goalposts move. Avoid three traps: the ideal player of yesterday (great today, lacking tomorrow's capabilities); the ideal player of tomorrow today (building capabilities before customers need them, premature investment); aim at the ideal player of tomorrow (capabilities matched to future factors, developed in time).
4. Specify the gap: rate competitive position against each factor; any shortfall is a gap; set realistic but stretching targets phrased with active verbs. Targeting the gap (what must improve) is distinct from bridging it (which strategic option achieves it).
Bridging options:
| Segment posture | Short term | Long term |
|---|---|---|
| Invest | Marketing push; price to gain share | Invest in assets, processes, staff and training |
| Hold | Cut variable cost; tweak price | Cut variable cost; recompete; alliances |
| Enter | Prepare a robust project plan | Leverage strengths into related segments |
| All | Benchmark overhead and marketing | Cut overhead; redesign processes; outsource; invest in core competences |
Worksheet: success factor; current rating (1 to 5); ideal rating; target in three years; gap; short-term bridge; long-term bridge; investment (from quotes). Illustration: a hotel targeting business travellers rates itself below the ideal on connectivity; the short-term bridge is a bandwidth upgrade, the long-term bridge is fibre plus backup link, each costed from supplier quotes.

## 8. Resource-based strategy and VRIO

A resource gives sustained advantage only if it is valuable (exploits opportunities or neutralises threats), rare, costly to imitate, and exploited by the organisation.
| Test | If no |
|---|---|
| Valuable | Competitive disadvantage |
| Rare | Parity only |
| Costly to imitate | Temporary advantage |
| Organised to capture value | Unrealised potential |
Limits to imitation: scarcity, social complexity, causal ambiguity, path dependence. Strategically valuable resources are distinctive, hard to copy, durable, hard to appropriate (not lost when staff leave) and unlikely to be substituted soon (Collis and Montgomery).
Three investment strategies: invest in resources you have; leverage them into adjacent markets; upgrade by building resources you should have. Winners are those that use resources best, not those with the most; keep the market context in view.
Worksheet: resource; valuable, rare, inimitable, organised (each with an explanation); implication (disadvantage, parity, temporary advantage, sustained advantage); investment plan. Illustration: a craft brewer's locally sourced ingredient network passes the tests because it took years of relationship building and is backed by a sourcing manager, quality protocols and seasonal contracts.
