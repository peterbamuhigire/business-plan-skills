# Product Development Lifecycle: From Idea to Growth

Use this reference when Section 03 (Products and Services) and Section 08 (Operations) must show how a product moves from idea to market with customer validation at each gate, what it will cost and how it will be funded. Stage names and gates follow value-driven development practice (Bates) combined with innovation-management theory (Tidd and Bessant). Cost, timeline and acquisition-cost figures are drivers to be estimated from quotes and pilots; do not copy any example range without a dated local source.

## 1. Stage-gate overview

Idea, concept development, prototype, IP protection, minimum viable product (MVP), launch, growth. Each stage has a customer-evidence gate (discovery, validation, testing, feedback, market data). At each gate update the business model canvas: mark assumptions validated or invalidated.

## 2. Stage 1: idea generation and validation

Idea sources: an observed market gap; the founder's own frustration; technology transfer to a new context; customer conversations; industry trends (for example urbanisation raising demand for ready-to-eat food). Local illustrations: cold-chain gaps for rural farmers; artisan marketplaces; solar irrigation adapted from other regions; mobile-money integration requested by retailers.

Painkiller versus vitamin: a painkiller solves an urgent, existing problem and customers pay now; a vitamin is optional and must prove habit formation. Frame each product as a painkiller: name the pain, who feels it and how severely.

Validation steps:
1. Identify the pain point.
2. Run customer discovery interviews (aim for at least 20 to 30).
3. Test willingness to pay.
4. Map to the canvas (segments, propositions, channels).
5. Decide build or buy.
Interview guide: Who has the problem (role, context)? How do they solve it today? What do they dislike? How much do they spend? What would ideal look like? Rule: never ask "would you buy this?"; ask about current behaviour and spending.

## 3. Stage 2: concept development

Customer persona template: name (fictional but realistic); demographics; role; goals; frustrations; current solution; decision factors (price, quality, convenience, trust); one-sentence quote. Build one per target segment from interview data.

Proof of concept options:
| Type | Use | Cost driver |
|---|---|---|
| Paper prototype | Services, apps | Time only |
| Landing-page test | Digital products | Domain and hosting |
| Manual MVP | Marketplace, logistics | Staff time running the process by hand |
| Physical mock-up | Consumer products | Fabrication or 3D printing |
| Pilot | B2B services | Serving five to ten customers manually |

Validation checklist: at least 10 customers have seen the concept; at least half show strong interest (not politeness); at least three offer to pay or pre-order; key objections documented and addressed; canvas updated.

## 4. Stage 3: prototype

Make or license:
| Factor | Make | License |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Full | Limited |
| Capital | High | Low (royalty income) |
| Speed | Slower | Faster |
| Risk | You bear it | Licensee bears production risk |
| Margin per unit | Higher | Lower (royalty rates vary by industry; obtain benchmarks) |
| Best for | Differentiated core product | Non-core products, geographic expansion |
Process: design specifications (function, materials, dimensions); first functional prototype; customer testing with 10 to 20 users; iteration (often two to five rounds); production-ready design; protect intellectual property before wide sharing.
Testing protocol: set the goal (usability, desirability, willingness to pay); recruit participants matching the persona; observe without leading; record systematically, including disconfirming feedback; treat what three or more users raise independently as the priority.

## 5. Stage 4: MVP

| | Prototype | MVP |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Does it work? | Will customers buy? |
| Audience | Team and test users | Real market |
| Revenue | None | Real charges |
| Quality | Rough, functional | Good enough to deliver value |
Estimate MVP cost and timeline by business type (app, physical consumer product, service, food and beverage, software platform) from quotes; show low and high cases.
Manufacturing options: local manufacture (lower transport cost, local benefit, limited capacity for specialised goods); import components and assemble locally (electronics, solar); full import (higher cost, faster for complex products).

## 6. Stage 5: launch

Pre-launch checklist: regulatory approvals (standards body, revenue authority, sector licences); supply chain tested through at least one full production run; prices validated with real transactions; at least two distribution channels; marketing materials (website, brochures, social media); customer-support process.
Four launch pillars (Bates): content marketing to educate the market; social media for community and proof; paid advertising to the ideal customer profile; public relations and partnerships for third-party credibility.
Customer acquisition cost by channel: referrals (lowest; services and B2B), organic social (consumer and apps), paid digital (e-commerce, software), agent networks (financial services, agritech), events and activations (premium and B2B). Measure your own cost per channel in the pilot; plan against measured values and compare with lifetime value.

## 7. Innovation strategy (Tidd and Bessant)

Four types (the "4Ps"): product (new or improved offer); process (new way of making or delivering, for example mobile-money collection); position (new market or segment); paradigm (new business model, for example pay-as-you-go replacing one-off purchase).
Strategy approaches by risk and return: incremental (low risk, steady); modular (new components within existing architecture, medium); architectural (new linkages between components, medium-high); radical (new product and market, high risk, very high potential return).
Dynamic-capabilities view (Teece and Pisano): the R&D section should cover competitive position (assets and competencies), technological paths (opportunities and industry direction) and organisational processes (ability to learn, adapt and reconfigure).

## 8. Funding by stage

| Stage | Appropriate sources |
|---|---|
| Idea validation | Bootstrapping, personal savings |
| Prototype | Family and friends, angel investors |
| MVP | Angels, grants, competitions |
| Launch | Seed funding, bank loans, crowdfunding |
| Growth | Venture capital, development finance institutions |
Size each round from the bottom-up cost build, not from typical ranges. For terms and valuation see `../../11-funding-request/references/equity-term-sheets.md` and `business-valuation-methods.md` there.

## 9. Canvas update by stage

| Block | Idea | Concept | Prototype | MVP | Launch |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer segments | Hypothesis | Validated personas | Confirmed | Paying customers | Segmented |
| Value propositions | Problem statement | Solution concept | Tested | Priced | Positioned |
| Channels | Assumed | Explored | Tested | Active | Optimised |
| Revenue streams | Model chosen | Willingness tested | Pricing set | First revenue | Scaled |
| Key resources | Identified | Costed | Procured | Operational | Scaled |

## 10. Plan-section prompts

- Which stage is the product at, and which gate evidence exists?
- What is the cost and time to the next gate, and who funds it?
- What is the make-or-license decision and why?
- Which approvals are needed before launch?
- What is the acquisition cost by channel, measured or assumed?
