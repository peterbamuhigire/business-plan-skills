# Business Model Design, Value Capture and Renewal

Use this reference when Section 03 (Products and Services) must explain how the business creates, delivers and captures value; when Section 06 (Competitive Analysis) must separate a copyable model from a defensible strategy; when Section 10 needs a revenue-model logic; and when the plan must show that management can renew the model. The core ideas follow Teece's work on business models and dynamic capabilities; the application is this engine's own.

## 1. Definition and the two halves of a model

A business model is the logic by which an enterprise creates value for customers and converts part of it into profit. It is logically prior to strategy: without a working model there is nothing to position.

| Half | Questions the plan must answer |
|---|---|
| Value-creation architecture | Which customer segments? Which needs? Which technologies and capabilities underpin the offer? What do partners, suppliers and channels contribute? |
| Value-capture mechanism | Pricing model (per unit, subscription, licence, freemium, usage-based, outcome-based)? Cost structure? How do revenues flow and margins arise? |

A model is more than a revenue model. Test it by showing the evidence for the customer value proposition and a viable structure of revenues and costs.

## 2. Model versus strategy

| | Business model | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Describes | Logic of creating and capturing value; the architecture | How the firm competes and builds advantage; the posture |
| Copyable? | Often, by rivals | Should make the model hard to copy |
| Role | Necessary, not sufficient | Built on the model |
A good model (for example subscriptions) is easily imitated; strategy makes this firm's version hard to replicate. A plan needs both: a viable model where creation and capture align, and a strategy that impedes imitation.

## 3. Value creation versus value capture

Creation is the utility delivered to customers. Capture is retaining enough of it as profit. Failure patterns to test for:
- The product is copied and customers, not the inventor, keep the value.
- Intermediaries take most of the margin so the business is not viable.
- A platform lets third parties extract value without contributing.
Appropriability mechanisms to evaluate and document:
1. Intellectual property (patents, trademarks, trade secrets; weaker for services, useful for branded goods).
2. Complementary assets that must be controlled to commercialise (distribution, manufacturing capacity, customer relationships).
3. Speed to build switching costs before competition intensifies.
4. Network effects.
5. Complexity and tacit knowledge embedded in processes and relationships.
Also list local ones: licences and approvals, exclusive supply agreements, location, community trust.

## 4. Types of business-model innovation

New revenue model (units to subscription, licence or usage); new customer segment (previously unserved); new value proposition; new delivery channel; new cost architecture (asset-light); ecosystem reconfiguration (changing roles of partners, suppliers and customers). Well-known illustrations: leasing equipment on a per-use basis to open access to those unable to buy; selling a low-priced core item with high-margin consumables; converting internal infrastructure into an outward-facing service; using a telecom network as a financial-services channel (mobile money).
Conditions favouring model innovation over product innovation: a nascent industry; a technology transition; regulatory change; shifting customer preferences; underserved markets. In East Africa, mobile money, digital agricultural extension, e-learning and telemedicine all meet these conditions.

## 5. Archetypes (choose and combine)

| Archetype | Logic | Local examples to consider |
|---|---|---|
| Product seller | Make and sell goods | Food processing, crafts, manufacturing |
| Service provider | Sell time, expertise or capability | Consulting, health, legal, construction |
| Platform or marketplace | Connect buyers and sellers for a commission | Online retail, ride-hailing, property listings |
| Subscription | Recurring fee for continued access | Software, security services, gyms |
| Freemium | Free basic tier, paid premium | Apps, professional networks |
| Lock-in (core plus consumables) | Low-price core, high-margin consumables or services | Printers and ink, generators and fuel |
| Asset-light or franchise | License brand and system | Franchised food outlets, agent networks |
| Aggregator | Pool supply and demand, take margin | Agro-dealer networks, transport cooperatives |
| Usage-based | Pay per use | Metered utilities, pay-as-you-go solar |

## 6. Designing the model (canvas mapping)

Work through nine blocks: key partners; key activities; key resources; value propositions; customer relationships (acquire, retain, grow); channels; customer segments; cost structure (fixed versus variable); revenue streams (pricing model, revenue per transaction). Map Teece's two halves onto them: creation (partners, activities, resources, propositions, segments, channels) and capture (revenue streams, costs, relationships).

## 7. Renewal: sense, seize, reconfigure

Advantage is not static. Describe how management will:
- Sense: monitor customers, technology, competitors and regulation.
- Seize: mobilise resources and adjust the model when an opportunity is confirmed.
- Reconfigure: retire old models and build new ones.
Plan action: list the key assumptions which, if they changed, would require the model to evolve, with trigger indicators, and name who monitors them.

## 9. Checklist

1. Is the value proposition stated with evidence?
2. Is the revenue model explicit and consistent with customer willingness to pay?
3. Which appropriability mechanisms protect capture, and how strong is each?
4. What would a competitor need to copy the model?
5. Which assumptions would force a redesign, and who watches them?
