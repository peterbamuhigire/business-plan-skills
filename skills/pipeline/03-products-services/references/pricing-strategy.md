# Pricing Strategy for the Products and Services and Financial Sections

Use this reference when Section 03 must justify prices, when Section 10 needs price, discount and volume assumptions, and when the plan must show how the team will hold price in negotiation. It combines value-based pricing practice (Hill) with standard margin arithmetic. All amounts below are placeholders: derive real prices from the plan's own cost data, customer research and dated competitor quotes.

## 1. Five growth levers and why price leads

A business can raise profit through only five levers: more leads; higher conversion of leads; higher transaction value; higher purchase frequency; higher price. Price is the only lever with no incremental cost of marketing, selling or fulfilment, so a price rise falls straight to profit, while the other four carry extra cost.

Test it with the plan's own numbers:
- Profit = volume x (price - unit variable cost) - fixed costs.
- Effect of a price rise p on gross profit, with volume unchanged: profit change = p x revenue.
- Effect of a volume rise v: profit change = v x contribution (revenue - variable cost).
- Because contribution is smaller than revenue, a 5% price rise raises gross profit by 5% / gross margin (at a 30% margin, about 16.7%), while a 5% volume rise raises it by 5%. The lower the margin, the stronger the price effect.
Show this comparison in the model. Most firms spend improvement effort on leads and conversion, which are the hardest and costliest levers, and little on price.

## 2. Cost-plus pricing

Formula: price = cost x (1 + mark-up).
Why it persists: simple, feels fair, finance teams default to it, gives a sense of control.
Flaws: ignores customer value; penalises efficiency (lower cost lowers price); rewards waste; confuses mark-up and margin (margin = mark-up / (1 + mark-up), so a 50% mark-up is a 33.3% margin); invites a race to the bottom against lower-cost rivals.
Use it only as a floor (minimum acceptable price) and a profitability check. Set the actual price from value. Record both in the plan: cost-plus floor and value-based price.

## 3. Value-based pricing

Value balance model: place price on one side and perceived value on the other.
| Case | Result |
|---|---|
| Price equals perceived value | Fair deal, sale likely |
| Price below perceived value | Bargain, sale very likely |
| Price above perceived value | No sale |
A sale slightly above perceived value can still produce post-purchase doubt, which erodes loyalty; acceptance is not satisfaction.
When the balance is off, avoid reflex discounting:
1. Explore the gap openly.
2. Add value the customer wants that costs little (training, support, guarantees).
3. Remove elements the customer does not value and lower the price.
4. Communicate hidden value (same-day delivery, expertise, stock availability); customers cannot value what they do not know.

Quality-speed-price triangle: when a customer challenges price, ask which two of good quality, fast service and low price matter most, since all three cannot be had together. This turns a price argument into a value discussion. Scripted example: offer fast tracked delivery at a higher price, cheaper next-day batch delivery, or fast and cheap without tracking, and ask which matters most.

## 4. Tiered packages

Principles: offering choice raises total sales (the question becomes "which one?", not "yes or no"); customers compare tiers with each other rather than with competitors; some always choose the top or bottom tier and the middle tier captures the majority.
Spacing guideline: larger gap between the lowest and middle tier than between the middle and top tier, which nudges buyers to the top. Decoy effect: a very expensive top option makes the next tier look better value.
Construction:
1. List every feature, service element and deliverable currently provided.
2. Sort into three columns: essentials; plus convenience (faster delivery, phone support, extended warranty, onboarding); plus premium or peace of mind (priority delivery, dedicated contact, full guarantee, ongoing consultation).
3. Price each tier by value logic.
4. Check that the gap from middle to top is smaller than from lowest to middle.
Illustration (service subscription): tiers differ by number of devices covered, response time, support channels, on-site visits, backup frequency and review cadence; prices are set from local willingness to pay, not from cost.

## 5. Bundling, cross-selling and concessions

Four ways to raise each sale: more volume of the intended item (with a take-back or refund mechanism for unused portions); complementary products; up-sell to higher specification; simply charge more.
Bundling favours the seller because customers assess total value rather than line items; unbundled (menu) pricing favours the buyer who can cherry-pick. Hybrid: show individual prices, then offer the bundle at a discount to the sum.
Bundle when you want a higher average transaction, items complement each other, you want to obscure high-margin components or blunt competitor comparison. Unbundle when needs differ widely, items have strong standalone value, transparency builds trust, or buyers require line-item quotes (for example public tenders).

Concession ladder: when a customer resists the price, work down this list from least to most costly to you, stopping as soon as the objection is met:
1. Better selling (explain features and benefits).
2. Free training or onboarding.
3. Free service or maintenance agreement.
4. Free complementary products.
5. Cash discount (the costliest, since it comes straight off profit).
Estimate the cost of each concession from own labour and goods costs.

## 6. Discount arithmetic

A discount comes off profit, not revenue. With gross margin m and discount d (both as fractions of price):
- Profit lost = d / m of the original profit (10% discount at 30% margin removes a third of profit).
- Extra volume needed to restore the same gross profit = d / (m - d). Examples: 30% margin, 10% discount needs 50% more volume; 15% discount needs 100% more; 20% discount needs 200% more. If d is at or above m, no volume increase recovers profit.
Build a discount-versus-volume table from the plan's own margins and put it in the financial section. At the thin margins typical of small firms, even modest discounts need volume surges that rarely happen.
Two valid reasons to discount: as a presentation tool when the list price was pre-inflated (the discount is then illusory), and when the price is genuinely wrong according to proper market research, not one customer's complaint.

## 7. Pricing psychology

- Round versus precise prices: round sums feel negotiable; precise prices (for example a computed figure ending in odd digits) signal careful calculation and reduce challenges in business-to-business and high-value sales; charm pricing suits small retail items. Test on your own market.
- Anchoring: present the highest-priced option first so later options look better value.
- Decoy pricing: add an option designed to make the target option look superior (for example a "premium" barely above the target, and a very high enterprise anchor).
- Presentation wording: "the price is" (take it or leave it); "was, now" (a saving); "this week only" (urgency); "your investment" (long-term value, suits professional services); "our price" (implies others may charge more). Avoid "about" or "approximately".
Verify psychological effects with a small local test before relying on them.

## 8. Competitive pricing

Customers cite price as the reason for not buying more often than it is the real reason. Real causes often are: no follow-up on quotes (chase within about three days), failure to explain features and benefits, slow response, weak trust. People buy what a product helps them become (credibility, confidence), not just what it does.
Value-added elements that support a premium: stock availability; technical advice; credit terms or flexible payment; guarantees; training and after-sales support; convenient location or delivery.
Competitor worksheet (update quarterly, for the top products): competitor; their price (dated source); included features; features they lack versus us; our value advantage.

## 9. Directional pricing

Use price differentials to steer customers to the items you want sold (higher margin, greater lifetime value, strategic). Reasons: clear excess stock; up-sell to higher margin by narrowing the gap between low and high options; raise lifetime value by discounting an entry product and earning on consumables or service; shift from a low-margin to a high-margin item by raising the low-margin price.
Worked pattern (food outlet with a small and large item): compute cost, price, profit, margin and sales share for each. If the large size costs only slightly more to make but earns much more profit, then raise the small price slightly, add visible extra value to the large size, or bundle the large size with a high-margin drink. Test each option in contribution per order.
Principle: many products cost almost the same to make in two sizes; steer customers to the version with high marginal revenue and low marginal cost.

## 10. Implementing price changes

Customer grading by profit contribution: A (top fifth): protect, explain changes in person, add value. B (middle 60%): communicate clearly, offer payment flexibility. C (bottom fifth, low profit and high maintenance): raise prices first; accept losses; use their reactions to refine the approach before touching A customers. (The 20/60/20 split is an illustrative starting point.)
Payment options that ease acceptance: instalments over three to six months for larger amounts; mobile-money payment; pay-on-completion for services; small early-payment discount (cheaper than a blanket discount).
Objection script: acknowledge; reframe (show what the price includes); use the triangle; give evidence from a similar client; offer options (lower tier with reduced scope); then stop talking and let the customer respond.
Building sales-team confidence:
| Problem | Remedy |
|---|---|
| Fear of rejection | Weekly role play of pricing conversations |
| Bad-experience bias | Track complaints as a share of all sales (usually small) |
| Defaulting to discounts | Remove on-the-spot discount authority; require next-day approval |
| Round-sum discounting | Ban standard round discounts; require specific figures |
| Cost-focused selling | Remove cost data from sales materials; train on value language |
Discount policy statement (adapt): prices are set to deliver quality, service and genuine value at a fair profit; further discounts would require lower quality or service standards, which the business will not accept.

## 11. Pricing action checklist

Immediate: test a modest across-board price rise (size from elasticity evidence); audit the current method (cost-plus, last year plus inflation, best guess); list the top ten products by profit contribution.
Within four weeks: form a pricing team of sales, finance and operations meeting fortnightly; compile a competitor intelligence file with an owner; create three-tier packages for the top three services and test with trusted customers; list hidden value features and make sure sales staff state them; eliminate round-sum discounts; remove cost data from sales materials.
Within three months: grade customers and phase increases from the lowest grade; write a ranked concession list; train customer-facing staff on the value balance, triangle and objection scripts; add a top "platinum" option well above the top tier; track quote conversion and chase every quote within three days; set up payment options.
Quarterly: review actual discounts against the breakeven table and flag products sold below margin; update competitor data; revisit tier spacing using uptake data; hold pricing workshops with non-competing peers; report average discount, quote conversion, revenue per customer and profit per product.
