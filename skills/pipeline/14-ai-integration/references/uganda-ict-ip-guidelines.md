## 2. Framework facts: laws, institutions, policy (data points)

### 2.1 Who the guidelines apply to
Source: MoICT&NG, Uganda ICT IP Guidelines, 2025. They apply to individuals and entities that develop innovations and applications for the Government of Uganda and the public, either to solve a specific need or after financial support from MoICT&NG, and cover the whole IP value chain (generation, protection, utilisation, enforcement). Coverage: IP in government-commissioned software and IT systems; IP from contractor or supplier relationships with MoICT&NG; innovations built with government funding or grants; technology transfer, commercialisation and open-source strategy for publicly funded ICT.

### 2.2 Legal framework (as listed in the guidelines, verify current status)
1. Trademarks Act, Act 17 of 2010
2. Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Act 2006
3. Trade Secrets Protection Act 2009
4. Industrial Property Act 2014
5. Plant Variety Protection Act 2014
6. Geographical Indications Act 2013
7. Data Protection and Privacy Act 2019
8. Computer Misuse Act 2011 (amended; part 8 of this brief notes a different enactment year in the guidelines, so verify)
9. TRIPS Agreement (1994); Uganda is a WTO member
10. Berne Convention (1886)
11. Paris Convention (1900)
12. Patent Cooperation Treaty (1970)
13. Kampala Protocol (2021): ARIPO voluntary copyright registration and regional database

### 2.3 Institutions and their role for a plan

| Institution | Role |
|---|---|
| Uganda Registration Services Bureau (URSB) | Primary national IP office: registers trademarks, patents, utility models, industrial designs and copyrights; enforces IP rights; keeps the national register |
| Ministry of ICT and National Guidance (MoICT&NG) | IP guidance for the ICT sector, support and incentives, commercialisation strategy, monitoring and evaluation, IP landscape analysis, awareness |
| National Information Technology Authority, Uganda (NITA-U) | IT standards and e-government; relevant in contractor and supplier settings |
| Uganda Communications Commission (UCC) | Telecoms regulator; responsible for the `.ug` domain space |
| Personal Data Protection Office | Registration of data collectors and processors under the 2019 Act |
| Uganda Industrial Research Institute (UIRI) | Industrial IP support |
| Uganda National Council of Science and Technology (UNCST) | Science and innovation policy |
| Uganda Investment Authority (UIA) | Investment facilitation |
| Public Procurement and Disposal of Public Assets Authority (PPDA) | Procurement rules that interact with IP terms in government contracts |
| WIPO | International IP forum; PCT filing |
| ARIPO | Regional IP; Kampala Protocol for copyright |

### 2.4 Policy objectives and context
Four objectives: (1) facilitate high-level ICT innovation, including adaptations from external sources; (2) improve ICT standards compliance; (3) establish shared IP infrastructure covering domestic and transferred technology; (4) promote IP rights and commercialisation so innovators earn marketplace income. Context: National Intellectual Property Policy approved by Cabinet in 2019, supplemented by the Intellectual Property Management Toolkit 2024; government treats IP as a driver of industrialisation under NDP III and Vision 2040 (verify against the current national plan).

## 3. Decision: which protection route for each digital asset

### 3.1 Forms of protection compared (data points, verify terms and fees)

| Form | Scope | Formal registration | Term |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copyright | Documents, books, broadcasts, music, computer programs, databases | Not required; encouraged as proof of authorship | 50 years for computer programs; life plus 50 years for individuals; 50 years after first publication for anonymous works |
| Trademark | Word, name, number, packaging aspect distinguishing a trader's goods or services | Required | 7 years from filing; renewable indefinitely in 10-year periods on payment of the prescribed fee |
| Patent | New, inventive, industrially applicable device, substance, method or process | Required | 20 years; annual maintenance fee |
| Utility model | New technical solution or incremental improvement | Required | 10 years |
| Trade secret | Formulas, processes, software algorithms, business information, customer lists, negotiation tactics | Not required | As long as secrecy is kept |
| Industrial design | 3-D features (shape, surface) or 2-D features (patterns, colours) | Required | 5 years, renewable for two further 5-year terms |

### 3.2 Asset-to-route guide

| Asset | Primary route | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Source code, object code, databases, UI designs (where original), documentation, manuals, specifications, website and digital content | Copyright (automatic) plus recommended registration | Registration gives proof of authorship and date, standing in disputes and access to enforcement; for protection abroad register in the relevant country |
| App name, logo, brand | Trademark | Territorial: apply country by country before commercialising; owner can sue over the same or a similar mark; registration blocks others from exploiting the brand's goodwill |
| Software-adjacent incremental features with short life cycles | Utility model | Quicker and easier than a patent; suited to SMEs |
| Novel hardware or process inventions | Patent | The guidelines do not explicitly exclude software patents but require novelty, inventive step and industrial applicability; algorithms embedded in hardware or process inventions may qualify |
| Algorithms, source code kept private, prototypes, manufacturing processes, pricing models, customer lists, go-to-market strategy | Trade secret | Needs no registration; depends on NDAs and confidentiality controls |
| UI/UX visual design, hardware aesthetics, packaging, graphic symbols | Industrial design | Protects appearance only, not technical features |
| Databases | Copyright (literary work) | Covered by the 2006 Act |
| Creative content (text, images, graphics, photographs, video, social content) | Copyright | Music and performing content may involve the Uganda Performing Rights Society (UPRS) |
| Domain name | Trademark plus UCC `.ug` registration | The guidelines do not cover domain-dispute procedure in depth; verify separately |

### 3.3 Copyright registration checklist (URSB, guideline section 2.4)
1. Submit the application through URSB online or physical channels, with date, location and a contact person; receipt letter is issued by email or hard copy; response target 24 hours or as early as possible.
2. Verification of the asset by experts who know local and international standards; the tool is certified and a certificate issued.
3. The accredited tool is forwarded to URSB for the registration record and maintenance status.
4. Challenge route: a dissatisfied applicant may challenge an assessment or valuation by letter to the registration office or the Permanent Secretary, MoICT&NG; response within 21 working days of receipt.

Plan implications: budget registration and renewal fees as verify inputs; state which assets are registered, which rely on automatic copyright and which are held as trade secrets.

## 4. Decision: open source or proprietary, and who owns commissioned software

### 4.1 Open source (data points)
Free and open-source licences allow use, study and redistribution of object or source code without further permission. Stated benefits for government contractors: no vendor lock-in, re-use by other agencies and private developers, avoidance of the cost of tailor-made solutions. Creative Commons style agreements can let a contractor earn revenue from solutions built through open innovation, signed for 3 to 5 years. An innovation counts as open when knowledge is disclosed voluntarily, collaboration enriches the open base, and disclosure is continuous rather than a one-off by a single person. When commissioning software the contractor should decide whether to release under an open-source licence, and either keep the copyright (obtaining the open-source licence) or require by contract that the developer releases it under a specified licence. Two mechanisms: (1) a licence, exclusive or non-exclusive, for government and private-facing use; (2) a deed of assignment with the vendor.

Deed of assignment: a registered legal document transferring rights and benefits in the IP; no payment is required and a duration is usually stated for the period of ownership.

### 4.2 When to keep the software proprietary
- It is a core business asset.
- It provides a competitive advantage that open-sourcing would lose.
- It is tied to future commercialisation revenue.

### 4.3 Ownership default for commissioned software (guideline section 3.1)
- Default: the contractor (the commissioning party) owns the IP in commissioned software and may use, exploit, disclose and reproduce it.
- Variation A: the contractor keeps copyright and licenses the developer to commercialise; non-exclusive licences to third parties are possible.
- Variation B: the developer owns copyright only under a written agreement; the default in that case is a perpetual, royalty-free, fully paid-up, non-exclusive licence including sub-licensing rights.
- Variation C: the developer assigns copyright to the contractor for a specified period where circumstances require.
- Variation D: joint ownership is possible but generally not recommended because of management complexity.

### 4.4 Licence terms to negotiate

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Exclusive or non-exclusive | Sole right to use versus owner retaining use and the right to license others |
| FRAND | Fair, reasonable, non-discriminatory terms for IP essential to a technical standard |
| Royalties and fees | Percentage of sales, fixed fee or combination |
| Scope of use | Products, services, territories |
| Quality control | Standards to protect the IP's reputation |
| Minimum use | Required level of use to keep the licence |
| Term and termination | Duration and exit conditions |

Plan implications: state the ownership position for every commissioned component in the technology and legal sections; model licence income or cost as an assumption to verify.

## 5. Decision: contracts and procurement IP terms

### 5.1 IP deliverables by procurement type

| Procurement type | IP deliverables |
|---|---|
| Software or software services | Code, user manuals, specifications, data, modifications |
| Hardware | Components, installation manuals, specifications, warranty documentation, maintenance guides |
| Consulting | Reports, strategic plans, implementation guidelines, project documentation |
| Telecommunications | Network diagrams, service contracts, SLA performance reports, user guides |
| Cloud services | Service configurations, migration plans, access documentation, compliance reports, backup and recovery procedures |
| Cybersecurity | Assessments, risk plans, incident response protocols, training materials, staff compliance records |
| Training | Manuals, course materials, assessments, attendance records, certification documents |
| IT support | Support contracts, knowledge bases, incident logs, troubleshooting guides, performance metrics |
| Data services | Data sets, analysis reports, data management plans, privacy compliance documents, access controls |

### 5.2 Contract clause checklist
- Identify IP brought to the project and IP to be delivered; list pre-existing IP in an IP schedule with nature, source and ownership; state ownership of deliverables.
- Require regular reporting on outcomes including new IP.
- Preserve the agency's rights of inspection, exercisable if IP is not reported.
- Build in a review period before any publication or transfer of materials, to identify IP and choose protection.
- Obtain written acknowledgement of NDA obligations from innovators and disclosure-team members (template in the guidelines' Annex B).
- Confidentiality covers trade secrets and confidential information shared during licensing.

Plan implications: procurement and contracts sections should show the IP schedule approach and the contract terms accepted.

## 6. Decision: data protection and cyber obligations

- The Data Protection and Privacy Act 2019 applies to any software that collects, processes or stores personal data.
- Contract requirements for developers: design to the data-protection law; robust cyber-security safeguards; respect for data-subject rights; implementation of the Act's principles.
- Developers are encouraged to register with the Personal Data Protection Office where they obtain, access, process or store personal data, at prototype stage and in deployment (verify whether registration is mandatory for the specific business).
- Definitions used: data (information collected, processed or stored in a filing system); data collector (an individual acting for self or a legal entity to collect data); data controller (determines the purpose and means of processing, alone or jointly or by statutory duty); data processor (a person other than the controller's employee who processes data on the controller's behalf).

Plan implications: compliance section states the role (controller or processor), registration status, and security controls; costs feed the operating budget.

## 7. Procedures: IP asset register, invention disclosure, valuation, international protection

### 7.1 IP asset register checklist (guideline section 2.5)
1. Identity of creators or inventors
2. Description of the IP
3. Registration details
4. Start and end dates of protection
5. Essential details of procurement and funding agreements
6. Ownership and licensing details

### 7.2 Invention disclosure form checklist (Annex A)
Name; employer; company registration number; physical address; contact person; contact email; information-sharing email; telephone; company telephone; website; estimated IP contribution; problem being solved; stage of development; detailed description of the innovation; date of creation; experimental data used; competitive advantage; where it can be applied; further research to add.

### 7.3 IP valuation (guideline section 2.6)
Government agencies value IP they hold and IP from contractors or suppliers and record the valuation. The registration office assesses and values IP through legal assessors, and the value may be modified or maintained by the valuers. Plan implication: treat any IP valuation as a verify input; accounting treatment of intangibles goes to the finance engine.

### 7.4 International protection (data points)
- WIPO member: PCT for multi-country patent filing; Madrid System for international trademarks.
- ARIPO member: Kampala Protocol (2021) for voluntary copyright and related rights registration; regional database and streamlined enforcement.
- Berne Convention: automatic copyright recognition in member countries (168 or more, verify current count).
- TRIPS: minimum standards for protection and enforcement.
- WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996): updates Berne for the digital age.

### 7.5 Four-step IP management cycle for innovators (guideline section 2.4)
1. List your IP; identify what is unique.
2. Confirm the idea is unique and registrable; run a prior-art search.
3. Build an IP portfolio and keep a development log.
4. Contract your IP so agreements and consultancies address ownership.

## 8. Enforcement and risk

### 8.1 Enforcement (guideline section 2.3)
URSB is the primary body for registering and protecting IP. It works with law enforcement, has set up an Enforcement Unit with the Uganda Police against infringement, counterfeiting and piracy, provides dispute-resolution mechanisms, runs public awareness programmes and is a signatory to international IP agreements.

### 8.2 Computer Misuse Act (guideline section 3.6)
Objectives listed: stronger provisions against unauthorised access; a ban on sharing information about minors without parental consent; a ban on hate speech and malicious content; addressing false information; a ten-year bar on public office for persons convicted under the Act. The right to privacy is constitutionally protected. The guidelines give the enactment year as 2001 in one place and 2011 in another; verify against the official text.

### 8.3 IP risks to place on the risk register (guideline section 2.7)
- Contracts that do not address pre-existing IP
- Infringing a third party's IP
- Not protecting your own IP appropriately
- Failing to keep confidential IP confidential

### 8.4 IP risk assessment gauge for software contracts (Figure 6)

| Level | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Low | Few IP disputes; limited third-party IP; little emphasis on IP rights and deliverables; strong emphasis on quality and timeliness |
| Moderate | Possible disputes from moderate third-party IP use; modification of existing products; confidential information; tight competitive market |
| High | Significant dispute risk; possible infringement of existing patents; conflicting IP within the software; significant relevance to the development team |
| Critical | Very high dispute risk; unlinked authorised third-party software; infringement of existing patents; confidential information at risk; unlinked unauthorised software; compromised confidentiality safeguards |

### 8.5 Mitigation controls
- Source code escrow: the developer deposits source code with a third-party agent; the licensee may call for release; the agent acts in a dispute or legal action; this protects continuity of operations.
- Code repository where the contractor commissions development and owns the code; the contractor has access.

## 9. AI and emerging technology

The guidelines contain no dedicated AI IP section; the following provisions apply by analogy. Verify with counsel and current law before relying on them.

- Trade secret protection explicitly covers software source code and algorithms, relevant to AI models, training algorithms and proprietary architectures.
- Utility model (10 years) suits SMEs and ICT features with short life cycles, including AI product features.
- WIPO Copyright Treaty (1996) is relevant to AI-generated digital content questions internationally.
- Innovation, as cited from OECD: implementing a new or significantly improved product, process, marketing method or organisational method. Types: product innovation; process innovation (including improved software in design, development, supply chain and delivery); business-model innovation (changes in how products reach the market).
- Government-funded innovation (section 3.1): unique product innovations, including software and new hardware such as microchips, radioactive technologies and VR gear, should be registered with URSB; IP from government-funded innovation applies immediately in Uganda, but IP rights should not be used only to prevent further innovation by others.

Plan implications: technology section states how models, prompts, training data and outputs are protected (trade secret, copyright, contract) and which data-protection duties apply.

## 10. Commercialisation, revenue share and government funding

- Full ownership (section 5.1): innovators with exclusive IP rights may distribute and sell commercially; government aims to promote local talent, entrepreneurship, jobs and value for money.
- Revenue share (section 5.2): for applications directly supporting core Government functions, a share may apply where agreed, stated as 25 per cent of revenue from the IP, intended to cover recurrent development and commercialisation costs and finance further innovations. Model it as a scenario input, verify per agreement.
- Registration by Government (5.3): where MoICT&NG owns IP it registers with URSB.
- Registration by innovators (5.4): innovations developed before funding must be registered with URSB by the innovator or hub; innovations built from scratch with funding and owned by the innovator are also expected to be registered; for government-funded programmes MoICT&NG runs a 3-year hand-holding process before clearing IP rights, checking technical standards, operational requirements and market readiness.
- Non-disclosure (5.5): releasing invention information before a patent filing, or disclosing information an NDA forbids, can disqualify patentability; disclosure includes abstracts, poster sessions, shelved theses and talks to open audiences. IP disclosures are treated as confidential; NDA acknowledgements are required.
- Abandonment (2.9): IP may be disposed of when it is no longer useful, when a third party can exploit it better, or when government attains ownership under a funding agreement.
- Benefits of IP protection (2.2): strategic IP use; compliance and accountability; resource efficiency; facilitated access; reduced duplication; enhanced protection.

### 10.1 MoICT&NG roles a plan can rely on
1. IP rights protection and guidance
2. Support and incentivisation
3. IP commercialisation strategies (licensing, technology transfer)
4. IP monitoring and evaluation
5. IP landscape analysis and guidance (prior-art searches)
6. IP awareness and capacity building (workshops; IP course units in higher education)

Affordability measures planned: mobilising funds for protection costs, advocating grants or subsidies, and partnering with private firms for discounted rates.

### 10.2 Common mistakes checklist
- Contract without clarifying background IP ownership
- Launching a product that uses someone else's protected technology
- Failing to register or secure key IP
- Letting trade secrets out without NDAs
- Government contracts: no IP identification and disclosure in procurement documents; missing URSB registration within the 3-year hand-holding period; no NDAs for team members with access; abandoning IP without following the abandonment rule.

## 11. Template annexes (what each provides)

- Annex A: Invention Disclosure Form (fields in part 7.2).
- Annex B: NDA template. Recipient keeps information confidential and does not copy or distribute; returns items on request; may not assign rights; Ugandan law governs; disputes go to a Ugandan court of competent jurisdiction.
- Annex C: Sample IP rights assignment. Copyright assignment, patent assignment, trademark ownership, covenant not to sue, damages and specific performance, no waiver, modification and integration, choice of Ugandan law.
- Annex D: Sample revenue and equity sharing agreement between the Trust (government), the Organisation (innovator or contractor) and a technology transfer group if any. Defines cumulative income, direct costs, equity, exploiting party, gross income, innovations, IP and net income; covers a revenue-sharing table tiered by cumulative income (X, Y, Z per cent), equity sharing, accounting and payments, monitoring of Trust-funded IP and exploitation safeguards. Payment terms: interest on late payments; amounts exclusive of Ugandan VAT; financial statements within 3 months of the annual accounting date; annual exploitation reports.
- Annex E: references: ict.go.ug, ursb.go.ug/intellectual-property, wipo.int, aripo.org, National IP Policy 2019, the four Acts above, and the IP Management Toolkit 2024.
