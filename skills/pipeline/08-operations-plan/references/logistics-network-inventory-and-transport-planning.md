# Logistics Network, Inventory, and Transport Planning

Logistics design links market promises to financial reality. The right design is the lowest total-cost way to meet the chosen service promise, not the cheapest network. This reference paraphrases planning rules from ASCM (2022) *Certified in Logistics, Transportation and Distribution (CLTD) Learning System*, Modules 2 (network design), 6 (inventory), and 8 (transportation). Trade rules, duties, and Incoterms editions change; verify the current Incoterms edition and national customs rules before stating them.

## 1. Network design

1. Map the current or proposed network: suppliers, ports, borders, factories, warehouses, cross-docks, branches, customers, carriers, trade lanes, and information flows.
2. Test each service promise against total cost: transport, warehousing, safety stock, handling, order frequency, delivery timing, administration.
3. Explain why the network takes its form: customer geography, freight volume, supplier location, product mix, infrastructure, trade routes, regulation, expansion timing.
4. Name the triggers for redesign: changes in customer expectations, product lines, supplier base, freight volume, trade policy, demographics, or service standards.
5. Include reverse logistics where returns, repairs, recalls, reusable packaging, recycling, or disposal affect cost or trust.
6. Treat cross-border movement as an operating system: Incoterms, customs brokers, duties, documents, inspections, and the point of risk transfer shape cash flow and accountability.
7. Sequence network changes: which facility, supplier, system, route, carrier, or control changes first, and how progress is measured.

## 2. Inventory policy

- Treat inventory as a service-level and working-capital decision: it protects the customer promise but ties up cash, space, handling labour, insurance, and management time.
- Separate cycle stock, safety stock, work in process, finished goods, packaging, spares, returns, and postponement stock where they behave differently.
- State the review method: perpetual review for high-value or fast-moving SKUs; periodic review for low-value, stable items when counts and controls are reliable.
- Apply ABC analysis to focus attention on high-value, high-risk, high-volume, or service-critical SKUs.
- Show how lead time, demand variation, transport delay, supplier reliability, minimum order quantities, duties, and route risk set reorder points and safety stock.
- Track stock-outs, backorders, excess, slow stock, expiry, shrinkage, and goods in transit as operating facts.
- Connect inventory policy to the sales forecast and cash-flow forecast; a sales plan needing stock the business cannot finance, store, move, or replenish is not credible.

## 3. Transport model

- Identify the model: own fleet, hired carriers, courier or parcel, freight forwarder, third-party logistics (3PL), fourth-party logistics (4PL), lead logistics provider, or a hybrid.
- Choose mode, carrier, route, schedule, consolidation, load factor, and delivery frequency against the service promise and margin model.
- **Own fleet:** vehicle capacity, maintenance, driver availability, utilisation, backhaul potential, fuel, insurance, licensing, compliance, dispatch control, replacement capital.
- **Outsourced:** carrier selection, rate basis, service-level agreement, claims process, tracking capability, exception handling, fallback carriers, carrier concentration.
- **Documents and controls:** freight bills, bills of lading, waybills, packing lists, commercial invoices, certificates, customs declarations, proof of delivery.
- **Exception workflows:** delay, damage, failed delivery, refusal, short or over shipment, wrong item, loss, expediting.

## 4. Section links

- **Operations:** physical flow and information flow for fulfilment, replenishment, facilities, quality, returns, and service during growth.
- **Finance:** working capital, transport, storage, handling labour, packaging, loss and damage provision, insurance, duties, broker fees, systems, capex. Inventory and freight must reconcile with revenue timing and gross margin.
- **Risk:** supplier failure, port or border delay, route disruption, carrier failure, fleet downtime, stock-outs, excess stock, documentation errors, hazardous goods, customs disputes, reverse-logistics obligations.
- **Growth:** what changes at two, five, and ten times current volume — replenishment frequency, warehouse space, slotting, carrier renegotiation, cross-docking, regional stocking points, route redesign, warehouse or transport management systems, outsourcing.

## 5. Minimum measures for physical-goods businesses

Fill rate and stock-out rate; backorder rate and average backorder age; inventory days and turnover; safety-stock coverage for critical SKUs; slow-moving and obsolete stock value; dock-to-stock time and order cycle time; pick accuracy and inventory accuracy; on-time pickup, on-time delivery, and on-time-in-full (OTIF); transport cost per order, unit, kilometre, or tonne-kilometre; load utilisation and route adherence; freight claims, damage rate, return rate; carrier or fleet scorecard.

## 6. Red flags

- Revenue growth assumes faster delivery with no change to warehouse, route, carrier, or inventory.
- Inventory appears as one line with no policy for safety stock, reorder points, expiry, spares, packaging, or slow stock.
- Transport cost is a flat percentage with no mode, distance, frequency, load, or carrier logic.
- Cross-border trade is planned without Incoterms, broker, duties, documents, clearance lead time, or risk-transfer assumptions.
- An own-fleet plan ignores downtime, utilisation, backhaul, drivers, compliance, or replacement capital.
- An outsourced plan ignores service levels, claims, tracking, escalation, and carrier concentration.
