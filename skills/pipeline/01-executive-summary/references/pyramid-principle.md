# Answer-First Structure for Executive Summaries and Plan Sections

Use this reference when writing or reviewing the executive summary and any plan section that must present thinking clearly to a funder, lender or partner. It applies the Pyramid Principle (Minto) and its SCQA introduction, with plan-specific procedures and prompts written for this engine.

## 1. The pyramid

Readers understand ideas best from the top down. Put the summarising idea before the ideas it summarises.
```
Governing thought (the answer to the reader's question)
        |  raises a question
Key line: 3 to 5 points that answer it
        |
Supporting points under each key-line point
```
Three rules, all of which must hold; if one fails, the thinking (not just the writing) is flawed:
1. Ideas at any level summarise the ideas grouped beneath them.
2. Ideas in a group are the same kind of idea (all reasons, all steps, all risks).
3. Ideas in a group follow a logical order.

Vertical relationship: each statement raises a question in the reader's mind (why, how, which?); answer it at the level below, then let the next question arise, until the reader has no more. This presents information exactly when it is needed.
Horizontal relationship: ideas on one level form either a deductive or an inductive argument, never a mixture.

## 2. SCQA introduction

Open with situation, complication, question, answer.
| Element | Function |
|---|---|
| Situation | A statement the reader will accept as true, anchored in time and place; shared ground |
| Complication | What happened in the situation to create tension, a problem or a need for action |
| Question | The question the reader would logically ask |
| Answer | The governing thought; the top of the pyramid |
Bare form: S, we have or do X; C, something changed, failed or was discovered; Q, so what should we do, is this right, how do we do it; A, the recommendation.
Order variations: standard (S, C, A); direct (A first, for readers who want the bottom line); concerned (C first, when the problem is urgent); question first (Q, S, C, A, for directive memos or when the question is already in mind).
Common document patterns:
| Document | S | C | Q |
|---|---|---|---|
| Directive | We want X | We need you to do Y | How do you do Y? |
| Funding request | We have a problem | We have a solution costing an amount | Should you approve? |
| Progress review | We agreed to do X | We have done it | What did we find? |
| Proposal | The client has a problem | They asked us to investigate | What should they do? |
Introductions remind rather than inform: include only what the reader already knows or would readily accept, with no exhibits or new data. Put historical chronology in the introduction, not in the body.

## 3. Deductive and inductive grouping

- Deductive: a chain to a "therefore" conclusion; each point comments on the subject or predicate of the previous one. Limit: about four points and no more than two chained "therefore" steps.
- Inductive: a set of the same kind of thing (describable by one plural noun), from which you draw an inference about what their sameness implies. The summary states that inference, not just the category label.
Choose:
| Situation | Form |
|---|---|
| Key line of the main structure | Inductive |
| Paragraph level | Deductive (short enough to follow) |
| The recommendation will surprise the reader | Deductive (argument before conclusion) |
| The reader must understand a method before its steps | Deductive |
| Anything else | Inductive: recommendation first, then support |
Present action before argument unless the argument is what the reader cares about.
Telling them apart: if the second point comments on the first, it is deductive; if it adds another member of the same class, it is inductive; if neither, the points do not belong together.

## 4. MECE grouping and logical order

Mutually exclusive: no overlap. Collectively exhaustive: nothing relevant missing. Practical test: can one plural noun describe every item (steps, reasons, problems, recommendations, risks)? If not, the grouping is invalid.
Three logical orders; a grouping without one is invalid:
| Order | Source | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Time | Steps in a process | Implementation phases, process description |
| Structural | Parts of a whole (geography, organisation, value chain) | Market segmentation, organisational or industry analysis |
| Degree | Ranking by a shared characteristic | Risks by likelihood, markets by size, problems by severity |

## 5. The "so what" test

After each statement ask "so what; why tell me this?" If you cannot answer, the point does not belong, belongs in the introduction as situation, or lacks its summary.
Avoid intellectually blank assertions that name the kind of idea without its meaning:
| Blank | Replacement |
|---|---|
| "The business has three problems." | State what they mean together: "The business cannot scale because no authority has been delegated." |
| "We have five recommendations." | State the net effect: "Simplifying methods and raising wages cuts unit cost by an estimated share." |
| "Four variables must be managed." | State why: "Together they decide whether the allocation process produces the right output." |
Finding the true summary: for action ideas, state the effect of carrying them out; for situation ideas (reasons, problems), state what their similarity implies. Both require finishing the thinking, not just listing.

## 6. Common structural errors

| Error | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Blank assertion | Summary says "three reasons" | Ask what the points mean together and state it |
| False grouping | No single plural noun; overlap; misfits | Identify the real classification; sort into sub-groups; find the order |
| Mixed reasoning levels | Causes and effects listed as equals (low productivity, high overtime, uncompetitive prices) | Recognise the cause chain; state the governing conclusion |
| Deductive key line | Summary reads "here is what is wrong, why, what to do", forcing the reader to hold three chains | Rotate 90 degrees: recommendations on the key line, findings beneath each |
| News instead of thinking | True facts with nothing in common | Remove them; a point is justified only if it explains or defends a higher point |
| Chronology in the body | Narrative of events without analysis | Move events to situation and complication; body holds ideas in logical order |
| Vague action wording | "Strengthen regional effectiveness" | Word each step as its end product ("assign planning responsibility to the regions") |

## 6a. Building a pyramid

Top-down (when the subject and reader question are known):
1. Name the subject.
2. Identify the reader's question.
3. State the answer (governing thought).
4. Find the first non-controversial situation statement.
5. Develop the complication.
6. Recheck that the complication leads to the question and the answer addresses it.
7. Find the key line: what question does the answer raise, and which plural noun labels the response (reasons, steps, changes)?
8. Support each key-line point by the same question-and-answer process, level by level.
Bottom-up (when data exist but structure does not): list all points; work out relationships (cause chains, groupings); draw a conclusion from each group; think through the introduction and work back to confirm the top point.
30-second test: can a reader get the governing thought and key-line points from the opening paragraph? If not, restructure.

## 7. Problem-definition framework

For complex documents map SCQA to a problem definition:
- Situation: starting point (a stable process the reader recognises, anchored in time and place) plus a disturbing event (external: competitor, technology, policy; or internal: new system, expansion, lagging performance revealed).
- R1: the undesired result (declining sales, eroding margins, inability to serve the market).
- R2: the desired result, specific and measurable (for example "cut time to market by a third"); without R2 you cannot choose among solutions.
- Question: how to get from R1 to R2, varying with the reader's position.
- Answer: the recommendation.
Reader positions and questions:
| # | Reader position | Question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does not know how to reach R2 | What should we do? |
| 2 | Has a proposed solution, unsure | Should we do it? |
| 3 | Knows the solution, not how to implement | How do we do it? |
| 4 | Tried a solution that failed | What now? |
| 5 | Has two alternatives | Which should we choose? |
| 6 | Has decided and wants a plan | How will you do it? |
| 7 | Wants to understand what happened | Why did it happen? |

## 8. Application by plan section

| Section | Application |
|---|---|
| 01 Executive Summary | The pyramid in compressed form: governing thought plus key line; SCQA opening paragraph; 30-second test |
| 02 Company Overview | S, what we do and where; C, the gap or opportunity; Q, why us and why now; A, our proposition |
| 03 Products and Services | Key line of three or four reasons the offer wins; MECE groupings beneath (features, benefits, evidence) |
| 04 Market Analysis | Structural order for segments (MECE); degree order to rank opportunities |
| 05 Target Market | Inductive grouping by a shared characteristic; inference about the unmet need |
| 06 Competitive Analysis | R1, customer pain with existing solutions; R2, what they want; the offer closes the gap |
| 07 Marketing and Sales | Time-ordered actions; summary states the effect, not "five components" |
| 08 Operations | Time-ordered steps; separate strategic objectives from operational steps; word each step as an end product |
| 09 Management Team | Inductive grouping (for example operators with hands-on sector experience); summary states what the combination enables |
| 10 Financial Projections | Deductive chain acceptable at paragraph level (assumptions, calculations, projections); key line stays inductive (three financial milestones) |
| 11 Funding Request | S, we have a validated business; C, we need capital to scale; Q, should you fund us; key line, use of funds, expected return, exit or repayment path |
| 12 Risk Analysis | Degree order by likelihood x impact; each risk as R1, R2, mitigation |
| 13 Implementation | Time order; phases, milestones, tasks; each phase summarised by its end product |
| 15 Appendices | No pyramid; evidence for claims in the body |

Local SCQA template:
```
S = [Sector] in [country] is [size, growth, state]. [Company] was established in [year] to [purpose].
C = Despite [opportunity], [gap or problem remains]. (or) Having reached [milestone], the business faces [constraint].
Q = How can [company] achieve [objective] in [timeframe]? (or) Is this the right time or approach for [decision]?
A = [Company] can achieve [result] through [three actions or reasons], requiring [investment or approval].
```

Sources consulted: Minto, The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving, Minto International, 2010 (also known as The Pyramid Principle).
