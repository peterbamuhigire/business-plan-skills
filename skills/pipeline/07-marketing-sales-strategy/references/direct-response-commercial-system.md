# Commercial system test for Section 07

This is the engine's test that a plan has a working commercial system rather than a list of channels. It asks five design questions in order and ends with a release checklist. The direct-response tradition informs it, in particular Kennedy, D. S. and Walsh-Phillips, K. (2018) *Magnetic Marketing*, ForbesBooks, which argues for fixing market and message before media and for designing attraction, conversion and retention as one system. The questions, rules and examples are this engine's. For the one-to-one sales conversation use `direct-response-selling-playbook.md`; for long written copy use `long-form-sales-letter-build.md`.

## Q1 — Is the order of decisions right?

Decide **who** (a named, high-probability segment; "everyone" is not a market), then **what to say** (a benefit-led message that asks for a response), and only then **where** (channels that reach that segment at an acceptable cost). Reject a draft that chooses channels first.

## Q2 — Does every stage of the customer's path exist and have a number?

| Stage | What it must achieve | Minimum design |
|---|---|---|
| Draw in | The right prospects identify themselves | Named segment, a first response offer, channel, assumed cost per enquiry |
| Win | Enquirers become buyers through trust-building steps | Follow-up sequence, proof and authority material, a consultation or trial step, assumed conversion per step |
| Keep and multiply | Customers buy again and refer | Contact rhythm after sale, updates, repeat-purchase offer, referral moment |

A plan fails if any stage is missing, unowned or unmeasured.

## Q3 — Is the first response offer designed?

The first offer lets a prospect raise a hand and give permission for follow-up: a guide, audit, checklist, short video, self-assessment or consultation. Rules: it leads straight to a commercial next step; it shows enough expertise to build trust while leaving a clear reason to buy; it collects contact details and consent lawfully (see the marketing orchestrator's location and scope calibration for Uganda and Kenya data-protection rules). Local formats may beat email: WhatsApp opt-in, SMS short code, radio call-in, community meetings, agent networks. Test before assuming.

## Q4 — Does every communication ask for a measurable response?

- One specific requested action, stated plainly.
- A genuine reason to act now.
- Response measured by source; marketing judged on response, not appearance.
- In high-trust services, authority shown before persuasion.
- Follow-up planned for longer than feels natural, with the number and spacing of contacts stated.

Design the win stage as a chain of small, measurable agreements (request the offer → open the first message → watch the explanation → read a case study → book a consultation → accept a proposal), each with an assumed rate and its evidence. Trust-heavy sectors (agribusiness advice, private health, education, legal, construction, financial and B2B services) need this sequencing most. "Post on social media and hope" is not a conversion plan.

## Q5 — Are retention and referral treated as profit sources?

- Existing customers hear from the business on a fixed rhythm (newsletter, update, service reminder).
- Referrals are requested straight after a clearly good outcome, not at random.
- At least one repeat-purchase mechanism and one referral mechanism exist where the model allows.
- Where acquisition budgets are tight and switching depends on relationships, show retention economics before new-customer growth.

**Illustrative example.** A Mbale agronomy advisory firm: segment = coffee cooperatives with over 300 members; message = "cut rejected cherries at the washing station"; first offer = a free pre-harvest quality checklist via the cooperative's WhatsApp group; win chain = checklist → field-day invitation → paid farm audit → season contract; keep and multiply = monthly agronomy tips and a referral request after the first improved grading report.

## Release checklist

- [ ] Segment and first response offer fixed before channels.
- [ ] Draw-in, win and keep-and-multiply stages each have an owner, a measure and a cost.
- [ ] First response offer linked to the next commercial step.
- [ ] Follow-up rhythm stated (number of contacts, spacing, channel).
- [ ] At least one referral and one repeat-purchase mechanism.
- [ ] No awareness-only activity without a measurable response path.
- [ ] Step conversion assumptions feed the Section 10 revenue model.

## Evidence to record in the plan

Segment definition; message; channel rationale; first response offer; sequence map with step assumptions and sources; retention rhythm; referral trigger; owner per stage.
