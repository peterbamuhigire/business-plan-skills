# In-House Advertising and Copywriting: Production Reference

Use this reference when a small business must produce its own advertising without an agency, and when a plan needs to show how promotional pieces will be written, produced and tested. It covers the mechanics of individual pieces: message structure, headlines, print, radio, direct response, trade shows, lists, directory listings and web copy. Medium-specific figures (audience reach, station and event details, response rates) are volatile: verify them with dated sources before quoting.

## 1. The advertising promise

Treat every advertisement as a binding promise defined by three elements before any creative work:

| Element | Question | Example form |
|---|---|---|
| What is promised | Which specific benefit? | "You will harvest more water per acre" (state the measured amount you can prove) |
| To whom | Which clearly identified prospect? | A named farmer segment in named districts |
| Under what conditions | Terms, proof, call to action | Free assessment this month; one contact method |

Common breaches: a vague or feature-led promise, an audience defined as "everyone", and conditions that are missing (no call to action) or too complex (several steps, unclear expiry).

## 2. Five-step message sequence (attention, interest, desire, conviction, action)

An extension of AIDA (Hahn) with conviction added. Every piece should carry the prospect through all five in order.

| Step | Function | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Stop the prospect (headline, visual, opening line) | Generic headline; company name leads |
| Interest | Frame a relevant problem or desire | Opens about the company, not the reader |
| Desire | Show the product solves the problem | Features without benefits |
| Conviction | Prove the claim (testimonials, credentials, guarantees, data) | Step skipped; claims unproven |
| Action | One specific, low-friction next step | Vague close, no deadline, buried number |

Diagnosis:

| Symptom | Likely failed step | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Read but no enquiries | Action | Specific, time-bound action, one contact method |
| "Sounds good" but no purchase | Conviction | Add testimonial, guarantee or verifiable figure |
| Ignored | Attention | Rewrite the headline with a benefit, number or "you" |
| Interest fades | Interest | Open with the reader's problem |
| Features understood, relevance not | Desire | Apply benefit-feature-proof |

## 3. Benefit, feature, proof

For each attribute build three layers: the feature (what it is), the benefit (what it does for the customer) and the proof (why to believe it: evidence, test, credential, guarantee). Lead with the benefit; keep features in supporting copy as credibility. Example structure: a solar water pump (feature: solar-powered pump with holding tank) gives clean water daily even in outages (benefit), supported by a count of installations, a call-back record and a regulator certificate (proof); use the business's own real proof. In a plan, describe each product as benefit, then feature, then proof rather than a specification sheet (products section), and reuse it in the marketing section.

## 4. Headline and body-copy rules

Headlines:
- Use numbers ("seven ways to cut your fuel bill"): specificity creates expectation. Test claimed uplifts locally rather than quoting generic percentages.
- Use "you" and "your".
- Make a specific, testable promise ("...or money back") rather than a vague boast.
- News-style openings ("Introducing", "At last", "Now") tend to outperform clever headlines in direct response.

Body copy:
- Write the way your best salesperson talks; read every sentence aloud and cut what you would not say.
- Short paragraphs, one idea each; white space is a design element.
- "So what?" test after every feature claim; answer it in the next sentence or cut the claim.
- Subheads every four to six paragraphs; headline, subheads, captions and the postscript should tell the whole story alone.
- Emphasise sparingly (one key phrase per section).

## 5. Print advertisement checklist

1. Headline states the benefit, not the category or company name.
2. Subhead adds a secondary reason to read on.
3. Body in second person.
4. One central visual that reinforces the headline, not a collage.
5. Logo and address small, at the bottom.
6. Deliberate white space; not cluttered.
7. Specific call to action: one number, address or web address with hours.
8. Tested at thumbnail size: is the headline still legible?

## 6. Radio script checklist

Length guide: about 75 words for a 30-second spot, about 150 for 60 seconds; always confirm by reading aloud with a stopwatch.
1. Business name at least three times (start, middle, end).
2. One central message; no benefit lists.
3. Local references (streets, cultural touchpoints).
4. Call to action at least twice.
5. Read aloud and timed.
6. Avoid humour unless the category suits it.
7. Close with the number or web address, slow and repeated.

Media selection: rank stations by signal area, language, audience profile and rate. Vernacular and regional stations usually cost less than capital-city English-language stations and reach up-country districts; premium English stations suit professional and small-business audiences. Radio is typically the most cost-efficient broadcast medium for small budgets in Uganda (reach much higher than television); verify current reach, station lists and rate cards from the media owners or an audience survey before use.

## 7. Direct-response package

| Component | Function | Digital or local equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Outer envelope | Earn the open | SMS preview line (first 160 characters); message subject line |
| Sales letter | Primary selling document, follows the five steps | Broadcast message body; bulk SMS body |
| Brochure | Visual proof (features, photos, testimonials) | Image or PDF attachment; catalogue link |
| Order form or reply card | Action device; restate the whole offer | Reply keyword, link, mobile-money till number |
| Lift note | Second-authority endorsement (optional) | Forwarded message from a known referrer; testimonial screenshot |

Letter-first rule: when improving a campaign, change the letter before anything else.

Letter checklist:
1. Opens with the reader's problem or desire.
2. Personalised salutation.
3. Core offer or teaser in the first paragraph.
4. Guarantee stated prominently.
5. Bold and underline used sparingly.
6. Reply device self-contained.
7. Postscript used as a second headline, restating the strongest benefit or the deadline (never new information); never omit it.
8. Postscript set differently (or handwritten style) to signal it was added personally.

Physical post is slow and unreliable for many small businesses locally; substitute messaging broadcasts and bulk SMS with the same five-component architecture. Check consent and data-protection rules for any list.

## 8. Trade show checklist

1. Company name readable from about ten metres, in the largest type.
2. One tagline, not a product list.
3. Staff briefed on three opening questions to qualify each visitor before pitching.
4. Lead capture tested before opening (card bowl, tablet form or capture sheet).
5. Literature rack apart from the conversation zone.
6. A show-exclusive offer clearly posted.
7. Pre-show invitations to existing clients with the booth number and a reason to visit (at least two weeks ahead).
8. Post-show follow-up prepared in advance (scripts, sequences, call schedule) and executed within 48 hours.
Event types to research locally: national trade fairs for buyers, importers and distributors; city festivals for consumer footfall; agribusiness forums; export-hub expos for export-ready firms. Verify dates, organisers and audience each year.

## 9. Testing hierarchy

Test in this order; higher levels produce larger swings in response (Hahn's ordering; the ranges are illustrative and must be measured locally).

| Level | Variable | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | List (who is contacted) | Wrong audience cannot be fixed by better copy |
| 2 | Offer (promise, price, terms) | A stronger offer beats better creative |
| 3 | Headline or envelope copy | The first seconds decide whether anything is read |
| 4 | Format (self-mailer versus package, SMS versus messaging app) | Affects attention and perceived value |
| 5 | Body copy and creative | Matters least until levels 1 to 4 are optimised |

One-variable rule: change one thing per test, otherwise the cause of any improvement is unknown.

Formulas:
- Break-even response rate = cost per piece sent / revenue per response. Example pattern: a message costing 50 currency units with 80,000 revenue per conversion breaks even at about 0.06 percent, which is roughly 625 responses per million sent (replace with your own costs).
- Target cost per order should not exceed roughly 30 to 40 percent of customer lifetime value (planning rule); for a customer worth 500,000 over a lifetime, an acceptable acquisition cost is about 150,000 to 200,000.

Cheap testing: run small paid-social tests (a small budget) on the offer and headline before committing to print, radio or bulk SMS.

## 10. Lists

- House list (own customers and enquirers) typically responds several times better than rented lists and is the most valuable asset: maintain, segment and protect it. A mobile-number list built from mobile-money transactions, loyalty programmes or messaging opt-ins fits this; respect consent.
- Rented lists respond poorly by comparison and vary in quality: always test.
- RFM segmentation: recency (most predictive), frequency, monetary value. Send the strongest offers and personalised messages to high-scoring customers; do not send lapsed and active customers the same message.
- Hotline names: when renting, request prospects who responded to a similar offer in the last 90 days.
- Test at least about 1,000 names before roll-out; at a 1 percent response that yields about ten responses, enough to estimate break-even.

## 11. Directory and business-profile listings

The rules for printed directory display ads apply to online business profiles and directories.

| Rule | Online equivalent |
|---|---|
| Lead with a person (owner or staff photo) | Profile photo shows a person, not only a logo |
| Unique selling proposition in the first line | Keyword-rich, benefit-led first line of the description |
| Show hours prominently | Verified hours, updated for public holidays |
| Colour discipline (second colour only for border and headline) | Consistent cover and post images; avoid clutter |
| Test and revise | Update posts, photos and offers; answer every review |

Ten mistakes to avoid (attributed to Barry Maher in Hahn): company name as headline; features instead of benefits; no call to action; overcrowding; a logo as the dominant visual; a phone number with no address or hours; assuming prospects know what you do; jargon; not matching the category customers search under; renewing the same ad for years without measurement.

Local equivalent: business profile plus Facebook Page plus messaging business account plus an online directory listing, following the same rules.

## 12. Website copy principles

- Benefit and primary call to action above the fold.
- Navigation labelled by what the visitor gets ("Save on fuel costs", "Book a free assessment") rather than company structure.
- One primary call to action per page.
- Email or messaging capture in exchange for real value (price guide, calculator, free consultation), not a bare newsletter prompt.

## 13. Local adaptation summary

| Principle | Local application |
|---|---|
| House list over rented | Build the mobile list; segment by RFM |
| Radio for small budgets | Apply the script checklist to regional and city stations |
| Trade show three-phase system | Invite, capture, follow up within 48 hours |
| Direct-mail package | Messaging broadcast and SMS with the five components |
| Directory equivalent | Business profile, Page, directory |
| Test before roll-out | Micro-test on paid social first |
| Guarantee as differentiator | Explicit money-back or replacement guarantees are rare in many local categories; state one prominently and support it with conviction |

## 14. Where to use it in the plan

- Marketing and sales section: define campaign objectives with the promise, structure channel copy with the five steps, allocate test budget by the hierarchy.
- Products and services section: benefit, feature, proof descriptions.
- Target market section: RFM segmentation for direct marketing.
- Operations section: trade show and direct-response checklists as campaign procedures.
- Executive summary: apply the headline rules and "so what?" test; the summary is a sales letter for the whole plan.

Sources consulted: Hahn, F. and Mangun, K., Do It Yourself Advertising and Promotion, 3rd ed. (2003); Maher, B. on directory advertising as cited there. Local media and event details must be re-verified.
