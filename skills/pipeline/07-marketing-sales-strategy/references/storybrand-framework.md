# Customer-as-Hero Messaging: The StoryBrand (SB7) Method Applied to Business Plans

Use this reference when writing messaging, positioning statements, the one-line pitch, website copy, email sequences, founder biographies or any passage that must invite the customer into a story. The method is the StoryBrand seven-part story structure (SB7) by Donald Miller; it is named and applied here with attribution, and the wording, examples and plan mapping are the engine's own.

## 1. Why messaging fails

1. It does not show how the offer helps the customer survive and thrive (save money or time, belong, gain status, grow income, find meaning).
2. It makes the customer work hard: cluttered, self-focused or complex messages are ignored because attention is scarce. Rule: if you confuse, you lose.

Noise (long backstories, internal goals, irrelevant detail) is the enemy. Story is a sense-making structure: a clear hero with a want, a problem, a guide, a plan, a call to action, stakes and a resolution. Build one message script for the overall brand and sub-scripts for each product line or division.

## 2. The seven parts (customer-story script)

### Part 1: a character who wants something
The customer, not the brand, is the hero and is looking for a guide, not another hero. Name one clear desire tied to survival or thriving (saving money, saving time, belonging, status, income growth, generosity or meaning, self-realisation). Naming the desire opens a gap between where they are and where they could be; that gap creates attention. One primary desire per script; further desires belong in sub-scripts. Script line: "The customer wants ____."

### Part 2: who has a problem (three levels plus a villain)
Companies sell solutions to external problems, but customers buy solutions to internal ones.
- Villain: the root cause of the conflict; a single, relatable, real source, not a symptom or manufactured fear (debt, distraction, bureaucracy, poor information).
- External problem: the tangible obstacle (leaking pipe, poor website, market gap).
- Internal problem: the frustration, doubt or inadequacy it causes ("I feel embarrassed by my lawn", "I fear I will not retire comfortably"). This is the real reason customers buy; speaking to it creates deeper connection.
- Philosophical problem: why it matters morally ("it is wrong that small businesses cannot get a fair loan"), which turns customers into advocates.

### Part 3: who meets a guide
The brand is the guide who has already solved the hero's problem. A guide shows two things:
- Empathy: "We understand how it feels to...", "Nobody should have to...".
- Authority, without bragging: a few focused testimonials (about three, not twenty), a quantified track record, small award marks, recognised client logos (business-to-business).
Customers silently ask "Can I trust this person?" (empathy) and "Can I respect this person?" (authority); answer both quickly.

### Part 4: who gives them a plan
Trust needs a clear path that lowers perceived risk. Two kinds:
- Process plan: three to four steps, before purchase (book a call, we assess, you receive a proposal) or after (download, enter details, start using).
- Agreement plan: commitments that ease fear (price match on verified quotes, refund if unhappy, no high-pressure tactics, honest disclosure).

### Part 5: and calls them to action
People act when challenged. Use a confident direct call ("Buy now", "Get a quote", "Enrol today"), placed prominently and repeated; and a transitional call for those not ready (free guide, short demonstration, free report) that captures contact details and keeps them in the story. Place the direct button top right and above the fold in a contrasting colour, with the transitional offer next to it.

### Part 6: that helps them avoid failure
Stories need stakes. Loss aversion (Kahneman and Tversky) means losses motivate more than equivalent gains. Define what the customer risks by not acting (health, money, relationships, career, opportunity), over one year and five. Use a few well-chosen consequences; too many create despair or distrust.
Local illustrations: crops fail and debt accumulates because planting had no plan; a competitor opens next door while you hesitate; a standards regulator closes a food business and its reputation does not recover; lose the bank's confidence before entering the room.

### Part 7: and ends in success
Tell customers plainly how life improves, with specific, positive images. Three resolutions to choose from: winning power and position (status, access, loyalty tiers); union that makes the hero whole (community, belonging, peace of mind); self-realisation (becoming a better version of oneself).
Before-and-after grid (Deiss): what they have, how they feel, what an average day looks like, what their status is, each before and after.

### Foundation: identity transformation
Customers want to become a more competent, accepted version of themselves. Define the arc "from [current identity] to [aspirational identity]" (for example passive owner to hero; confused to competent; uncertain entrepreneur to fundable owner; job-seeker to valued professional; subsistence farmer to commercial farmer). A brand that addresses external, internal and philosophical problems and celebrates the customer's transformation creates passionate advocates.

## 3. The one-liner

Formula: "We help [character] who [problem] [achieve result or transformation]."
It should tell anyone who hears it who you serve, what problem you solve and what transformation you offer, replacing a rambling pitch.
Examples: "We help small-business owners who are frustrated with confusing marketing get a clear message that grows revenue." "We help entrepreneurs who cannot get a bank loan create a bankable business plan that opens doors to finance." "We help farmers losing money to poor yields adopt a simple system that raises harvests."

## 4. Putting the script to work

### Website (five essentials)
1. An offer above the fold that passes the grunt test: a distracted visitor understands it in about five seconds.
2. Obvious calls to action (direct plus transitional, repeated further down).
3. Images of success: customers transformed, not the building or a team selfie.
4. A short breakdown of revenue streams so visitors self-select.
5. Very few words: cut about half, use bullets and images.

### Lead generator
A transitional offer trading genuine value for an email address: guide, checklist, video series, tool, quiz. Title patterns: "How to [outcome] without [feared cost]" or a numbered tip list. It must be immediately useful, building reciprocity and positioning the brand as guide.
Local ideas: reasons bank applications are rejected and how to avoid each; how to price products so you never sell at a loss; a business registration checklist for start-ups (verify current requirements).

### Automated email campaign
- Nurture email (repeat about three times before an offer): a problem the customer faces, a plan to solve it, life once solved; include a postscript (highly read).
- Offer email (about every fourth): the problem, your product as the solution, the transformation, a direct call to action with a time limit if possible.
- Open rates of around 20 percent are a common benchmark (verify); even deleted emails keep the brand present. Consistency beats perfection.

### Transformation testimonials
Collect stories that follow the hero arc, not generic praise. Five questions: what problem did you face before? what did you find when you started? what specific result did you get? what would you tell someone hesitating? how has your life or business changed? Edit into a case study or video.

## 5. Internal culture use

An organisation without a unifying story suffers a "narrative void": staff disengage because they do not understand the story they are in. Onboard with the customer's story, frame roles as parts in the customer's journey, share customer transformation stories, and use the script as a filter for internal communication and decisions.

## 6. Local application

Customers in many markets feel overlooked or talked down to by institutions; positioning the customer as hero and the brand as guide breaks that pattern.

| Sector | External problem | Internal problem | Villain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agricultural inputs | Low yields, crop failure | Fear of losing everything | Erratic weather plus lack of knowledge |
| Financial services | No credit history | Shame of poverty | Inaccessible or predatory lenders |
| Education and skills | No certificate | "I am not smart enough" | A system that ignores informal talent |
| Food processing | Compliance gap | Fear of closure | Unclear regulatory requirements |
| Business planning | No bankable plan | "Banks never help people like me" | Complexity and financial jargon |

Aspirational identity examples: struggling motorcycle-taxi rider to asset-owning transport entrepreneur; informal food seller to certified food brand owner; first-generation business owner to job creator in the community.

## 7. Mapping to plan sections

| Plan section | Use |
|---|---|
| Executive summary | One-liner; hero-framed opening instead of a company-centred one |
| Company overview | Guide positioning; mission as the guide's empathy statement |
| Products and services | Products as the tools the hero needs to win, not feature lists |
| Target market | Define the hero (want, aspirational identity) and the villain and three problems |
| Competitive analysis | Rivals who cast themselves as heroes are vulnerable; position the business as guide |
| Marketing and sales | Script, one-liner, website essentials, lead generator, email sequence, testimonials |
| Management team | Founders as guides: biographies built around what they conquered that the customer now faces |
| Pitch deck | One-liner slide 1; hero problem slide 2; guide credentials slide 3; plan slide 4; success vision slide 5 |

## 8. Script template

- Character: [business] helps ______ who want ______.
- Problem: villain ______; external ______; internal ______; philosophical ______.
- Guide: empathy "We understand ______"; authority ______.
- Plan: step 1 ______; step 2 ______; step 3 ______.
- Call to action: direct ______; transitional ______.
- Failure: if they do not act, ______.
- Success: their life will look like ______.
- Transformation: from ______ to ______.
- One-liner: we help [character] who [problem] [achieve result].

Sources consulted: Miller, D., Building a StoryBrand (HarperCollins, 2017); Kahneman and Tversky on loss aversion; Cuddy on first impressions; Deiss (before-and-after grid). Examples and mapping are the engine's own.
