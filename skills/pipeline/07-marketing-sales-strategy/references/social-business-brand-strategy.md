# Brand Content Engine and Social Business Governance

Use this reference when a plan must show how the business will act like a small media company: define a single brand story, run a content programme across owned, earned and paid media, govern who may publish what, and organise the people who do it. It draws on the "brand as media company" and social business strategy ideas of Brito (2013), adapted for small businesses.

Audience statistics on device use, research behaviour and trust change quickly; verify any figure with a dated source before quoting it.

## 1. Premise

Every brand, from a neighbourhood business to a multinational, competes for attention in a content surplus. Customers use several devices in sequence, filter hard, form their own opinions and influence each other more than advertising does. An authentic, consistent content engine outperforms push marketing.

## 2. Understanding the social customer

- Multi-screen behaviour: customers move between phone, tablet, computer and television to finish tasks. Design mobile-first; check every asset on a small screen.
- Attention filtering: customers engage only with content relevant to them at that moment.
- Relevance: understand the customer's interest graph (what they care about beyond the product). Content should add value to their existing interests and conversations, not just promote the product (a jeweller may discuss fashion, not cereal).
- Customers as influencers: most research online before buying, negative information changes decisions, favourable reviews confirm them, and quick problem resolution raises recommendation. In markets where word of mouth spreads through messaging groups, community pages and local radio, good and bad experiences both travel fast: invest in service first, content second.
- Repetition: people typically need several exposures (commonly cited as three to five) before they believe a message; a single advert or post is not enough.
- Converged media: paid (display, search, promoted posts), earned (influencers, advocates, press, word of mouth) and owned (website, page, messaging channel, video channel, blog) must tell the same story. Paid amplifies earned and drives people to owned; the goal is to convert audiences into owned subscribers you can reach directly and repeatedly at no per-message cost.

## 3. Social business strategy

Definition (paraphrased from Brito): a documented plan that changes how the organisation thinks and links internal and external social activity, creating collaborative connections and shared value for customers, partners and employees.

### Common internal failures
1. Inappropriate employee posts.
2. Unclear roles (who owns the accounts?).
3. Inconsistent measurement.
4. Slow, outdated crisis communication.
5. Launching in new markets without resources.
6. Disjointed content and community management (many dormant accounts).
7. Tool-selection paralysis.
8. No content governance.

### People, process, platforms
1. People: leadership behaviour first; team structure; employee empowerment and advocacy training.
2. Process: social media policy; content supply chain (ideate, create, approve, distribute, optimise); governance and workflows; crisis escalation.
3. Platforms: choose technology after people and process are defined; match capability to real requirements (social CRM, publishing, monitoring, advocacy tools).

### Value model

| Actor | Gives | Receives |
|---|---|---|
| Customers | Revenue, feedback, advocacy | Problem resolution, engagement, product improvement |
| Brand | Engagement, problem-solving, relevant content | Loyalty, advocacy, community insight |
| Employees | Innovation, process improvement, brand journalism | Empowerment, training, shared knowledge |

Social brand versus social business: a social brand focuses on external communications, is owned by marketing and measured by likes and reach; a social business builds internal and external collaboration, is owned by the whole organisation, empowers staff to communicate and is measured by behaviour change.

## 4. Brand story and content narrative

Principle: consistency of one story across every touchpoint separates great brand storytelling from mediocre content. A brand can build its story around a lifestyle or ideal the customer values rather than the product itself, provided every post, event and placement contributes to it.

### Inputs to gather before writing any post
1. Brand messaging and product benefits (foundation, not the story).
2. Non-business issues the brand cares about (causes, community values).
3. Media and influencer perceptions.
4. Community perceptions (what customers say unprompted).
5. Fan and customer interests.
6. Historical and current content performance.
7. Consumer search behaviour leading to the brand.
8. Customer service pain points (unresolved complaints reveal deep needs).

### Output: the hero narrative
The one story the brand tells consistently across channels. It is not a tagline, attribute list or mission statement; it shows how the business relates to customers' lives. Simplification test: say it in one sentence to a stranger.

Framework: brand core (what you sell), brand attributes (quality, reliability, affordability, innovation), customer interest graph, and the intersection of the last two is the content narrative; every piece is a chapter of it.

### Development process
1. Gather inputs.
2. Identify what the brand cares about that customers also care about.
3. Draft the hero narrative as a one-paragraph story.
4. Test the one-sentence version.
5. Set editorial principles (topics in and out of scope).
6. Map content types to channels.
7. Build the calendar: planned, real-time and reactive content.

## 5. Channel strategy

Principles: map the narrative to specific channels; use content tiers (hero: large productions; hub: regular updates; help: FAQs and tips); tailor tone and format per channel; diversify types (video, photography, infographics, text, audio).
Visual content usually outperforms text-only content; where mobile data is expensive, short fast-loading clips beat long videos, and visuals cross literacy barriers.
Long-form builds authority, search visibility and credibility with lenders and partners; short-form drives engagement and reach. Long-form titles should be specific and promise a benefit (weak: "our new product launch"; strong: a named audience achieving a stated saving with your solution).

| Channel | Content | Frequency (starting assumption) | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page on a social network | Stories, community, product updates | Three to five times weekly | Awareness, trust |
| Business messaging | Offers, service updates, support | As needed | Conversion, retention |
| Visual platform | Product, behind the scenes, testimonials | Three to four times weekly | Image, discovery |
| Video channel | How-to, demonstrations, customer stories | Two to four times monthly | Education, credibility |
| Website or blog | Articles, case studies, FAQs | One to two times weekly | Search, credibility |
| Community radio | Awareness, promotions | Campaign-based | Mass and rural reach |

Real-time marketing: connect to what is happening now (holidays, local events, harvests, school terms, weather, major sports finals, fee-payment periods, graduation season). A small "creative newsroom" responds within hours.

## 6. Governance

Without governance: unapproved posts, conflicting messages, proliferating unmanaged accounts, no crisis route.

Components: written social media policy; approval workflow; password and access management; approval before creating any new account; crisis escalation matrix.

Proactive workflow: ideation, creation, review, approval, scheduling, publishing, monitoring, reporting.

| Level | Situation | Response | Timeframe (starting assumption) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Negative comment on own page | Community manager replies | Within 2 hours |
| 2 | Complaint going viral | Marketing lead notified, response drafted | Within 4 hours |
| 3 | Media coverage | Chief executive or communications team | Within 24 hours |
| 4 | Legal or regulatory matter | Legal team; no public comment until advised | As directed |

## 7. Organising the content team

Models: by channel (specialist per platform; large organisations), by brand or product (different audiences and messages), by region (local content within a shared brand framework; useful for branches in several towns).

Roles: content strategist (narrative, channels, editorial calendar), content creator, community manager (replies, moderation, escalation), analytics manager, brand journalist (finds stories inside the business). In a small business one person often fills all five; a documented strategy tells them what to create, for whom and when.

Employee advocacy: employees are usually more credible external voices than advertising. Empower them, with guidelines, to share company content, tell customer success stories and capture behind-the-scenes material.

## 8. Checklists

Brand story audit: narrative stated in one sentence? visible consistently across website, social and print? content reflects customer interests, not only sales? top five topics customers discuss known? same story told by owner, employee and customer?

Social media readiness: policy shared with staff; approval process documented; password system; calendar planned at least four weeks ahead; response-time standard (for example all comments within 24 hours); crisis matrix communicated; analytics reviewed monthly.

## 9. Where to use it in the plan

- Marketing section: structure by paid, earned and owned media; state the repetition principle; treat word of mouth and community referrals as earned media.
- Brand and identity section: define the hero narrative before writing copy; use hero, hub and help tiers for the content plan.
- Digital strategy: channel matrix adapted to the business; mobile-first, messaging and the leading social network as primary channels.
- Organisation section: content roles, governance and crisis matrix.

Sources consulted: Brito, M., Your Brand, The Next Media Company (Pearson, 2013); Edelman Trust Barometer and Google research as cited there (verify current editions).
