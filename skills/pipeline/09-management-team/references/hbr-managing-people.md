# People Management Practice: Evidence-Based Methods for the Management Section

Use this reference when Section 09 must describe how managers will lead, motivate, give feedback, build inclusive and civil teams, run dispersed or overcommitted teams, and set people policy for a small East African business. It is organised by management task. Research findings are summarised in general terms; where a figure matters to the plan, re-verify it against the primary source.

## 1. Developing managers

Framework: effective management rests on three linked disciplines (Hill and Lineback).
| Discipline | Meaning | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Manage yourself | Trust rests on competence and character; know your values and blind spots | Trying to be liked or feared instead of trusted |
| Manage your network | Interdependence and scarce resources create politics; build relationships across the organisation and upward before they are needed | Refusing politics, leaving the team without resources |
| Manage your team | A group committed to a shared purpose is a stronger lever than one-to-one management | Managing only individuals, with no shared norms or accountability |

Daily development routine: prepare (choose a development goal for the day's interactions), do (use a deliberate new approach), review (replay key conversations, note progress). Self-rate on the three disciplines; a uniform middle score signals comfortable mediocrity. Choose two or three development goals; take ownership because organisations rarely provide enough support; call team meetings and set team purpose and norms.
Local point: proactive relationships with regulators, suppliers, customers and community leaders are professional social capital, built before a licensing or supply problem arises. Neither authority-only nor paternalistic styles build the trust that sustains performance.

## 2. Permitting constructive dissent and individuality

Why people conform: social pressure, status-quo bias and confirmation bias (Gino). Conformity costs the organisation ideas and early warning.
Practices:
1. During onboarding and reviews, ask each person what makes them distinctive and how they will use it.
2. Match roles to signature strengths and let role definitions bend to the person.
3. Encourage "why" and "what if" questions once people master current practice.
4. Vary tasks and rotate people to broaden perspective; hire for diversity of thinking.
5. Design dissent into decisions: ask what evidence suggests the path is wrong; appoint an advocate, a sceptic and a neutral evaluator for major proposals.
6. Write job descriptions in terms of outcomes, not fixed methods.
Diagnostic: if roughly a third or more of staff say they regularly suppress their views, engagement and innovation are being lost. In deferential cultures, the manager must model vulnerability and never punish a "wrong" idea. Design roles around demonstrated skill, not certificates alone, given many vocationally skilled staff.

## 3. Feedback method

Position (Buckingham and Goodall): conventional rating-based and critical feedback rests on three weak assumptions.
1. Others see your weaknesses better than you do. Ratings of another person reflect the rater's own standards and biases (the idiosyncratic rater effect); averaging biased ratings adds noise. People are reliable reporters only of their own experience.
2. Learning means filling gaps. People learn most by building on existing strengths; criticism triggers a defensive state that narrows learning, while strengths-based conversation opens it.
3. Excellence is universal and definable in advance. Excellence is individual, and excellence and failure often share surface traits, so a deviation from a competency model shows adequacy at best.

Practices for a management team:
- Catch and name what works in the moment ("that, yes, that") so the person can recognise and repeat it; keep a highlight-reel mindset.
- Report your own experience, not a verdict: "here is how it came across for me", "here is where I lost the thread", "when I do not hear from you I worry we are not aligned", "here is what I would do".
- Treat correction as remediation and use it when needed, but make catching strengths the priority interrupt.
- For a person with a problem, explore the present (three things working), the past (what worked in a similar case), and the future (what they already know they need to do). Ask "what" questions, not "why".
- Replace rating-scale multi-rater instruments with narrative descriptions of observed behaviour where possible.
Local adaptation: in high power-distance cultures the recipient cannot push back, so first-person, specific, private conversation suits oral, relational communication; public criticism damages dignity and triggers withdrawal or resignation; one demoralising episode ripples through a small team. Give team-level recognition publicly and individual strength-spotting privately to avoid peer resentment. Describe this approach, not rating-form appraisals, in the plan.

## 4. Motivation through progress

Progress Principle (Amabile and Kramer): the strongest daily driver of motivation is progress in meaningful work, and managers usually underrate it.
| Event | Effect | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Progress | Strongly positive | Milestone reached, useful input |
| Setback | Strongly negative | Work abandoned, scope changed |
| Catalyst | Positive | Clear goals, autonomy, resources |
| Inhibitor | Negative | Micromanagement, ambiguity |
| Nourisher | Positive | Recognition, respect, encouragement |
| Toxin | Negative | Disrespect, dismissed contributions |
Negative events weigh more than equal positive ones, so removing inhibitors and toxins matters as much as adding catalysts.
Ways managers strip meaning: dismissing ideas, removing ownership through abrupt reassignment, signalling work will not be used, and failing to communicate priority changes.
Manager practice: create a calm response to crises one event at a time; stay aware of daily progress without judging; target support to recent events; act as a resource, not a supervisor; share information openly.
End-of-day checklist: what progress and setbacks occurred; which catalysts, inhibitors, nourishers and toxins were present; what one thing will best help progress tomorrow.
Local application: manufacture visible small wins in slow periods; communicate supplier, permit and customer changes immediately and explain what they mean for the team's work; link tasks to community and family purpose; recognise that a public humiliation poisons every team member's motivation in a small team.

## 5. Civility

Rudeness reduces effort, quality, creativity, helpfulness, commitment and customer treatment, and causes departures; witnesses are affected as well as targets (Porath and Pearson). Treat it as a business cost.
Personal practice: model courtesy, give full attention in meetings, keep promises, seek honest feedback, avoid gossip.
Organisational practice: screen for civility in hiring and treat serious incivility as a disqualifier; teach it with role play and video; let teams define their own norms; reward civil behaviour; penalise uncivil behaviour consistently rather than moving the person; hold post-departure interviews after several months for candid accounts; include a civility dimension in reviews; use a simple in-meeting signal to call out disrespect; adopt simple greeting norms.
Local forms: ethnic or regional dismissiveness and language exclusion; gender-based interruption and credit-taking; authority-based public reprimand; phone use in meetings; condescension by formally educated managers toward skilled artisans. The plan should state concrete norms (no phones in team meetings, no public reprimands, respectful disagreement welcome), not a values slogan.

## 6. Gender, ethnicity and structural conditions

Evidence summary (Tinsley and Ely): differences between men and women in negotiation, confidence and risk appetite are small in organisational settings; observed outcome gaps are better explained by conditions: exclusion from informal networks, harsher scrutiny of errors, and less day-to-day coaching. The explanatory biases are attributing behaviour to personality, hearing stereotypes repeatedly, and noticing only confirming cases.
Four-step approach: question the narrative; generate a plausible structural explanation; change the conditions and assess; share learning and hold managers accountable.
Manager audit:
1. Is informal coaching, stretch work and network access evenly distributed across gender, ethnicity and region?
2. Before judging someone as lacking confidence, have I made speaking up harder (airtime, reaction to imperfect ideas, no advance preparation)?
3. Does the appraisal system disadvantage people who took leave or are less embedded in informal networks?
4. Counter stereotypes with data in hiring and promotion.
Local points: women managers may hold formal but not informal authority; absence is interpreted differently for women; women founders face extra scepticism, so foreground track record and systems in their management profile; ethnic bias operates the same way. State in the plan how the firm manages diversity: hiring decisions, mentoring and network access, and a route to surface bias.

## 7. HR policy design: treat staff as responsible adults

Principles (McCord):
1. Hire, reward and tolerate only responsible adults; replace detailed policy with clear expectations. Most policy exists to control a small minority at the cost of the majority. Ask of each policy: does it punish the many for the few?
2. Tell the truth about performance frequently and informally. Elaborate improvement plans built to document failure are dishonest; prefer an honest conversation, fair severance and a clean exit.
3. Managers own building a strong team: picture the team's required output in six months, derive the skills needed, then test the current team against it; do not fight with the team you have.
4. Leaders own culture through modelled behaviour. Failure modes: espoused values contradicted by behaviour; staff who do not understand how the business makes money; split subcultures (salaried versus hourly) needing different management.
5. Think as a businessperson first: ask what is good for the company and how staff can understand what high performance means. Test: ask any employee what they should be doing now to earn their bonus.
Adaptation: unlimited leave and large severance suit few small firms; the transferable ideas are honest fit conversations, simple expectations with autonomy, culture shown in daily behaviour and dignified exits (people leave and return as customers, suppliers or referrers in small business communities). Identify three to five non-negotiable expectations and trust judgement elsewhere.

## 8. Taking over an existing team

Three phases (Watkins): assess, reshape, accelerate.
- Assess: hold one-to-ones using a consistent question set (biggest challenges; what would your priorities be); observe who volunteers, deflects or blames; judge each person on competence, judgement, energy, focus and trustworthiness (weight trustworthiness most); vary criteria by situation (turnaround needs ready people; sustaining success allows development).
- Reshape: composition (remove toxic members, redeploy mismatches, develop high potential); alignment (what, why, how, who, stressing why); operating model (separate strategic, operational and learning meetings); integration (ground rules, model behaviour, intervene early, diagnose and repair trust).
- Accelerate: secure early wins, plan the first 90 days with achievable goals, document accountabilities, secure stakeholders, publicise results.
Team trust elements: confidence in capability; open information; commitments honoured; psychological safety to disagree; confidentiality; unity behind decisions.
Local considerations: inherited loyalties and family or board ties; tenure-based deference; low psychological safety after past blame; information hoarding between departments. Use face-saving transitions for underperformers and rebuild safety consistently over a long period.

## 9. Overcommitted teams (multiteaming)

Costs: weak cohesion, context switching, burnout, reduced motivation from thin credit, and risk transmission between teams (Mortensen and Gardner).
Team leader actions: launch teams deliberately with mutual working-style and constraint disclosure (a proper kickoff improves performance materially; repeat it when about 15% of membership changes); map skills explicitly; surface competing priorities and crunch periods early; design learning moments and pair juniors with seniors; frame small-share contributions in terms of what people personally value.
Organisational actions: map interdependence of people across projects; deliberately move knowledge between isolated teams; keep a small pool of flexible "firefighters" so crises do not drain committed teams.
Checks: count each person's concurrent teams (more than about five active projects signals burnout and quality risk); hold a midpoint review on every project; ask whether a struggling project's issue is overcommitment; protect the highest-stakes projects during crunch.
Local point: multi-role staff (a supervisor also inspecting, purchasing and dispatching; an accountant also doing payroll, tax and grant reporting) are the norm; role-clarity and capacity conversations give permission to say "I am at capacity". Borrow work plans, role matrices and midpoint reviews from donor-project practice.

## 10. Dispersed and cross-border teams

Social distance is the core problem. Diagnose with the SPLIT audit (Neeley): structure, process, language, identity, technology.
| Source | Leader action |
|---|---|
| Structure (site size and location create perceived power gaps) | State who we are, what we do and that you are available; contact remote members often |
| Process (no informal contact) | Add five minutes of unstructured time before calls, routine feedback on collaboration, explicit invitation to disagree |
| Language (fluency imbalance) | Fluent speakers slow down and avoid idiom; less fluent speakers ask for clarification and stay engaged; leader balances participation |
| Identity (cultural meanings of silence, interruption, directness) | Leader as learner; ask, do not assume; debrief cross-cultural interactions |
| Technology | Match channel to purpose: video for persuasion and emotion, written for information, phone for follow-up; model the preferred channel |
Post the three meeting rules visibly; reach out personally to minority-site members.
Local application: headquarters and partner-country teams can create perceived subordination; multilingual teams need deliberate language management; relationship-first norms clash with transactional partners; voice notes suit less formally educated staff; use video for sensitive performance talks, messaging for daily coordination and formal email for commitments.

## 11. Designing a workplace people believe in

Six characteristics to evaluate (Goffee and Jones): let people be themselves; unleash information flow (candid, complete, timely; beware cultures where bad news never reaches the top); magnify strengths through investment in people; stand for more than shareholder value; make daily tasks coherent and meaningful; keep rules people believe in and can see the purpose of, with authority based on credibility, not title.
Actions: run an anonymous 30-item style diagnostic and treat the gap between intent and experience as the development agenda; explain or remove any rule whose purpose nobody knows; make one visible investment in staff development without direct business payoff; create a protected bad-news channel; express what the business stands for in language its lowest-paid employee would believe.
Local application: community, religious and family purpose drives commitment beyond pay; too many unenforced formal rules coexist with unclear informal norms, so simplify; a bad-news channel is a survival mechanism where subordinates shield bosses from stress; visible development investment offsets the pay gap to non-profit and public employers.

## 12. Competing with non-profit and public employers on pay

Levers: credible, specific link between commercial work and community benefit; frequent progress moments; visible investment in training, mentoring, stretch assignments and funded education; authentic permission to contribute and disagree. State the levers and their costs in the compensation section.

## 13. Adaptations from large-firm practice to an East African SME

| Large-firm practice | SME adaptation |
|---|---|
| Rating-scale multi-rater tools | Specific first-person observations of moments that worked |
| Annual reviews | Frequent check-ins using progress and setback framing and present-past-future coaching |
| Policy for every scenario | Three to five non-negotiable expectations; judgement elsewhere |
| Team-building retreats | Meeting rituals, short informal time before calls, explicit norms, consistent modelling |
| Individual incentives | Add team recognition; ensure each person knows exactly how to earn any bonus |
| Upward feedback surveys | Informal bad-news channels; receive bad news without punishment; ask for dissenting views |
| Competency ladders | Deploy roles to observed strengths; invest in credentials |
| Improvement plans and paperwork on exit | Honest early conversation, dignified exit, protected reputation |
| Diversity programmes | Audit who receives coaching, stretch work and introductions; correct imbalances |

## 14. Cross-cutting cultural notes for Uganda and East Africa

- High power distance: feedback and safety require sustained signals from authority; one visible punishment of dissent erases months of trust.
- Collectivism: team-level goals and recognition often motivate better than individual ratings; private, specific individual feedback avoids resentment; dismissal is a community event affecting family, clan and referral networks.
- Ethnic diversity: apply the structural-conditions audit; avoid tribally homogeneous hiring that produces conformity; state operational diversity practices.
- Mixed education levels: design roles for skill; extend progress recognition to production staff; guard against condescension.
