# Management Practice Toolkit for the Management Section

Use this reference when Section 09 must show how the management team will lead, coach, align and change the organisation, and when the plan needs a credible management-development or team-health programme. It is organised by the management job to be done. Each entry gives the purpose, the procedure, the evidence it produces for the plan, and a Uganda and East Africa adaptation.

## 1. Choosing what to include in the plan

| Situation in the business | Tools to draw on | Plan evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Founder-led, 5 to 20 staff, no formal management system | Sections 2, 3, 4 (trust, delegation, performance conversations), 6 (meetings) | Written authority matrix, appraisal calendar, meeting rules |
| Growing to 20 to 100 staff, first-time managers | Sections 2, 3, 5, 7 | Manager onboarding plan, team health check, hiring and training budget |
| Multi-site or regional operations | Section 8 | Communication norms, site-visit schedule, delegation limits |
| Restructuring, new system, new owner | Section 9 | Change plan, readiness scores, communication schedule |
| Recurrent turnover or low morale | Sections 3.4, 3.5, 9.4 | Retention actions, survey baseline, action log |

State only the tools the business will actually use, with owner, frequency and cost. A plan that lists every tool signals a training catalogue, not a management system.

## 2. Diagnosing and developing leaders

### 2.1 Interpersonal skills self-assessment
- Purpose: identify which of eight interpersonal skill areas (understanding people, clear expression, assertiveness, feedback exchange, influence, conflict resolution, teamwork, adaptability) limits a manager.
- Procedure: score each area on a five-point scale; rank the lowest two or three; recall one recent situation where each cost the team something; agree one practice action per weak area; repeat in three to six months.
- Localisation: assertiveness and feedback exchange are culturally suppressed and therefore usually the priority; reading social cues and face-saving are strengths to preserve.

### 2.2 Multi-rater (360-degree) leadership review
- Purpose: compare self-ratings with ratings by direct reports, peers and the line manager across about twenty competencies grouped into strategy, influence, results, people management and integrity.
- Procedure: manager self-rates; three to six raters rate independently; average by competency; flag gaps larger than one scale point; discuss the two or three largest gaps in a coaching conversation; write an individual action plan; follow up quarterly.
- Use: annual review, promotion decisions, leadership transition.
- Localisation: hierarchy suppresses upward honesty. Guarantee anonymity structurally, use an external facilitator for the first cycle, and frame the exercise as helping the manager serve the team.

### 2.3 Style diagnostics
- Empowerment patterns: classify a manager's dominant pattern as enabling (energising, celebrating) or limiting (over-rescuing, perfectionist, withdrawn, resistant to authority, critical, rule-bound, or analysis-bound). Name the pattern without blame, agree three to five behaviour changes, and share with a peer or coach for accountability. Rule-bound and perfectionist patterns are common where management style has been shaped by civil-service norms; over-rescuing is common where a family founder cannot delegate.
- Autocratic-to-participative continuum: rate about sixteen leadership characteristics, locate the default style, then choose deliberately by situation (directive for new or low-skill staff, participative for experienced staff). Situational flexing is a culturally acceptable route away from purely autocratic habits.
- Star-performance check: assess self-expression, support for others' expression, and orientation to growth; improve the lowest of the three each quarter. Support for others' initiative is the usual gap; present empowerment as a sign of strength.

### 2.4 First 90 days for a new manager
Complete this checklist on appointment and audit it annually:
1. Read the organisation's plan and understand the targets that matter.
2. Confirm role, procedures, reporting line and decision rights.
3. Establish in writing what may be approved without escalation.
4. Map internal and external stakeholders and build relationships with them.
5. Confirm how personal and team performance will be measured.
6. Find out what rewards and recognition exist.
7. Inventory budget, equipment, support staff and training funds.
8. Secure a mentor, peer network or coach.
9. Schedule feedback on own performance.
10. Set aside monthly time for learning.
Authority, stakeholders and performance criteria are the items most often skipped in small businesses; write them down.

### 2.5 Delegation
- Diagnose delegation failure through a discussion case: a manager who holds every decision blocks learning and growth. Ask what the non-delegation costs in money, morale and growth capacity, then have each participant commit to one delegation action within a week.
- Plan-level output: a delegation table (decision, current owner, target owner, limit, review date).

## 3. Communication, trust, coaching and performance

### 3.1 Everyday communication discipline
Prepare purpose, agenda and desired outcome before important conversations; confirm the other person is ready; listen for content and feeling and reflect it back; pause after key points; open with broad questions and narrow to closed ones; separate a quick reply from a considered response; give information in layers (headline, detail, context); close a failing conversation and return later. Where informal, context-driven communication is the norm, signal structure aloud ("I want to cover three things").

### 3.2 Building and repairing trust
Trust behaviours to build into manager standards: keep promises and deadlines; protect confidences; act consistently with stated values; share information widely and early; give and receive feedback; admit errors; apply authority fairly and visibly so across ethnic and family lines; answer requests and ideas promptly; credit others; show personal interest in staff. Use the list as a repair plan after redundancies, broken promises or public criticism. Relationship-first cultures reward personal interest and recognition most.

### 3.3 From boss to coach
Ten shifts: observe before intervening; listen more than speak; ask options-questions instead of issuing answers; give frequent specific feedback; set clear standards; notice what is right; link work to purpose; build on strengths; inspect results, not activity; model coachability. Start with structured questions ("name three options you see") because staff trained to await instructions find open prompts unfamiliar.

### 3.4 Coaching quality inventory
Assess coaching across professional development commitment, performance development commitment, assessment and planning, face-to-face feedback, attending and listening, and follow-up. The manager completes a self version; each report completes a parallel version (anonymously if necessary). Compare by category; large gaps where the manager rates high and staff rate low are the priority. Listening is typically the weakest category.

### 3.5 Retention and motivation
- Retention self-check: score behaviours that keep or lose talented staff (a 26-item style checklist with alert, caution and strong bands); convert each zero-scored behaviour into one action. Low-cost retention levers are flexible hours, public recognition and training.
- Motivation: identify each report's primary motivator from six drivers (enjoyment, feeling important, personal benefit, clarity, success, positive outlook) or a longer list of about fourteen (for example achievement, autonomy, challenge, clarity, camaraderie, harmony, order, stability, variety). Design one action per person per month. Feeling important and clarity are the most neglected; naming a contribution in a staff meeting costs nothing.
- Zero-cost morale actions: handwritten thanks, greeting staff by name, personal business cards, a rotating recognition token, a weekly appreciation box, visible senior-manager participation, a photo board of team life.

### 3.6 Performance management design
- Phase 1, preparation: analyse each job; set standards before the review period; explain purpose and process; set goals with, not merely for, the employee.
- Phase 2, appraisal meeting: open with development as the purpose; describe behaviour, not personality; use documented examples; hear the employee first; agree a development plan with milestones.
- Phase 3, follow-up: check in at 30, 60 and 90 days; monitor against the plan; offer help before failure; state consequences of sustained performance and non-performance.
- Precondition: current job descriptions. Where none exist, writing them is the first task.
- Difficult-relationship reflection: for a person you find difficult, compare style, gender, age and culture; write one hypothesis for each difference and one changed behaviour for the next contact.

## 4. Team diagnosis and alignment

### 4.1 Team health check (goals, roles, relationships, procedures)
Use a 40-item instrument (ten items per dimension, scored 0 to 5, maximum 200) completed independently by all members including the leader. Average by dimension, focus on the lowest, ask what specifically drives the score, and agree two or three actions within 30 days. A related quarterly "functioning team" diagnostic covers shared purpose, mutual accountability and productive processes. Present results as the team's score, and have the leader own a share of any low score. Interpersonal warmth often scores high while candid confrontation of performance scores low; procedures are usually weakest in small firms.

### 4.2 Team alignment session
Collect written answers from each member before the session to sixteen questions covering goals, values, decision rights, communication norms, escalation and success measures. Discuss only items where answers diverge, do not force early consensus, issue a written alignment summary, and revisit it at the next meeting. Prior written input equalises junior voices.

### 4.3 Role clarification
Have each member map their own responsibilities visually, place maps side by side, list overlaps, gaps and unclear boundaries, negotiate and record agreed roles, and re-check after 30 days. Three designs fit established teams with overlaps, new teams starting from scratch, and cross-functional project teams. Visual mapping suits mixed-literacy teams.

### 4.4 Team-building exercises
Use for specific problems, not as filler: a critical-incident interview in triads (interviewer, interviewee, observer, rotate twice) to surface strengths; a multi-round bidding game to expose cooperation and defection between departments; a scarcity-trading puzzle to expose trade-offs; a planning game with investor, department heads and customer roles to open strategic planning. In each, the debrief carries the learning: ask what drove trust, defection and trade-offs and how that mirrors real relationships. Use local scenarios; invented problems disengage participants.

### 4.5 Perspective and listening exercises
- Ambiguous-image exercise: show a picture that supports two readings to demonstrate that both perceptions can be valid; useful where disagreement is read as disrespect and requires no literacy.
- Active-listening practice (30 minutes): trainer models, pairs practise reflecting content and emotion, then a reverse role play of a real difficult conversation. Normalise role play by modelling first and using work scenarios.
- Time-management simulation: plan a day, absorb interruptions, replan as a team, debrief on urgent versus important and on protected time. Telephone, messaging and walk-in interruptions make protected time a system need, not a personal one.

## 5. Meetings, decisions and problem solving

### 5.1 Meeting roles
Assign facilitator, timekeeper, minute taker, records keeper, flip-chart scribe, researcher, energiser, planner, liaison and process observer. Rotate them so junior staff gain visibility. Timekeeper and process observer roles produce the greatest gain where meetings overrun and decisions go unrecorded.

### 5.2 Managing meeting process
Principles: distinguish symptom from cause (talking too much is a symptom of unequal airtime); invite the group to self-correct first; protect face; keep adult-to-adult relationships. Responses: go-around for dominant speakers; written response cards or a warm direct invitation for silent members; pause and absorb side conversations into the main discussion; acknowledge feeling and redirect to the issue for antagonism, and never allow personal attack. Non-participation by juniors, women and minority-group members is the commonest fault; use written input as a structural fix.

### 5.3 Participation and consensus methods
Participation: open discussion, written response cards, go-around, subgroups before plenary, speaker-nominates-next, fishbowl. Idea generation: fast (timed, volume) and slow (reflective, depth) brainstorming. Consensus: multivoting, polling on an A to E support scale to surface blocks, and explicit commitment checks. Teach multivoting in five minutes to prevent silence being read as agreement.

### 5.4 Nominal group technique (seven steps)
Silent individual idea writing (10 minutes); round-robin listing without discussion (20); clarification discussion (30); private first ranking (10); discussion of divergent votes (20); final private ranking (10); confirmation of priorities, owners and next steps (20). This is the strongest equalising method for hierarchical groups. Allow 90 minutes or more.

### 5.5 Problem-solving relay
Four groups rotate through identify, generate, prioritise and implement stages on one real problem (30 to 45 minutes each), each inheriting the previous group's chart work; all review the final plan together. Allow three to three and a half hours. Distributes ownership across functions.

### 5.6 Discussion quality
- Advocacy versus inquiry: participants hold a marker for each contribution type and review the ratio after 20 to 30 minutes. It gives juniors a legitimate structure for questions.
- Stalemate breaking: name the conflict; each side states underlying concerns; each proposes a solution addressing both sides' concerns; agree concrete commitments. Trigger when a topic has looped more than twice or a decision has been deferred past one meeting cycle. Frame as a decision-quality process.
- Build-on ("yes, and") versus block ("yes, but") warm-up: a five-minute pair exercise before brainstorming; repeat as a standing meeting norm.

## 6. Project risk in the management plan

Use a 20-item project risk questionnaire (scored 0 to 3) to feed a likelihood-impact matrix and a control plan. Classify unanticipated risk as change (scope, people, environment), capability (skills, knowledge, tools) or capacity (time, budget, people). Run five activities: identify early; assess likelihood and impact; define mitigation; assign owners; review at every checkpoint. Capacity is the commonest failure in small firms (staff diverted, resigning or ill), so add timeline buffers and cover for key-person dependencies.

## 7. Distributed and multi-site teams

Five disciplines: nurture (regular one-to-one and team check-ins, deliberate social contact); accessibility (stated response times per channel); interaction skills (video etiquette, explicit acknowledgement in place of non-verbal cues); delegation by outcome with agreed checkpoints and matching authority; trust (scrupulous follow-through, visibility of remote staff).
East African practice: messaging apps carry most management traffic; agree response-time norms (for example voice note within four hours, formal request within 24 hours; treat as planning assumptions and confirm locally); keep audio-only fallbacks for weak connectivity; schedule regular headquarters visits where budget allows; keep a social group separate from the task group; give regional managers explicit local decision authority and review outcomes; recognise regional teams by name in headquarters communications.

## 8. Employee opinion surveys

Principle: a survey creates a commitment to act; an unanswered survey damages trust.
- Design: use focus groups to find issues first; one idea per item; mix scaled and open questions; use few negatively worded items; collect only the demographic detail needed, because detail reduces perceived anonymity.
- Administration: group sessions give the highest response and highest logistics; postal returns give the highest perceived confidentiality but low response; online is fastest but raises privacy concerns. Use an external facilitator and short voice or messaging pulse surveys for lower-literacy staff.
- Analysis: report simple statistics, tell the findings as a story (why, what, recommendation), avoid over-reading small differences, and add an importance scale if satisfaction alone is not enough.
- Action: tie each recommendation to data, assign owners and dates, and publish the response.

## 9. Leading change

### 9.1 Readiness assessment
- Individual resilience profile: a 20-item instrument with five bands from highly flexible to very inflexible. Aggregate for a team; teams in the lower bands need longer preparation and more information before action is expected. Externally imposed change tends to meet more resistance than personal resilience scores predict.
- Organisational readiness survey (22 items, reverse-scoring alternate items): distinguishes first-order change (surface adjustment) from second-order change (structure, process, culture). A low total means pause and re-plan before launching; moving from founder-led to professionally managed is second-order change and needs far more time than usually planned.

### 9.2 Individual change cycle
Diagnose each person's stage: unaware or in denial, considering, decided and planning, acting, maintaining, or relapsing. Match the support: give facts and choice to the unaware; listen to ambivalence and weigh pros and cons for those considering; help people build their own plan when decided; give practical support during action; provide skills training and celebration during maintenance; treat relapse as data and re-enter at consideration. Programmes fail when launched at the action stage while most people are still unaware.

### 9.3 Seven-step change plan
1. Confirm mission and purpose and who must shape it.
2. Audit the organisation (offer, markets, capabilities and gaps, cost and revenue performance).
3. Scan the external environment (competitors, their markets, regulation).
4. Research the specific question, choosing quantitative or qualitative and internal or external.
5. Set short- and long-term goals: what changes, order, dates, champions, resources, change agents.
6. Build and run the action plan with interdependencies and evaluation.
7. Embed an early-warning and feedback system and a negotiation and communication plan.
Assign one person, even part-time, to external scanning.

### 9.4 Empathy and mindset exercises
- Loss exercise: contestants give up objects of unequal personal value while an audience predicts choices, showing that leaders undervalue what others must give up. Ask what each group is asked to surrender and what could replace it.
- "Knowing the answer closes exploration": use a wagon-with-square-wheels image to prompt teams to find their own unused better practices. Link to the four levers of change: discomfort with the status quo, attractiveness of the vision, past success with change, and peer support.

## 10. Adapting for Uganda and East Africa

1. Low literacy: read items aloud; use show-of-hands or coloured cards for scales; hold facilitated discussion with a scribe; collect voice-note answers; pair readers with non-readers for non-confidential team work; prefer exercises over questionnaires.
2. Hierarchical deference: guarantee anonymity structurally, use external facilitators, have the most senior person complete instruments and share first, frame as improvement, not rating.
3. Group harmony: use private scoring before discussion (nominal group, written alignment input); prefer tools that name group dynamics over individual feedback; present conflict tools as process tools.
4. Age and seniority: use written instruments when a junior manager must give feedback to an older report.
5. Ethnic diversity: prefer structures that equalise voice (cards, go-arounds, secret ballots); watch for coalitions that follow ethnic lines.
6. Relationship-first communication: build greetings and personal enquiry into meeting design and team-building.

## 11. Plan-section prompts

- Which management tools will be used, by whom, how often and at what cost?
- What is the delegation and authority matrix?
- How will performance be appraised and how often?
- How will staff opinion be measured and acted on?
- What is the change plan for any restructuring or new system?
- How are remote sites managed?

Sources consulted: Silberman (ed.), The Active Manager's Tool Kit, McGraw-Hill, 2003; Prochaska, Norcross and DiClemente on the transtheoretical model of change; Delbecq, Van de Ven and Gustafson on the nominal group technique. Cost, timing and response-time figures are planning assumptions to be verified locally.
