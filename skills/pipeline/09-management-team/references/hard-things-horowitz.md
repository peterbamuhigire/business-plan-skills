# Leadership Under Pressure: Crisis Leadership and Founder Resilience

Use this reference when Section 09 must profile the founder or chief executive, when Section 12 (Risk Analysis) needs a crisis-leadership pathway, and when Section 13 (Implementation) needs culture and training milestones. It applies to any venture that expects competitive or operational crises, which includes most early-stage businesses in East Africa.

Core position: plans usually describe how the team will act when things go well. Sophisticated funders test what the team does when things go badly. Show that the founding team has thought about the bad periods.

## 1. Founder resilience profile

Every founder meets a period when the product has faults, the market is slower than forecast, key staff leave, cash runs low and self-doubt is severe. Treat it as normal, not as proof of failure, and prepare for it.

Preparation actions to describe in the plan:
1. Share the burden. Give the senior team the real position so more minds work on the problem.
2. Keep looking for another move. Complex businesses nearly always have options (restructure, renegotiate, pivot, raise bridge funding, sell an asset).
3. Survive long enough for conditions to change; protect the cash runway first.
4. Separate self-worth from business outcomes; decisions were made under uncertainty.
5. Build a support network of peer founders and a mentor or board member.
6. Write down the reasoning behind hard decisions; writing separates judgement from emotion.
7. Focus on the route, not the obstacle: agree the next three actions and do them.

Plan implication: a plan with no downside scenario, no crisis pathway and no founder resilience profile lacks credibility. Include a short "when things go wrong" paragraph naming the trigger events, the decision-maker, the first 30 days of response and the support available to the founder.

## 2. Transparency and information flow

Set out a communication standard the plan can describe:
- Trust reduces the communication cost inside a team. If staff are treated as unable to handle bad news, every message needs extra checking and rumour fills the gap.
- More people on a hard problem shortens the time to solve it, but only if they know about it.
- Healthy culture moves bad news up quickly and good news at normal speed. Failed companies typically had staff who knew of fatal problems early but were discouraged from saying so.
- Avoid the rule "never bring a problem without a solution"; it suppresses early warning. Ask for the problem first and develop the solution jointly.

Measurable proxies: frequency of all-staff updates, existence of an anonymous escalation route, time from issue raised to owner assigned.

## 3. Restructuring and redundancy done properly

If the business misses its plan so far that jobs must go, the way it is done decides whether the culture survives. Procedure:
1. Leadership settles its own position first; remaining staff need a leader, not a victim.
2. Decide and act quickly; delay creates leaks that force managers to lie, stay silent or confirm.
3. State the reason plainly and truthfully (the business missed its plan and must correct its cost base). Do not describe people as "positions eliminated".
4. Brief and train managers to deliver the news personally, with answers on severance, references, benefits and next steps.
5. Speak to each person directly and with dignity; no euphemisms; provide a complete package; thank them.

Local check: retrenchment in Uganda is governed by employment legislation on notice, consultation and severance. Verify the current provisions (Employment Act 2006 and amendments) with counsel and the Chwezi finance engine before costing any severance in the plan.

## 4. Removing a senior executive

Leaders usually act late; the organisation has often decided months before the chief executive. Procedure:
- Prepare: take legal and board advice; plan cover for the function and the direct reports; prepare the exact words.
- Conversation: state the decision directly, give one honest reason, avoid over-explaining (which invites negotiation), and treat the person with respect.
- Aftermath: tell the team the same day, state truthfully that the fit or role changed, and never disparage the departing executive.

## 5. Ordering of priorities: people, product, profit

A good workplace lets people focus on work and trust that results bring fair reward. A poor one consumes effort in boundaries, infighting and broken processes, with no clear role or measure and denial from management. The quality of the workplace matters most when economics deteriorate, because good staff leave first and the decline then feeds itself. Treat workplace quality as an objective in itself and describe how it will be measured (retention, engagement survey, promotion fairness).

## 6. Training as a high-leverage management activity

Formula to use in the plan: leverage = (hours gained across the trained group) divided by (preparation and delivery hours). Illustration for the plan to recompute with its own figures: a 12-hour programme that lifts the output of ten staff by 1% over a year of about 20,000 total work hours yields 200 hours gained. Managers who train their own teams deliver specific, relevant content. Add a one-page "what excellent and poor performance look like" note for each key role to calibrate the team.

## 7. Minimising politics

Political behaviour usually starts at the top: rewarding an employee who agitates for a raise teaches everyone that agitation pays.
1. Hire for the right ambition: ambition for the company's success, with personal success following from it. Interview signals: the "team" candidate rarely says "I", credits others and asks how the company will win before asking about pay; the "me" candidate treats the company as a career accessory.
2. Build fixed, visible, defensible processes for evaluation and pay, organisation design and territory, and promotion. Involve the board in senior decisions. Grant no exceptions, because each becomes the new standard.
3. Handle executive-to-executive complaints by type: behavioural complaints are resolved with both parties present; competence complaints are not debated, are neither endorsed nor dismissed, and trigger an independent reassessment within days.

## 8. Managing the leader's psychology

Two failure modes: taking events too personally (terrorising the team, or freezing) and not personally enough (rationalising, failing to fix real problems). Controls: peer-founder network, written reasoning, attention on the path, and persistence. Name the support mechanism in the plan.

## 9. Founder type: strategy-led versus execution-led

| Type | Strength | Typical gap |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy-led | Direction, decisions under thin information | Process design, goal setting, training, structured accountability |
| Execution-led | Operational delivery, clear goals, accountability | Strategic thinking, decisions under high uncertainty |

Most founders are strategy-led. Profile the founder on this axis and show how the other skill is covered (finance manager, operations co-founder, experienced operations hire). Succession between types creates drift or executive turnover, so plan it deliberately.

## 10. Three leadership attributes to evidence

1. Ability to articulate a compelling vision that survives setbacks.
2. The right ambition: staff believe the leader cares about them and the company more than about personal gain, which produces ownership behaviour.
3. Competence to deliver the vision, which is learnable through study, teaching and practice.

Test question: how many, how good and how varied are the people who choose to follow this leader?

## 11. Peacetime and wartime leadership

- Peacetime: large advantage in a growing core market. Encourage broad creativity, empower detailed decisions, tolerate deviations, minimise conflict, build consensus, set ambitious goals, train for career development.
- Wartime: an existential threat from competition, market change, macro shock or supply disruption. Focus on one priority, show intolerance of deviation, attend to detail that affects the priority, decide without full consensus, train for survival, and hold the capacity to run layoffs.

Few leaders do both well. State which mode the business is in and whether the leader is calibrated for it. Many East African SMEs operate in near-permanent wartime (thin cash, late payments, informal competitors, compliance change).

## 12. Accountability versus creativity

Hold people accountable for: effort (non-negotiable); routine promises (strict); results, adjusted for seniority (senior people forecast more accurately), degree of difficulty, and avoidable risk (good risk that failed differs from stupid risk). Aim to be urgent without being punitive.

## 13. Feedback standards

Be authentic; give feedback because you want the person to succeed; never disguise a dismissal as feedback; never humiliate anyone before peers; fit the tone to the person; be direct but not unkind. Positive-negative-positive sequencing becomes predictable and loses credibility. Frequent, routine feedback makes each instance less charged.

## 14. Hiring for strength

Define the strengths the role needs first, then test for them, accepting visible weaknesses. Funders reading bios will see gaps (short tenure, no sector history, unusual background); acknowledge them and explain why the strengths outweigh them.

## 15. Uganda and East Africa adaptation

| Principle | Local application |
|---|---|
| Founder resilience | Currency movement, late public payments, mobile-money outages, power cuts, regulatory surprises. Show a resilience plan. |
| Transparency | Deference upward can hide bad news; lenders who hear bad news late lose confidence permanently, so report early. |
| Redundancy | Follow statutory notice and severance; verify current law. |
| People first | Owner-managers often know each employee's family and welfare situation; treat this as an informal contract that retains loyalty, and formalise pay and appraisal alongside it. |
| Politics and family | Family hires with personal-income ambition create the same political dynamics and are harder to address; set role expectations and accountability from day one. |
| Wartime | Compete against informal operators with no overhead; do not manage by consensus alone. |
| Training | Few staff receive structured role training; a plan with shadowing, written procedures and monthly role reviews is stronger. |
| Founder type | Founders are often strategy-led; name the gap and the hire or partner that covers it. |
