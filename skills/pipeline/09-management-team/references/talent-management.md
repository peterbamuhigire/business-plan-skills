# Talent Management System: Identify, Develop, Reward, Retain and Succeed

Use this reference when Section 09 must show how the business finds, grades, develops, rewards and retains its best people and covers key-person risk, and when Section 13 or the financial model needs a talent budget. It gives a full system for larger firms and a minimum viable version for an East African SME with no HR department. Proportions and percentages are illustrative planning assumptions to calibrate against the firm's own data.

## 1. Purpose and design principles

Talent management links appraisal, competency assessment, potential forecasting, career planning, succession, development, pay and selection into one system aligned with strategy. Long-run studies of sustained performers point to six conditions: a performance culture; low turnover among the most valuable staff; high satisfaction; qualified replacements for key positions; effective investment in pay and development; and use of shared competencies in selection and appraisal.

Three outcomes to design for:
1. Identify and retain the small group of exceptional contributors whose loss disproportionately damages growth.
2. Keep qualified replacements ready for key positions (a small share of roles, often in the range of 8% to 12%).
3. Allocate pay, training and development differently according to actual and potential contribution, not equally.

Failure mode: uniform treatment ("the mediocrity trap"), which drives out the best and rewards the weakest.

Align the talent type to strategy and culture (a risk-averse bureaucracy and an open innovative firm need different people). Chain: strategy, operating model, critical objectives, human-capital success factors, target talent portfolio, then attract, retain, develop and leverage processes. Classify talent segments as strategic (competitive advantage), core (essential operations), requisite (support) and non-core.

## 2. Grading people

### 2.1 Four working groups (illustrative proportions)
| Group | Definition | Share (assumption) | Investment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top talent | Greatly exceeds expectations, lifts others, embodies core competencies | about 3 to 5% | Very high: accelerated career, mentoring, sponsorship |
| High performers | Exceed expectations and help others improve | about 20 to 25% | High: fast progression |
| Core performers | Meet expectations | about 70% | Moderate: enough to sustain contribution |
| Underperformers | Below expectations | no more than about 5% | Remedial only; exit unless quick improvement |

Economic case: in complex roles superior performers produce materially more than average (published estimates rise from roughly a fifth more in low-complexity jobs to about half more in professional and managerial jobs and considerably more in sales); two average performers do not replace one superior one. Verify current evidence before quoting figures.

### 2.2 Four building blocks
1. Competency framework (behavioural expectations).
2. Performance appraisal (results and competencies).
3. Potential forecast (how far and how fast someone can progress).
4. Replacement and succession planning.

### 2.3 Competency framework
Competencies are observable, measurable knowledge, skills and behaviours; they predict job success better than academic credentials or general ability. Most organisations use nine to eleven. Representative set: action orientation; communication; creativity and innovation; critical judgement; customer orientation; interpersonal skill; leadership; teamwork; technical or functional expertise.
Building methods, from most to least reliable:
1. Behavioural event interviews with acknowledged superior versus average performers, coding the differences.
2. Statistical analysis of multi-rater ratings against performance outcomes.
3. Selecting from a standard list the 8 to 12 that most separate superior from average performers (ask which two or three would be most damaging if absent).
Expert panels are the least reliable and produce vague definitions.
Refinement: map each competency to levels (all staff, professional, supervisor, manager, senior management, executive) so the same competency has level-appropriate behaviours; optionally weight levels like a job-evaluation scheme. Review as strategy changes; top leadership must own the framework because people behave as they are measured.

### 2.4 Appraisal design
| Type | Basis | Use | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trait-based | Stable traits | Broad culture fit | Weak validity, legal challenge |
| Knowledge and skill-based | Skills cause performance | Technical roles | Misses behaviour |
| Results-based | Measurable outcomes | Roles with clear outputs | Misses process and competency |
Combine results-based appraisal (accountable outcomes) with competency ratings (behaviours). Use a five-point scale from greatly below to greatly exceeds expectations.
Results measures should be specific, practical, obtainable, relevant and timely. Support-role examples: proposals implemented per proposals made; internal-client service ratings; exceptions found by outside audit; complaints and commendations; spend against plan.
Feedback conversation: separate expectations, neutral observations, personal assessments and consequences. The conversation is the intervention; discipline in running the process matters more than perfect forms.

### 2.5 Potential forecast
Scale: high potential (several levels, likely top talent); promotable (ready or nearly); lateral (best moved across functions); marginal; none.
Factors distinguishing potential from present performance: breadth of management scope; breadth of problem-solving (systemic thinking); breadth of impact beyond the team; self-awareness; motivation and drive; self-confidence. Three quick questions for high-potential leaders: how do they handle complex challenges; how well do they develop others; how do they present as a leader. Only those strong on all three qualify.
Promotion trap: excellence in the current role does not predict success at the next level because required competencies change. Assess capacity for the next level.

### 2.6 Performance-potential grid
Plot performance against potential (5 x 5, or 3 x 3 for small firms). Broad groups: high performance and high potential (invest heavily, about a fifth to a quarter); high potential and moderate performance (develop, the largest group); low or moderate performance and low potential (manage or exit, about a tenth to a seventh); high performance and low potential (retain and leverage; watch for blocking promotable staff). Segmentation can also use strategic impact, performance data, unit or job family, skills profile, demographics, and values and preferences (security versus growth, mobility).

## 3. Acquiring talent

- Magnetic culture: pride in the workplace, willingness to recommend it and discretionary effort come from mixed extrinsic and intrinsic rewards, clear career paths, a compelling purpose, a respectful workplace and investment in growth.
- Competency-based selection: use the same framework as internal assessment; adapt the scale to compare candidates against each other. Process: identify critical competencies; define scales by level; write a structured interview outline; score self-assessment, interviewer assessment and references; test values fit; track pre-hire scores against later performance to validate.
- Selection tool principles: credibility, validity, user-friendliness, simplicity (no more than about eight competencies in a tool), inclusion of HR, IT and business leaders, and continuous testing of predictive value.
- Recruiting exceptional talent: employee referrals of outstanding people elsewhere (with a tracking list); adjacent non-competitor firms; search consultants who hold passive-candidate networks; downturns, when people feel insecure. They move for career clarity, growth and respect, not salary alone; recruit them to a mission, not a vacancy.
- Pipelines: graduate schemes, internships (extended assessment), rotational programmes, referral incentives, and succession planning as a continuous development system.
East African partners: universities and business schools, technical and vocational institutions and their placement offices; internships cost less than reactive recruitment.

## 4. Developing people

### 4.1 Twelve development methods
| Method | Goal | Target |
|---|---|---|
| On-the-job coaching and mentoring | Close current competency gaps | Everyone, when a gap appears |
| Staff meetings on live problems | Problem-solving practice; reveal ability | Everyone |
| Job rotation (allow at least about 18 months) | Breadth; latent ability | Top and high performers |
| Emergency fill-in assignments | Adaptability under pressure | Top and high performers |
| Special one-person projects | Strengthen specific competencies | Top and high performers |
| Task forces | Group leadership, pre-management skill | All levels with supervisor approval |
| Internal courses and e-learning | Foundation skills | Everyone |
| External courses and e-learning | External perspective | Priority to top and high performers |
| Guided reading | Self-directed knowledge | Everyone, on manager recommendation |
| Teaching courses | Deepen knowledge, communication, values | Strong performers |
| Counselling or psychological support | Personal development blocks | When manager coaching fails |
| Extracurricular leadership | Apply leadership outside work | Top and high performers |

### 4.2 Individual development plan (IDP)
Contents: strengths with concrete examples; development areas with evidence; target behaviours; accountable actions with deliverables; company support (resources, coaching, assignments); timeline and review dates. The supervisor drafts and discusses it with the employee; it turns ratings into a roadmap.

### 4.3 Multi-rater feedback
Choose instruments that are valid, reliable across raters, behavioural and positively phrased. Steps: align with the competency framework; train raters (avoid halo and leniency); manage anonymity; compare self and others' ratings; run a facilitated feedback workshop; agree development actions and re-assess after four to six months. Misuse warnings: do not base pay or promotion mainly on it; never use without trained facilitation; supplement with manager observation and results data. Link results to IDPs, quarterly reviews, mentoring and next-role targets.

### 4.4 Coaching
Why it works: more complex jobs, scarce honest feedback, flat structures needing people skills, rapid change, openness to support, demonstrable return. Outcomes: retention, productivity, development of high potentials, satisfaction, objective achievement.
Seven conditions for a successful engagement: contracting (objectives, scope, process, measures); commitment of leader and sponsor; coachability of person and issue; a course of action; confidentiality; chemistry; clarity on outcomes.
Leader categories: emerging (career mapping, visibility, strategic perspective); developing (specific derailing behaviours, stakeholder relationships); strategic (senior effectiveness, legacy, succession, major transitions).
Coaching for top talent is a collaboration among employee, boss, HR and coach; key themes are traction, relationship building, momentum, candour, trust, boss involvement and willingness to address difficult behaviour. Share only thematic progress with the sponsor.

### 4.5 Self-directed learning
Five discoveries: ideal self (personal vision); real self (multi-rater data, strengths and gaps); learning agenda (build on strengths while closing critical gaps); experimentation and practice; trusting relationships (coaches, peers, mentors).
Emotional-intelligence clusters (Goleman, Boyatzis and McKee): self-awareness; self-management; social awareness; relationship management. No leader is strong in every competency, so adapt competency models to context.

### 4.6 The 70/20/10 principle
About 70% of learning comes from challenging work, about 20% from relationships and coaching and about 10% from formal training (a widely used heuristic; treat it as a planning rule of thumb). Budget for stretch assignments and coaching before courses. This favours small firms because the best tools cost little.

## 5. Succession planning

### 5.1 Why plans fail
Common causes: treated as an annual HR exercise; leadership not truly involved; no link to development spend; focus on who replaces whom instead of capability pools; unclear communication with candidates about what high-potential status means.

### 5.2 Design types
| Type | Description | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Individual-based | Named successors per position | Small firms, critical roles |
| Role-based | Planned by role family | Larger firms with similar roles |
| Pool-based | Cohort developed for future senior roles | Large firms, general management capability |

### 5.3 Five stages
1. Design and preplanning: define future leadership competencies; make succession a chief-executive accountability; celebrate talent identification; train participants.
2. Assessment: use the competency framework and grid; identify business-critical positions; calibrate assessments; draw replacement charts (position, incumbent, successors, readiness).
3. Development: IDPs for all candidates; stretch, rotation and coaching; exposure to the board for senior candidates.
4. Monitoring: structured reviews at least annually with quarterly chart updates; annual bench-strength report to the board.
5. Communication: tell candidates they are valued and invested in without promising specific roles or dates.

### 5.4 Readiness and bench strength
Readiness classes: ready now (0 to 12 months), ready in 12 to 24 months, ready in three to five years.
Bench-strength summary per key position: incumbent; potential rating; performance rating; readiness status; named replacements.
Five critical situations and responses:
| Situation | Meaning | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Void | No replacement | Draw from the pool, accelerate development, name backups, start external recruitment |
| Surplus | More than one replacement | Redirect careers, move high potentials, rotate, task forces |
| Blockage | Non-promotable incumbent blocks promotable staff | Move the incumbent within a year, rotate, give blocked talent projects |
| Performance problem | Incumbent below expectations | One more review on results and competencies; exit if no fast improvement |
| Crown jewel | Exceptional talent | Accelerated path, mentor and sponsor, top pay growth, heavy development |

### 5.5 Chief-executive succession
The board is actively responsible; the chief executive develops successors with real responsibility; all candidates meet the board directly; part of the chief executive's incentive links to succession effort; the number two must be respected and given meaningful scope or will be recruited away.

### 5.6 Family businesses
- Family participation plan: terms for entering and advancing, with merit criteria.
- Successor development plan: required skills and qualities, timetable, measurement and performance-based milestones.
- Stewardship mindset: the business is held in trust for later generations.
- Shareholders' agreement drawn up early: ownership transitions, buy-out mechanisms, decision rights.
East African minimum: name one or two potential successors explicitly; give them independent management of a department or project; assign a mentor from outside the family; draft the shareholders' agreement before it is needed; document key processes so the business does not depend on the founder's knowledge.

### 5.7 Key-person contingency
For every key position: at least one backup (two where possible; one ready now for critical roles); document held knowledge, relationships and processes; if there is a void, recruit externally and develop internally in parallel; for top talent, prepare a retention plan before any resignation.

## 6. Differentiating reward and development

| Group | Pay | Development | Career | Recognition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Top talent | Well ahead of market | Major | Very rapid | Very high |
| High performers | Ahead of market | Substantial | Rapid | High |
| Core performers | Rise moderately until competitive | Current and future competencies only | Moderate | As appropriate |
| Underperformers | No increase | Only if improvement is realistic | None | None |
Communicate the basis of pay often and openly; staff who understand it are more satisfied. Answer five questions for every reward programme: what it does for me; how decisions are made; whether it is fair; what I must do to succeed; where to get questions answered.

## 7. Retention

### 7.1 Why people leave
Typical reasons among senior staff (especially women): culture (style criticised, ideas ignored, integrity or inclusion issues); no growth; work-life imbalance; gap between stated values and management behaviour; too little recognition. Many firms keep hiring while restructuring and some create targeted retention programmes; motivating after hiring is as hard as attracting.

### 7.2 Practices
- Stay interviews: regular one-to-ones asking what they look forward to, what keeps them, what might entice them away, what would make work more satisfying, what they are learning, and what the manager could do differently. Hold them monthly with top talent for early warning and quarterly for the top five in a small firm (15 minutes).
- Written career plan co-owned by manager and employee (direction, growth, responsibility, reward path).
- Early-warning monitoring of engagement.
- Say explicitly that the person is valued and has a future.
- Support external board or industry-body service to build visibility.
- Avoid golden handcuffs that make people feel trapped, not valued.

### 7.3 Total rewards
Four dimensions of the value proposition: individual growth; a compelling future; a positive workplace (respect, manager quality, safety, balance, inclusion); total pay (competitive and fair, not necessarily highest).
Reward elements: membership-based (cost-of-living adjustment, profit sharing, share purchase, training, insurance, social events) and performance-based (merit pay, bonuses, options or restricted shares, development opportunities, special projects, recognition awards). Use both: membership-only creates entitlement; performance-only creates a transactional culture.

### 7.4 Long-term incentives
| Type | Mechanism | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Stock options | Right to buy at fixed price | Listed, high-growth firms |
| Performance plans | Cash or shares for achieving targets | Any firm |
| Restricted stock | Shares vesting over time or on performance | Retention |
| Phantom stock | Cash linked to share price without transfer | Private and family firms |
| Dividend equivalents | Cash equal to dividends | Supplement |
High-performing firms commonly combine two types. For private East African SMEs, practical alternatives: transparent profit-sharing formulas; deferred cash bonuses vesting after two to three years; formal revenue-share or ownership stakes at defined milestones; hard-to-copy benefits such as housing or vehicle allowances. Verify tax and company-law treatment with the Chwezi finance engine and counsel.

### 7.4a Regional pressures and counters
Pressures: non-profit and development-agency pay premiums; multinational regional offices; diaspora and overseas study pull; public-sector security and pensions. Counters: development mission and purpose; learning intensity (be the "talent school"); flexible working; public recognition; wage benchmarking against national statistics, employer-federation surveys and informal checks, keeping core pay for top talent competitive.

## 8. Annual talent review

Design: chief-executive ownership (a performance metric, not an HR programme); each business leader reviews reports and presents upward; assessments cascade up and adjustments flow back; annual review with at least quarterly chart updates; bench-strength summary to the board and board contact with senior candidates.
Calibration: managers assess performance, potential and competencies; peers challenge ratings with behavioural evidence; HR ensures consistent standards; the executive team reviews the whole grid, spending most time on the top-talent group; unresolved disagreements are settled with more data (multi-rater results, performance evidence). Misclassifying either way is costly (under-investment or the wrong signal).
Communicating: tell candidates they are valued, what will be invested, that their trajectory is positive and what they must do to progress. Do not name them as successor to a specific post, give promotion dates or reveal others in the pool.

## 9. Minimum viable system for a 10 to 100 person business

1. Define 5 to 8 core competencies in about two hours, grounded in what makes people excellent in this business (for example results delivery, customer orientation, technical expertise, problem-solving, teamwork, communication in the working languages, initiative).
2. Assess every employee twice a year (about 30 minutes each): three-point scale (exceeds, meets, below) on each competency plus overall performance, and a potential view (yes, maybe, no). Produce a one-page snapshot.
3. Plot a 3 x 3 grid on a whiteboard (about one hour): top right are top talent; the middle band core performers; bottom left need remediation or exit.
4. Write one-page IDPs for the top three to five people: what they excel at, most important development area, the stretch assignment in the next six months, the mentor, the likely next role.
5. Build a succession table: one named backup per key role (operations, sales, finance, any holder of key customer or supplier relationships); no backup means a void and the top recruitment or development task.
6. Hold quarterly stay interviews with the top five.
7. Differentiate reward: above-inflation raises for top talent, market-tracking raises for others; best stretch work and learning to top and high performers; public recognition by name.

Low-cost tools:
| Tool | Cost | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Acting-up assignments | Nil | Cover for a manager, then debrief |
| Cross-functional project leadership | Nil | Coordinate operations, sales and finance |
| Internal mentoring | Nil | Pair juniors with experienced leaders monthly |
| Structured reading | Low | One book per quarter with discussion |
| Industry association participation | Low | Sector forums and manufacturers' or export bodies |
| Teaching a topic internally | Nil | 30-minute briefing by a staff member |
| Short external courses | Medium | Local business schools and management institutes |
| Peer coaching | Nil | Two high performers from different functions, structured monthly agenda |

No HR department: the managing director owns talent management; line managers are the main development vehicle, so train them to give feedback and coach; keep forms to one page and use a whiteboard rather than unused software; use external mentors from business associations, management institutes and peer networks; formalise (standard IDP template, replacement chart, biannual review) as the firm reaches 30 to 50 staff.

## 10. Plan-section prompts

- Which roles are key and who is the named backup for each?
- How are top talent identified, and what is the retention plan and budget?
- What is the competency set and assessment calendar?
- What development budget and low-cost tools are planned?
- What incentive structure suits the ownership form?
- What is the founder succession plan and when will shareholders' agreements be signed?
