# Pitch Message and Delivery: Clarity, Conviction and Q&A Control

Task reference for the Pitch Preparation meta-skill. Use it to sharpen what is said (message architecture), how it is said (voice, pace, presence) and how questions are handled (steering and bridging), for live pitches and, by translation, for the written plan. It synthesises broadcast-journalism and executive-coaching practice, including the seven-principle approach popularised by Bill McGowan, with general public-speaking guidance.

Planning assumption: claims that many proposals fail through poor communication, and that attention spans are shrinking, are widely repeated but weakly sourced. Do not quote them in a plan without a dated primary source.

## 1. Seven working principles

| # | Principle | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lead with the headline | The first half-minute decides the audience's engagement; open with your strongest, newest material |
| 2 | Show, do not tell | Use concrete stories, images and analogies so listeners see the point |
| 3 | Reduce | The more you cut, the stronger the message; brevity signals mastery |
| 4 | Do not outrun your thinking | Pace should match certainty about the next sentence; pause instead of filling |
| 5 | Sound certain | Words, voice, eyes and posture must agree; remove hedging |
| 6 | Show curiosity | Be as interested as you are interesting; listen at least half the time |
| 7 | Steer the conversation | If a line of questioning hurts, move it to ground where you are strong, politely |

## 2. Headline: opening procedure

1. Find the line you would say after "you will not believe this" or "did you know". It is often buried mid-script; move it to the front.
2. Make it short, surprising and slightly suspenseful. Do not reveal the topic label before creating curiosity.
3. Test it at home on a distracted listener.
4. Rehearse the first sixty to ninety seconds until word-perfect, because nerves peak there.

Avoid these openers:
- Agenda setting ("today I will talk about...").
- Announcing the duration.
- Long thanks, or "delighted to be here".
- Apologising for the hour, room or state of the audience.
- Untested jokes.

Opener types that work: a short story, a counter-intuitive number, a provocative declaration, a question the audience privately holds.

## 3. Show, do not tell: storytelling procedure

Structure: build, reveal, exit.
- Build: set the scene with few characters and moving parts; do not assume the audience knows context; make it collapsible, so you can shorten two minutes to fifteen seconds if attention drops.
- Reveal: deliver the surprising or counter-intuitive point after a beat of suspense.
- Exit: pause after the reveal; do not explain it away.
- Do not announce "I will now tell you a story"; weave it in.

Signs you are losing the listener (act by skipping to the reveal): shifting feet; eyes scanning the room; a "wrap it up" hand circle; checking a device; looking over your shoulder; finishing your sentences for you.

Making numbers vivid: pair each key figure with an analogy from the audience's world (scale against a familiar object, a day, a stadium, a household budget). Signal significance with voice: raise pitch for a surprising figure, lower it for an unacceptable one, slow the pace on the number.

Rehearsal tools: storyboard the story (one sketch per step; an empty frame means missing detail); write it as a screenplay (show through action and dialogue, not abstract statements).

## 4. Reduce: brevity rules

Common reasons for over-long delivery: belief that more words persuade; wish to seem clever; obligation to fill the slot; poor timing in rehearsal; a captive audience. Repetition of an agenda and recap wastes time.

Techniques:
1. Fix the opening and the close; let the middle expand or contract with the room.
2. Detach from content; most decks improve when cut by around a quarter.
3. Put the point first.
4. Time every rehearsal and keep trimming.
5. Do not commit to a numbered count of points aloud; say "a few" unless the structure is written on the slide.
6. End on the future benefit or the requested action, not a recap.

Attention planning: assume drift after several minutes and a clear decline in the second quarter-hour; build in three-to-five-minute segments, each with its own hook.
Listening ratio for client pitches: spend about three times as long on their challenges as on yourself.

## 5. Pace and pauses

Fast speech reads as anxious or defensive. Fillers (um, like, you know, sort of) are what the mouth produces when it runs ahead of thought; suppressing them by willpower fails, whereas slowing down works. Use the "fast on familiar ground, slow on unfamiliar" rule: brisk through rehearsed material, slower at decision points where the wording matters.

Reasons to slow down: holds attention (structure and silence create anticipation); conveys confidence; reduces self-correction; shows you heard the question; removes fillers.

Clean speech: plain words, no jargon, no showy vocabulary, moderate pace, full attention on the sentence being spoken.

Vocal tools:
- Pitch: higher for surprise, lower for seriousness.
- Pace: vary on purpose.
- Volume: dropping slightly on the most important phrase draws listeners in.
Pause after a key reveal and before a punchline; a pause that feels long to the speaker is short to the audience.

## 6. Conviction

Remove hedges: "kind of", "sort of", "I think" (before opinions you hold), "you may not like this but", "let me just quickly", "I will only take a minute", and pre-emptive self-defeat ("this may be a silly idea"). Replace with the plain declarative form and state evidence.

Three habits to avoid in corporate speech:
1. Clichés ("at the end of the day", "level the playing field", "journey").
2. Industry jargon and invented words; test by recording, transcribing and reading. If you cannot explain it simply, you may not understand it well enough.
3. Over-simplification; address the audience as intelligent but unfamiliar with your field.

Physical conviction:
- Standing: shoulders back, arms relaxed at about a right angle, hands loosely together at belt level when not gesturing, weight slightly forward.
- Postures that signal doubt: hands behind the back, arms crossed, hands in pockets, hands on hips, arms hanging (makes gestures look staged).
- Sitting: chair high enough, sit forward, forearms on the table edge, no swivelling, feet grounded.
- Expansive postures held for a couple of minutes beforehand are reported to raise confidence; treat the physiological claims as unproven and use the posture for comfort.

Difficult feedback: sandwich criticism between real strengths; acknowledge a dissenting view without conceding the point.

Speaking anxiety usually moves through dread, tolerance and enjoyment. Simulate enjoyment while practising until it becomes real.

## 7. Curiosity and listening

- Interest: ask organic follow-up questions from what was said, not a scripted list.
- Generosity: include others, seek their views, treat dissent respectfully.
- Modesty: in networking, let the other person ask about you; going second lets you tailor.
- Face: a relaxed, closed-mouth, slightly smiling, warm expression while listening; avoid a blank or stern resting face and distracting mouth movements.
- Eye contact: if uncomfortable, look at a point near the other person's eye line (an ear or the hairline); it reads as contact.
- Rule: listen and ask for at least half of any conversation; do not steer every topic back to yourself.
- Smiling improves perceived sincerity and sociability; use it sincerely.

## 8. Steering questions (bridging)

Poor techniques: reciting the script whatever is asked; abrupt topic change (listeners trust such speakers less); saying "I am not here to discuss that"; swerving between topics; starting to answer, then stopping.

Techniques that work:
1. Widen the topic just enough to leave a hostile narrow zone while staying related (for example, from one incident to what firms in the sector commonly face).
2. Pick up one word from the question and bridge from it.
3. Address the topic in general terms, then state your point.
4. Reclaim the floor from a dominant speaker by finishing the tail of their sentence and moving on without a gap.
5. Use a frank pivot word ("look", "listen") followed by your key point in an open tone.

Before answering any question, settle: my point; how I will illustrate it (story, example, data); my first five words. Begin forming the answer as soon as the topic is signalled, not at the end of the question.

Do not repeat negative words while refuting them; state what is true. Do not open with "great question"; pause and answer.

Do not run from an ambush; show willingness to engage later, give a contact route and a time.

## 9. Message-architecture tools

- Hook, body, payoff: hook (headline); body (visual stories, figures with analogies, evidence, collapsible by a third); payoff (big idea, silence for a beat, close on the future benefit or action).
- Flagging: signal a key point before delivering it ("here is the number that changed the plan").
- Transitions: a one-sentence bridge recaps the last point and announces the next.
- Cocktail-party test: could you explain this point to a stranger so that they ask a follow-up?
- "So what?" test: for each claim ask why this room should care; reframe in terms of consequence.
- Prepare three or four key messages; every answer returns to one of them.

## 10. Scenario playbooks

### Job or panel interview
- "Tell me about yourself" is the opener; give your value proposition, not a timeline.
- Prepare three to five stories showing key strengths.
- Treat the interview as a presentation, not a Q&A; use "we" language when discussing what could be done.
- Prepare responses to the sector's sensitive questions with specific data.
- Do not ask questions for the sake of it; close on interest in next steps.

### Sales or client meeting
- Research the person; find a genuine common point.
- Listen three times more than you talk.
- Short-form pitch of four moves: recent similar client experience, positive outcome, a low-key offer of help, and an indication of familiarity.
- Avoid flattering every question.

### Investor pitch
Headline the problem or traction; use a customer story; cut the deck by a quarter; slow down on valuation and use of funds; remove hedges from projections and state assumptions; ask about the investor's thesis; bridge from hard questions to sector context. Acknowledge risks clearly and follow at once with the mitigation.

### Media or investor Q&A
Fix three or four messages first; steer every answer to one; affirmative wording only; have a route for questions you cannot answer.

### Admitting a mistake
1. Own it without qualification.
2. Present it as out of character.
3. Pivot to the fix and the result.
Do not blame others or add excess detail.

### Panel discussion
As panellist: prepare as for a speech, seek the questions early, test stories beforehand, steer off-message questions, do not ask permission to speak. As moderator: allocate time per topic and per speaker, open with a short round, mix question lengths, name the respondent only at the end of the question, and retake the floor with the finish-and-redirect technique.

## 11. Common mistakes and fixes

| Mistake | Effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Agenda opener | Signals nothing new | Lead with the strongest material |
| Apology opener | Suggests the audience has better things to do | Treat the slot as a privilege |
| Hedging language | Weakens the idea | Declarative statements |
| Jargon | Hides knowledge; alienates | Record, transcribe, replace with plain terms |
| Speaking too fast | Sounds anxious; produces fillers | Slow; pause; stop when unsure |
| Mid-sentence restarts | Halting | Accept the first version |
| Over-talking | Bores; poor planning | Cut a quarter; no recap |
| Buried headline | Loses audience early | Move it to the start |
| Bullets instead of stories | Forgettable | Add build, reveal, exit |
| Repeating negatives | Embeds the negative | Affirmative wording |
| Clichés | Sounds formulaic | Fresh phrasing |
| Praising the question | Reads as stalling | Pause, then answer |
| Over-reliance on improvisation | Unprepared remarks cause damage | Prepare foreseeable scenarios; test new material in low-stakes settings |
| Blank listening face | Looks disengaged | Warm, attentive expression |
| "Only two minutes of your time" | Devalues you | "I will get straight to the point" |

## 12. Application to the written plan

| Principle | Written-plan rule |
|---|---|
| Headline | Every section opens with its most compelling point, not background. Weak: "This section provides an overview of the company." Strong: a specific, sourced outcome for the reader |
| Reduce | Ask of each section what can be cut; remove any passage the reader would not miss, and any sub-point that cannot change the decision |
| Show, do not tell | Give figures an analogy (for example, compare market spend with a familiar national quantity, labelled as illustrative and sourced); use brief customer stories or testimonials |
| Conviction | Replace "we hope to", "possibly expand", "we believe there may be demand" with committed statements backed by evidence ("customer validation shows demand: [evidence]") |
| No tailgating | State the point first, then place caveats in a structured risk section rather than burying the reader in qualifications |
| Steering | Address known weaknesses (thin team, negative first-year cash flow, regulatory uncertainty) openly, place them in sector context, then give the mitigation; diligent readers will find them anyway |

## 13. Delivery mapping by audience

| Principle | Investor (equity) | Bank loan | Client proposal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headline | Market opportunity and traction | Repayment security: collateral, cash flow, track record | The client's problem, stated precisely |
| Show | Customer story; analogies for numbers | Turn projections into a story with the drivers of the coverage ratio | Comparable case study |
| Reduce | Around ten slides; leave room for conversation | One-page financial summary to leave behind; model in appendix | Proposal within the client's attention span; one-page recommendation |
| Pace | Slow on valuation and use of funds | Slow on the repayment schedule | Pause after each recommendation |
| Conviction | Projections as decisions | State the surplus after debt service as a figure | Recommend without hedging |
| Curiosity | Ask about their portfolio and thesis | Learn the lender's checklist (for example the CAMPARI criteria) first | Ask them to describe the problem in their words |
| Steer | Prepare bridges for the three hardest questions (competition, exit, team weakness) | Prepare for coverage, collateral shortfalls, sector risk | If price is challenged, steer to the cost of not solving the problem |

## 14. Preparation checklist

Content
- [ ] Three or four key messages identified; all content returns to them.
- [ ] Headline written.
- [ ] One visual story or analogy per message.
- [ ] Figures paired with analogies.
- [ ] Three hardest questions and bridges prepared.
- [ ] The sector's sensitive topic prepared.
- [ ] Collapsible structure; know what to cut if time is shortened.
- [ ] Opening sixty to ninety seconds memorised.

Delivery
- [ ] Rehearsal recorded; reviewed for pace, fillers, restarts and hedges.
- [ ] Full talk timed within the attention limit.
- [ ] Listening face practised.
- [ ] Posture checked.
- [ ] Jargon replaced.
- [ ] Hedges removed.
- [ ] Research done on the person or institution; an icebreaker prepared.

Day of delivery
- [ ] Slow breathing before starting: long inhale, brief hold, slow exhale.
- [ ] Treat adrenaline as energy and channel it into expression.
- [ ] Disable notifications and network on the presenting device.
- [ ] Final words fixed, ending decisively.

## 15. Glossary of working terms

Headline; build-reveal-exit; verbal backspacing (restating what you just said); tailgating (speaking faster than you think); filler; jargon-modified word; word hoarding (inability to cut content); curse of knowledge (assuming the audience shares your context); bridge; flag; pivot; listening face.

## 16. Uganda and East Africa calibration

- Bank meetings: many SME applications open with a description of the business. Open instead with the repayment case in local currency: collateral value and repayment capacity (surplus against instalment). Planning assumption: use only the applicant's own verified figures.
- Grant applications: open with one specific person (with consent) whose circumstances illustrate the problem, before the statistics.
- Attention: informal bank meetings may allow far less time than a formal presentation; keep a three-minute version covering the business, loan purpose, repayment plan and collateral.
- Hedging from cultural deference reads as uncertainty in credit and investor meetings; replace it with evidence plus declarative statement (for example, mobile-money turnover trends from the last twelve months, stated with the actual figures).
- Regulatory questions (EFRIS e-invoicing, national identity and business registration, NSSF, local authority licences, tax clearance): raise them proactively, then place them in context; bring the registration numbers and certificates. Tax and statutory points: verify with the Chwezi finance engine.
