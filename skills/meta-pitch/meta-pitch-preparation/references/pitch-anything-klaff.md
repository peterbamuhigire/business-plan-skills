# Pitch Dynamics: Frame Control, Investor Psychology and Deal Momentum

Task reference for the Pitch Preparation meta-skill. Use it when the pitch is a live or high-stakes interaction (investor meeting, bank credit committee, client proposal, board paper) and the plan must be presented in a way that survives a busy, sceptical, status-conscious audience. The method below is the STRONG approach associated with Oren Klaff, applied with general persuasion and negotiation practice. Treat the neuroscience language as a working model, not as settled science.

## 1. Design principle: build for the receiver

Decision-makers filter incoming pitches quickly and unconsciously. Abstract projections, dense tables and long company histories are discarded early; the listener retains only what is safe, new, simple and visual. Apply four tests to every message, slide and paragraph:

1. Safe: does it avoid triggering alarm (jargon, hidden risk, aggression)?
2. Novel: is there something the listener has not heard in this form?
3. Simple: is it concrete and big-picture rather than granular?
4. Visual: can it be shown or pictured rather than only read?

Working model: people form an emotional preference ("hot" reaction) before they analyse ("cold" reaction), and a listener in analytical mode is rarely excited at the same moment. Create wanting first; supply proof second.

## 2. STRONG sequence (architecture of the pitch)

| Step | Job | Output to prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Set the frame | Establish who controls context, time and agenda | Opening time statement; venue and seating plan |
| Tell the story | Explain why now | Three-forces narrative; one-sentence idea statement |
| Reveal the intrigue | Keep engagement, prevent analytical lock-down | A short, unresolved personal story |
| Offer the prize | Make access to you something the audience earns | Selectivity statement; qualifying questions |
| Nail the hookpoint | Reach the point where the audience asks for more | Signals list; deliberate pause |
| Get the decision | Close from strength | Real deadline; withdrawal script |

Steps overlap; frames are set, stacked and reinforced throughout.

## 3. Frame control

A frame is the context that gives a situation its meaning. Whoever sets it shapes how facts are read; logic rarely overturns an established frame. When two frames meet, the stronger absorbs the weaker, and the presenter will feel a short spell of discomfort. Hold the frame calmly and keep moving; do not seek reassurance.

### Frame types and counters

| Frame | How it appears | Counter |
|---|---|---|
| Power | The audience controls agenda, timing, attendance (lateness, interruptions, delegates sent in place of the decision-maker) | Small, good-humoured acts of non-compliance; state your own shorter time limit; keep your composure |
| Time | "We have ten minutes" | Announce your own time budget first ("about twenty minutes for the main idea, then discussion"); if they impose one mid-meeting, accept it and prepare to leave, since real interest extends the meeting |
| Analyst | Questions on unit costs, discount rates, detail; the room turns cold and analytical | Give the headline figures only, offer the workings afterwards in writing, and steer back to fit and next steps; use an intrigue story to break the mode |
| Intrigue | Your own narrative that pulls listeners out of analysis | See section 3.1 |
| Prize | You are the scarce, selective party being qualified, not a supplicant | See section 3.2 |
| Moral authority | The proposal rests on an ethical or community purpose larger than the money | See section 3.3 |

### 3.1 Building an intrigue story (checklist)

- Brief and relevant to the pitch.
- You are at its centre.
- Contains risk or uncertainty.
- A clock is running.
- Something blocks you.
- Failure carries real consequences.
- Left unresolved: stop at the most tense point, move on, and close the arc only at the end.

Prepare the story in advance and rehearse it to under a minute.

### 3.2 Prize frame

Present the deal as valuable and yourself as selective. Three behavioural drivers support it: people pursue what moves away, want what they cannot easily have, and value what is hard to obtain. Practical moves:
- Ask the counterparty to explain why they are the right partner (fit, added value, speed, networks).
- Mention other live options truthfully.
- Prefer "always be ready to leave" over "always be closing"; never chase.
- State selectivity about partners without arrogance; use light humour.

### 3.3 Moral authority frame

Anchor the venture in legacy, community benefit, protection of vulnerable people or stewardship of a public asset. It is hard to attack without appearing mercenary. Use it only where it is true and evidenced (jobs, smallholder incomes, service access, environmental outcomes).

### 3.4 Beta traps (rituals that lower your status)

Watch for: waiting-room routines and sign-in ceremonies; sitting alone in a meeting room while the audience arrives late; delegates replacing decision-makers; public-space meetings without privacy; manned trade-stand pitching; procurement processes that reduce everything to price.

Responses: recognise the ritual; comply politely without eagerness; set your own time limit ("I can wait fifteen minutes"); move the venue to your own premises where possible; do not supplicate to gatekeepers; arrive early to shape the room.

## 4. Status

Status is the platform of the pitch. Distinguish:
- Global status: accumulated rank (wealth, position). Do not pretend to it.
- Situational status: temporary standing within a domain. You can create it.

Six steps to build situational status:
1. Ignore power rituals; avoid beta traps.
2. Be unmoved by the counterparty's global rank.
3. Use small, humorous denials and challenges that reinforce your frame.
4. Move the discussion to ground where your expertise is unquestionable.
5. Apply the prize frame.
6. Invite the counterparty to state why they want to work with you, and acknowledge it lightly.

Further rules: arrive on time; create momentum early rather than waiting; avoid idle small talk; show visible enjoyment of the work; remember that status resets after every break and must be rebuilt.

## 5. Twenty-minute pitch architecture

Attention is limited; plan the core message for about twenty minutes and leave the remainder for discussion.

| Phase | Content | Indicative time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Introduce yourself and the big idea | 5 min |
| 2 | Budget and "secret sauce" | 10 min |
| 3 | The offer | 2 min |
| 4 | Stack the closing frames | 3 min |

Opening line pattern: state the time you have and that discussion follows. It sets a time frame you control and signals competence.

### Phase 1
- Background: at most two minutes, track record only. Impressions average; do not dilute the strongest achievement with mediocre ones.
- Why now: name three converging forces, economic (cost, credit, rates, purchasing power), social (behaviour, awareness, urbanisation, adoption) and technological (an enabling or disruptive change). Show the forces moving towards a window, not two static states, so the audience sees the transition.
- Idea statement (Moore's positioning template): For [target customers] who are dissatisfied with [current offerings], [product] is a [new category] that provides [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], it [differentiator].

### Phase 2
- Tune the content to how the audience thinks: people, narrative and concrete comparison beat statistics and probability.
- Numbers: experienced financiers assume every plan calls itself conservative. Demonstrate budgeting skill: assumptions, cost control, and what happens if the plan goes wrong.
- Competition: answer how easily new entrants can arrive and how easily customers can switch.
- Secret sauce: one durable advantage (technology, process, exclusive relationship, licence, first-mover position, asset). Name it briefly.

### Phase 3
State what the counterparty receives: deliverables, timing, method, and their role. Their confidence in you as operator underpins the deal.

### Phase 4
Use the frame stack in section 7.

## 6. Attention formula

Attention = desire + tension.
- Desire comes from novelty, surprise and a reward in prospect.
- Tension comes from something to gain or lose.

Creating novelty: a short demonstration; a surprising presentation of a known idea; a strong metaphor; visual contrast and movement; information that pleasantly violates expectations. Avoid over-explaining; once listeners feel they understand everything, curiosity fades.

Creating tension with push/pull: push away, pause, then pull back.
- Low intensity: suggest the fit may not be right, then note the potential if it works.
- Medium: state that you only work with partners who share a given value, then acknowledge that they evidently do.
- High: suggest the fit is poor, propose to close the meeting, and be prepared to leave.
A pitch narrative is a series of tension loops: create tension, then resolve it.

## 7. Frame stack for the final minutes

Apply in sequence: intrigue (unfinished story), prize (you are selective; they qualify), time (a genuine deadline), moral authority (values above money).

- Time frame: use a real critical path, not manufactured pressure. Sample logic: the deal proceeds on a timetable and a decision is needed by a stated date.
- Moral authority: connect the deal to a commitment you will honour regardless of the outcome of this negotiation.
- Expert stance (the "doctor" pattern): where appropriate, present recommendations with the authority of expertise, not as requests.

## 8. Removing neediness

Validation-seeking is the leading deal-killer. Typical signs: asking "what do you think so far", offering to sign immediately, trial closes ("are we in the ballpark"), repeated follow-up.

Cause: wanting something only they can give, sensing disengagement, believing acceptance will relieve anxiety, then a fear loop of anxiety, validation-seeking, visible need, withdrawal, more anxiety.

Three-step remedy:
1. Want nothing from this specific meeting; prepare alternatives beforehand.
2. Demonstrate one thing you do excellently.
3. Announce that you are leaving and begin to withdraw.

Practical rules: never trial-close; make one follow-up and then wait; end on your terms and invite them to contact you.

## 9. Hookpoint

The hookpoint is when the audience moves from receiving information to asking for it and to asking how they might participate. It is created by held frames, hot reactions, a working prize frame and absent neediness. Signs: leaning in, questions on how to take part rather than whether to, requests for next steps or references. Pause and let them lead when you see it.

## 10. Competitive pitch decision rules

When competing against a larger, better-resourced rival:
- Choose a frame that neutralises their advantage (for example, "the best idea wins" rather than scale or polish).
- Reframe the rival's size as cost, bureaucracy or distance from the community, without disparaging.
- Use three market forces, a legacy-level big idea, vivid visuals matching the audience's own interests, and one real human voice affected by the project.
- Keep the presentation short; a long, polished, analysis-heavy deck tends to induce fatigue.
- Close by inviting them to come to you rather than asking for the deal.

## 11. Application by pitch type

### Investor pitch
| Element | Use |
|---|---|
| Structure | Full STRONG sequence |
| Slides | Minimal, visual; no dense spreadsheets in the room |
| Opening | Twenty-minute time statement |
| Why now | Three forces |
| Big idea | One-minute positioning template |
| Status | Arrive on time; do not grovel; use small defiance early |
| Prize | Ask why they are the right investor |
| Intrigue | Prepared personal story |
| Close | Frame stack, then leave before being asked; no trial close |

### Bank loan presentation
| Element | Use |
|---|---|
| Analyst risk | Credit officers are analysts; use a brief intrigue story early |
| Status | You bring an opportunity to their capital |
| Timing | Frame the request against market forces, not need alone |
| Credibility | Show budgeting discipline |
| Prize | Truthfully mention other lenders or options considered |
| Moral authority | Employment, community, local economy |
| Time frame | Real milestones |
| Neediness | Prepare an alternative facility before the meeting |
| Human element | Use imagery and stories to create interest |

### Client proposal
Establish domain expertise; three forces for acting now; prize by selectivity about timeline and commitment; high-level numbers in the meeting and detail in the written proposal; lead with the solution pattern, not a company history; intrigue through a similar hard problem solved; a single follow-up.

### Internal board or management presentation
Build situational status through expertise; treat the boardroom as a potential beta trap and take control of space and time; state a twenty-minute structure leaving discussion; redirect detail questions to the appendix; frame against external forces; present options for choice; use customer, community and legacy stories; ground in governance, community and welfare obligations; aim for questions on how to implement rather than whether.

## 12. Application to the written plan

| Section | Apply |
|---|---|
| Executive summary | Open with why-now forces; use the positioning template first; lead with a founder insight or customer problem; give one strong credential; avoid dense tables in the first two pages |
| Products and services | State the secret sauce; explain in analogy for technical features |
| Market analysis | Show movement (drivers) more than static size; add a customer story |
| Financial projections | Show budgeting skill; label assumptions conservative and sourced; explain why capital is needed at this point |
| Funding request | Lead with what the capital enables, not the amount; let the funder earn access; anchor in outcomes beyond return; close by offering an in-person presentation at their convenience |
| Risk | Tension loop: name the risk, then the mitigation; disclosing risks others hide builds credibility |

## 13. Skill-building path (seven steps)

1. Learn to recognise beta traps without engaging them.
2. Step around them in small ways ("I can wait fifteen minutes").
3. Identify and name frames in daily interactions.
4. Practise collisions with safe counterparties, with humour.
5. Use small denials with a light touch; if defensiveness appears, reset.
6. Do not force frame control; it is not theatre.
7. Practise with experienced peers and coach others.

Glossary: frame control, collision, reframing, power frame, beta trap, situational status, prize frame, intrigue frame, time frame, analyst frame, moral authority frame, push/pull, tension loop, hot and cold reaction, hookpoint, alpha and beta positions.

## 14. Uganda and East Africa calibration

- Status and respect: seniority and institutional authority carry weight. Express "small defiance" as confident expertise and calm preparation, not confrontation.
- Prize frame: works universally; a true statement that a development finance institution or another lender is reviewing the application is legitimate.
- Moral authority: community employment, smallholder incomes, youth employment and national development plan goals resonate with development lenders, government-linked investors and impact funds; use it where accurate.
- Why-now forces: candidate material includes regional trade arrangements (AfCFTA, EAC Common Market), identity and payment digitisation, GDP growth and demographic growth. Planning assumption: quote current figures only from a dated, cited source (Uganda Bureau of Statistics, Bank of Uganda, IMF, World Bank) verified at drafting.
- Beta traps: bank and government offices often involve long waits, gatekeepers and junior first reviews. Arrive early and prepared, remain courteous and composed.
- Neediness: common among SME loan applicants. Reduce it by preparing an alternative funding option, rehearsing the prize frame and using the withdrawal principle.
- Storytelling: personal narratives are culturally natural; the founder origin story or a solved community problem suits the intrigue frame.
- Finance and tax claims: verify with the Chwezi finance engine.

## 15. Core rules (quick reference)

1. Design for the receiver, not the sender.
2. Set the frame early and hold it.
3. Create wanting before full explanation.
4. Build situational status through expertise.
5. Attention needs desire and tension; plan for about twenty minutes.
6. Want nothing, be excellent, withdraw.
7. You and the deal are the prize; money is a commodity.
8. Willingness to leave is the strongest signal.
9. Novelty captures attention; tension holds it.
10. Use humour; it signals confidence and defuses tension.
