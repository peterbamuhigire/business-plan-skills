# Presentation Delivery Toolkit: Objectives, Structure, Voice, Nerves, Questions and Logistics

Task reference for the Presentation Design meta-skill. Use it as a practitioner's toolkit covering the whole life of a live presentation: setting the objective, structuring, drafting, voice and body, nerves, visual aids, questions, rehearsal, room logistics and self-development. It is deliberately broad (many small techniques rather than one model) and synthesises common public-speaking practice. Use the separate coaching protocol in this folder for the client-training procedure; the coaching phases here summarise the same ground.

## 1. What makes a good presentation

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Content | What the audience needs, paced for live absorption; not the same as a written report |
| Structure | Logical beginning, middle and end, sequenced so listeners can follow |
| Packaging | Prepared thoroughly; the audience cannot re-read |
| Human element | The presenter's personality makes the content memorable |
Decision rule: before committing, ask whether a written report would serve the audience better; if so, write the report.

## 2. Objectives first

Communication succeeds at the point of reception, not transmission: every choice (structure, language, visuals, pace) is made for the receiver.

Formulating objectives:
1. Write a single sentence of intent ("the purpose is to obtain approval for [amount and purpose]").
2. Keep to at most three objectives, one of them primary; add others only if they do not dilute it.
3. If you cannot state it in one sentence, you are not ready.

Audience aims: find out who will be present and what they want; frame the talk so achieving your objective helps them achieve theirs (a lender worried about repayment should hear coverage and collateral before the product story).

## 3. Structure models

| Model | Shape | Best for | Technique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sequential argument | Linked statements to a conclusion | Cause and effect | Frequent summary statements ("we have shown X, therefore Y") |
| Hierarchical decomposition | Topic, sub-topics, units | Complex subjects | Keep simple verbally; state each section's place; summarise at its close |
| Question-oriented (problem-solution) | Problem, background, options with advantages and disadvantages, preferred solution | Seeking approval or explaining a decision | Establish decision criteria during the talk to steer the outcome |
| Pyramid | Whole story first, then layers of added detail | Complex cases; audiences who may leave early | Builds familiarity; easily cut from the bottom when time runs short |
| Simple sandwich | Beginning, middle, end | Short or single-topic talks and every subsection | Promise, substance, payoff |

## 4. Preparing content

### 4.1 Planning questions
- [ ] Why am I presenting (explain, convince, justify, entertain)?
- [ ] Who is the audience, what do they know, which terms need explaining?
- [ ] What will they ask?
- [ ] How will I keep them interested?

### 4.2 Draft and edit
1. Write a rough draft as for a report.
2. Delete the irrelevant and superfluous.
3. Check flow and consistency.
4. Omit anything you cannot express easily; it may reflect unclear thinking.
5. Do not write a full delivery script; it leads to reading.

### 4.3 Cue cards
Use small cards (postcard or A5) with key words, phrases and sketches; number them; mark the visual aid that goes with each; avoid A4 sheets, which show trembling hands.

### 4.4 Logic tree
Main idea (the one thing to remember or do) with three branches, each answering a decision-maker's concern and carrying supporting data. Avoid the category trap of a topic outline, which produces "talking about" a subject instead of telling decision-makers what they need. After building the tree, add an arresting opening, a punchy close and relevant visuals. In delivery, if the audience agrees on a branch, skip its supporting data and move on.

### 4.5 Structure options for a 45-minute talk
At most seven main points, arranged as: timeline; climax (rising importance); problem-solution-benefits; classification; simple to complex.

## 5. The opening: five required elements

| Element | Purpose | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Get attention | Ends side conversations | Startling fact, dramatic visual, strong question |
| Establish a theme | Activates prior knowledge | State the main objective; invoke familiar context |
| Present a structure | Sets expectations | Brief overview |
| Create rapport | Win the room in the first minute | Decide beforehand how to appear (expert, peer, guide) and establish it at once |
| Administration | Prevents logistical distraction | Announce breaks, housekeeping, timing |

Attention-grabbers: a startling fact or statistic; an unexpected pertinent question; a short relevant personal story (under a minute); a dramatic visual; a timely news reference.
Avoid: untested jokes; opening with "I am nervous"; opening with "can everyone hear me at the back?".

## 6. The ending

- Plan it as carefully as the opening; the final impression is the one remembered.
- Recapture wandering attention with a change of pace, new visual or culminating idea.
- Do not announce a summary; listeners switch off. Summarise without labelling it.
- Options: an unexpected close leaving the last phrase hanging; or a crescendo of rising pace and voice to an inevitable conclusion.
- Ask for the action you want.
- "One more thing" device: hold back one critical point, appear to conclude, then deliver it. Use once per presentation, ideally mid-talk before you use it at the very end.

## 7. Voice

### 7.1 Four parameters
| Parameter | Target |
|---|---|
| Volume | Audible without shouting; lower to draw in, raise to make a point |
| Tone | Match the emotion to the content; fear in the voice frightens the audience |
| Pitch | Vary to avoid monotone; rhetorical questions naturally raise pitch |
| Pace | Slow for key points; vary to sustain interest |

### 7.2 Self-improvement
1. Listen actively to your own voice (recordings; cupping a hand behind the ear and another at the mouth approximates how others hear you).
2. Practise vocal variety by delivering one neutral sentence with surprise, irony, grief and anger; over-act in practice so variation feels natural in delivery.

### 7.3 Projection versus shouting
Projection lifts the natural voice without losing control. Aim at the furthest listener; in the first minute watch faces at the back and ask if all can hear; practise in a large room speaking to the back wall.

### 7.4 Pacing rules
The safe style is slightly louder and slower than ordinary conversation. Watch the audience and adjust. Pause at key points (silence signals importance). Replace written punctuation with pauses.

## 8. Body language

| Facet | Rules |
|---|---|
| Eyes | Most powerful signal of honesty and confidence; hold one direction about five to six seconds, then move, with a slight smile; in large rooms look at a group so individuals feel seen; if direct contact is hard, look at foreheads or noses |
| Gestures | Avoid aimless waving, fiddling with pens or coins; find a neutral rest position; gesture in unison with speech; fewer, stronger gestures; use them for active concepts (separation, merger, growth, decline); smile |
| Posture | Erect with slight forward lean signals engagement; slouching signals indifference; choose your stance deliberately; avoid pacing, standing in front of the screen, talking to visual aids |
| Face | Amplify natural expression by roughly a quarter to a third to compensate for distance and nerves; a listless face produces a listless room |
| Proximity | Moving through the room increases interaction; watch for discomfort (rocking, tapping, gaze aversion); in large rooms move towards the audience |

## 9. Managing nerves

### 9.1 Reframe
Welcome nerves; adrenaline is the mechanism that improves athletic performance. Strong performers use it as an edge; weaker ones withdraw. Nerves are less visible than they feel.

### 9.2 Physical techniques
- Floor relaxation: lie flat, knees up, relax toes, feet, legs, torso, shoulders, neck; rise slowly.
- Standing shake-out: shake hands, arms, shoulders, torso, legs; roll shoulders forward and back; rotate the head gently both ways.
- Breathing: in through the nose, hold about five seconds, out through the mouth; three to six cycles only; do it about half an hour beforehand, not at the last moment.

### 9.3 Mental preparation
- Positive visualisation over the preceding days: picture the room, calm, a clear voice. Do not rehearse failure.
- Fully memorise the first four minutes (word and gesture) to carry the highest-anxiety window.
- Hold in mind: first impressions form within seconds, so enter with a smile and open posture; you look better than you feel; the audience wants you to succeed; a mistake will not matter much.

### 9.4 During
Take a sip of water, one deep breath, relax the tightest muscle group. If you lose the thread, smile, check the cue card and take your time; the silence feels longer to you than to them. Do not use alcohol to calm nerves.

## 10. Delivery mechanics

### 10.1 Attention cycle
Attention resets roughly every two to four minutes. After drafting, place an attention device at each interval: change of inflection, new visual, a prop, moving to another part of the room, a short story, a rhetorical question, writing on a flip chart.

### 10.2 Making an impression
| Technique | Use |
|---|---|
| Repeat | State the most important point several times with different framings |
| Signpost | "The most important point is..." tells listeners when to listen |
| Picture | Metaphor and analogy |
| Narrative | Weave the message into a story |
| Short and sweet | Test: can you crystallise the message in thirty seconds for a senior executive? |

### 10.3 Transitions
Plain verbal transitions are safe but dull. Better: change position, pause, introduce a visual, pick up a prop, sharply vary volume; use one-liners, anecdotes, rhetorical questions. Recaps at section ends are welcomed.

### 10.4 Delivery checklist
- [ ] Speak clearly; judge acoustics.
- [ ] Do not read from notes.
- [ ] Vary speed and pitch at each section change.
- [ ] Pitch the voice to the back row.
- [ ] Do not face or block the screen.
- [ ] Avoid fillers.
- [ ] Keep hands visible and purposeful.

## 11. Visual aids

### 11.1 Design rules
- Text limit about ten words per slide (another rule of thumb: five words per line, five lines).
- Minimum font size: eighteen points for projected material, larger is better; a useful rule is to halve the age of the oldest person present and use that number as the minimum size.
- Prefer images to bullets.
- Avoid tables with many rows and columns (balance sheets do not project).
- Two or three colours; avoid orange and yellow text.
- Typeface and colour decisions: use the design engine (design-system-skills) for the final choice.

### 11.2 Colour associations (heuristic; check against local and sector conventions)
| Colour | Association |
|---|---|
| Dark blue | Stable, trustworthy, calm; suits finance |
| Light blue | Cool, refreshing |
| Green | Growth, health, money |
| Grey | Neutral, mature |
| White | Clean, hopeful |
| Red | Danger, urgency; use sparingly |
| Orange or yellow | Warm; poor for text |
| Black | Serious, heavy |
| Purple | Regal, mysterious |
Flip charts: black, blue and green are most visible; avoid purple, brown, pink, yellow.

### 11.3 Handling
- Keep posters and collages covered until needed.
- Hand out materials when needed or at the end (face down until directed).
- Point to the screen, not the projector surface.
- Blank the display when not discussing it; do not leave a slide up over five minutes without comment.
- Check every seat has a clear line of sight to the screen and to you.

### 11.4 Screen size guide (planning assumption; adapt to viewing distance and resolution)
| Audience | Minimum screen diagonal |
|---|---|
| 3-5 people | about 21 inches |
| 10-15 | about 37 |
| 16-35 | about 60 |
| 36-50 | about 72 |
| 51-140 | about 120 |
| 141-220 | about 150 |

## 12. Questions

### 12.1 Protocol
1. Always allow time; do not rush to conclude.
2. After inviting questions, wait at least six seconds before assuming there are none.
3. Repeat each question for the room and to confirm understanding.
4. Address the answer to the whole audience.
5. Tie the answer to the main points.
6. Aim for about ten to forty seconds.
7. If you do not know, say so, note the contact details and follow up.

### 12.2 Hostile questions
Stay calm; agree with part of the position ("I understand your concern", "I am glad you raised that"); never become combative, or the room will side with the questioner.

### 12.3 If none come
Have a seeding question ready ("a question I am often asked is..."); do not close the session in a terse tone that implies asking is inattentive.

## 13. Rehearsal

| Method | Best for |
|---|---|
| Mirror | Posture, gesture, expression (exaggerate until it feels normal) |
| Audio | Vocal variety, pace, fillers |
| Video | Most honest feedback |
| Trusted audience | Clarity, timing, impact |
| Mini-rehearsal | Difficult sections mentally in spare moments |

Preparation ratio (planning assumption): at least one hour of preparation per five minutes of speaking for an ordinary business presentation; more for senior, funding or investor presentations.
Rehearse aloud at least four times; live delivery runs roughly a quarter longer than mental rehearsal; rehearse with slides and props; finishing slightly early beats overrunning.

Breaking habits (sign method): identify the two or three worst habits through a rehearsal audience; each member writes one on a large sheet; hold up the sign the moment the habit occurs in later rehearsals; daily practice for one to two weeks.

## 14. Logistics and room management

- Arrive early; expect minor faults. Check sound, microphone distance, projector alignment, flip-chart paper and markers, seating, lighting controls, temperature, sightlines from every seat, and radio-frequency conflicts if several wireless devices are in use.
- Temperature: set slightly cool (bodies warm the room); acknowledge discomfort and invite adjustments.
- Seating: semi-circular seating improves engagement and laughter; theatre style is acceptable but reduces audience interaction; keep the front row close; set the room along the long side; avoid rows longer than about six seats; if seating cannot change, you move.
- Lighting: too much light on the screen impairs reading; total darkness sends people to sleep; know the switches and avoid constant cycling; if you stand in dim light, put a soft light on your face.
- Sound: set the level slightly loud at the check because a full room absorbs sound; assess from the back when another speaker precedes you; keep a backup microphone if amplification is essential.

## 15. Developing skill over time

- After each presentation identify the single weakest element (preparation, structure, delivery or engagement), write a reminder note and place it before you next time; fix one thing at a time.
- Oral communication is learned by practice. Ask colleagues periodically which habit to address; listen analytically to other speakers.

Seven pillars of advanced presentation:
1. Decide what matters: one core message; specialise.
2. Say what you want them to know, not everything you know; use complementary formats.
3. Drop the notes; use memorised two-minute chunks.
4. Paint pictures with stories and word pictures.
5. Involve the audience (activities from thirty seconds to fifteen minutes).
6. Use humour: learning is best in enjoyment; open with a genuine compliment; localise material.
7. Prepare, practise and participate as one cycle: collect material, work it into conversations, take every chance to speak.

## 16. Storytelling in presentations

Everyone stays for a story; it bypasses the critical filter and creates images that outlast words. Requirements: short (under a minute for an opening story), relevant (it carries the audience into the content), personal (makes the speaker a person), specific (concrete detail).
Classic structure ("say what you will say, say it, say what you said") applies to each major section as well as the whole.

## 17. Using data and evidence

- Never project a table with many rows and columns.
- Convert statistics to charts (bar, pie).
- Extract only the data you will discuss; surplus creates noise.
- Signpost evidence ("the important point here is...").
- Put dense data in a handout; show highlights on screen.
- "So what?" test: if you cannot answer it for a slide, remove the slide.

## 18. Recovering from mistakes

Continue. Most errors go unnoticed unless the presenter panics visibly. If you lose the thread, smile, check the card and take time. Do not apologise for stumbles; apologies magnify them.

## 19. Props

Benefits: warm up the audience; act as notes (anchor a section to an object); add variety; are harder to ignore; are remembered longer; serve visual learners.
Technique: keep hidden until needed; visible from everywhere; speak to the audience, not the prop; if surprising, hold it in view before revealing its effect.
Business plan uses: a product sample; a printed one-page financial summary as a hand-held prop; three objects standing for three scenarios in a risk analysis.

## 20. Audience and context analysis

- Audience: average age and background; familiarity with the subject; primary concerns; attitude to you (neutral, friendly, sceptical, hostile); expected learning or decision.
- Context: purpose of the gathering; other presenters and their topics; decisions taken immediately afterwards.
- Language and formality: technical level; formal pitch, workshop, investor meeting or board presentation.

## 21. Client coaching protocol (summary)

| Phase | Timing | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Audience and objective | Week 1 | Identify who will be in the room (credit officer, committee, grant panel, development evaluator); learn what they value; write "the objective is to [outcome] for [audience]"; agree at most three main points |
| 2 Structure and logic tree | Weeks 1-2 | Build the tree (message, three or four branches, evidence); choose the model (question-oriented for a bank loan, pyramid for investors); map each branch to a slide, with evidence for every branch |
| 3 Content editing | Week 2 | "So what?" test on every slide and datum; tables become charts; about ten words per slide; write opening and closing with care; three key-word cue cards |
| 4 Delivery coaching | Weeks 2-3 | Record the first full run without saying what will be reviewed; pick the top three habits; use the sign method; memorise the opening four minutes; rehearse the five hardest questions with ten-to-forty-second answers |
| 5 Final rehearsal | Day before | Full timed run with visuals; practise the entrance and eye contact before speaking; choose one habit to avoid; ten-minute visualisation |
| 6 Debrief | Within 48 hours | What worked (anchor it); weakest element; one improvement for next time |

## 22. Self-assessment checklist

Preparation
- [ ] Objective in one sentence.
- [ ] Audience's primary concern known and used as the frame.
- [ ] Logic tree or outline, at most seven main points.
- [ ] "So what?" applied to every slide.
- [ ] Cue cards with key words only.
- [ ] No slide above about ten words.
- [ ] Rehearsed aloud four or more times, timed within the slot.
- [ ] Five hardest questions rehearsed.
- [ ] First four minutes memorised.

Logistics
- [ ] Room and equipment confirmed.
- [ ] Arrived early; sightlines checked.
- [ ] Sound check done; light switches located.
- [ ] Temperature slightly cool.
- [ ] Backup plan for projector failure.

Delivery
- [ ] Introduced myself and my link to the topic.
- [ ] Attention gained in thirty seconds.
- [ ] Structure previewed.
- [ ] Logical sequence, clear transitions.
- [ ] Eye contact held five to six seconds per person or group.
- [ ] Pace and pitch varied at section changes; deliberate pauses.
- [ ] Hands purposeful; no reading from notes or slides; no talking to the screen.
- [ ] Watched audience body language.
- [ ] An attention device every two to four minutes.

Questions
- [ ] Repeated each question; answered to the whole room.
- [ ] Answers ten to forty seconds.
- [ ] Admitted gaps and offered follow-up.
- [ ] Stayed non-defensive.

Closing
- [ ] Summarised without announcing it.
- [ ] Call to action or memorable phrase.
- [ ] Thanked the audience; waited six seconds after inviting questions.
- [ ] Within 48 hours, identified the weakest element.

## 23. Before, during and after timeline

Before
- Two weeks: confirm room, equipment and audience size; build the logic tree; write opening and closing first; prepare answers to the three hardest questions.
- One week: complete slides and apply "so what?"; first full recorded rehearsal; pick the top three habits; begin daily mini-rehearsals.
- Day before: full-voice timed rehearsal with slides; prepare cue cards; visualise for ten minutes; confirm logistics.
- Day of: arrive at least thirty minutes before the audience; check sightlines and sound; set temperature; number handouts; shake-out and breathing; greet arrivals; drink water and avoid caffeine and dairy in the last hour.

During
- Opening (ninety seconds): confident entry and smile; eye contact before speaking; attention-grabber; theme; structure; rapport.
- Body: follow the tree, no digressions; attention device every two to four minutes; pause and change pace at section changes; read the room; point to the screen; keep any slide up no more than five minutes without discussion.
- Closing: signal the finale by pace and tone; do not announce a summary; call to action; a final memorable phrase, then stop.

After
- Immediately: take follow-up questions individually; note unanswered questions and reply within a day.
- Within 48 hours: debrief (what worked, weakest element, one improvement) and attach the note to your preparation template.
- Ongoing: seek speaking opportunities; study other speakers; review recordings periodically.

## 24. Situation quick reference

| Situation | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Funder does not know the business | Pyramid: big picture first |
| Several decision-makers with different priorities | Logic tree with one branch per concern |
| Presenting projections | Charts not tables; about ten words per slide; "so what?" |
| Nervous presenter | Memorise the first four minutes; shake-out and breathing; welcome adrenaline |
| Hostile banker's question | Repeat it; find partial agreement; ten-to-forty-second answer; stay calm |
| Single credit officer | Question-oriented structure; address their criteria explicitly; slightly louder and slower than conversation |
| Investor group | Semi-circular seating if possible; involve the audience; attention device every two to four minutes |
| Slide error mid-talk | Continue without apology |
| Filler-word habit | Sign method for one to two weeks |
| Long, complex plan | At most seven main points in 45 minutes; hierarchical decomposition; summarise each section |
