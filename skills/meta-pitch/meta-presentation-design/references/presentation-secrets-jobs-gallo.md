# Story-Led Presentation Craft: Narrative, Simplicity, Numbers, Rehearsal

Task reference for the Presentation Design meta-skill. Use it to plan the story before touching slide software, to make slides and numbers simple and memorable, to script and rehearse, and to run client coaching sessions. It synthesises well-established keynote-style presentation practice (the approach popularised by Carmine Gallo's analysis of Steve Jobs's keynotes) with classical rhetoric and general presentation guidance.

## 1. Three-phase structure

| Phase | Job |
|---|---|
| Create the story | Headline, purpose, road map, problem ("antagonist"), solution ("hero"), planned high point |
| Deliver the experience | Simple visuals, meaningful numbers, demonstrations, props, shared stage |
| Refine and rehearse | Presence, voice, memorisation, appearance, recovery from mishaps |

## 2. Phase 1: create the story

### 2.1 Plan on paper first
- Spend most preparation time thinking, sketching and scripting before opening slide software. Planning assumption: professional designers report tens of hours for a one-hour deck, only about a third of it building slides; use it as a scale, not a rule.
- Three-step storyboard method (Atkinson, Beyond Bullet Points): write the scenes; sketch the slides; produce them in software.
- Avoid default bullet layouts; they push towards text-heavy decks. Slides complement the story; the speaker tells it.
- Napkin test: if the core idea cannot be sketched simply, it needs more work.

### 2.2 Classical persuasion sequence (five steps)
1. A story or statement that arouses interest.
2. A problem or question to solve.
3. The solution.
4. Specific benefits of adopting it.
5. A call to action.

### 2.3 Nine planning elements (complete before building slides)
1. Headline: one big idea in about 140 characters or fewer, subject-verb-object.
2. Passion statement: why you are excited ("I am excited about this because...").
3. Three key messages recallable without notes.
4. Metaphors and analogies.
5. Demonstrations.
6. Partners sharing the stage.
7. Customer evidence and third-party endorsements (word of mouth is the strongest influence).
8. Short video clips (two to three minutes).
9. Flip charts, props and show-and-tell for visual, auditory and kinesthetic learners.

### 2.4 The question every audience asks: "why should I care?"
Answer within the first minute. Do not open with specifications, company history or an agenda. Identify what you are really selling: not the product, but the improved life it enables (a better outcome, security, time, freedom). Business-plan rule: never open with the registration number, the organisation chart or a product list; open with the customer's daily problem in their language.

### 2.5 Purpose and passion
Conviction is contagious. Identify your genuine motivation and write a one-sentence passion statement; it will outlast the mission statement in the listener's memory. Aim at where the market is going, not only where it has been.

### 2.6 Headline (one-line description)
Write it during planning, before the deck, the press release or the marketing material, and repeat it consistently across channels. Three criteria: concise (tweet length), specific, and stating a personal benefit. Compare "a new storage device" with a statement of what the customer can now carry or do.
Investor test: if you cannot describe what you do in about ten words, many investors will disengage. Rehearse the ten-word version until automatic. Build the entire deck around the headline; it is about the audience's better future, not about you.

### 2.7 Road map and the rule of three
Tell the audience at the start what to expect ("three things today"). Use verbal signposts (first, second, third) and make each point bigger than the last. Short-term memory holds only a few items (Miller's classic estimate of seven, plus or minus two has been revised by later research towards three or four); three is easier to remember and more persuasive than five. Newspaper structure is a useful template: headline, introduction, three key points, conclusion.

### 2.8 The antagonist (problem)
Establish the problem before the solution, so the audience shares the frustration and a "space" exists for the answer. An investor's rule of thumb: a solution without a set-up problem is a pot of coffee without a cup.
The antagonist may be a competitor, an inefficiency, a customer frustration, a regulatory or social problem.
Elevator-pitch questions: what do you do; what problem do you solve; how are you different; why should I care.
Rules: make the problem tangible ("build the pain"); one rhetorical question can introduce it; people care about solving their problem, not about your product.

### 2.9 The hero (solution)
Introduce it after the problem is fully established. It must solve the problem in plain, jargon-free language, offer tangible benefits (not specifications), and aim to improve lives, not merely defeat the antagonist. Translate specifications into benefit statements; if one villain-and-hero contrast keeps recurring, it can carry a whole campaign.

### 2.10 Ten-minute rule
Attention drifts after about ten minutes of one mode. Change medium roughly every ten minutes: demonstration, video, guest speaker, prop, sample. In a thirty-minute pitch plan three segments with a change of sensory experience between them.

## 3. Phase 2: deliver the experience

### 3.1 Simplicity in slides
- Eliminate bullets, multiple fonts and inconsistent styling, text the speaker reads, and more than one idea per slide.
- Images are far more memorable than words (picture superiority effect); paint a picture rather than a paragraph.
- Slide headlines state conclusions, not topic labels.
- Refer audiences to a website or handout for specifications.
- Plain English: write so the audience understands and can act at first hearing (replace "should you require further particulars we will be pleased to furnish them" with "if you have questions, call").
- If a slide needs explanation, redesign it.
- Test: could the typical competitor slide (many specifications in several sizes) be replaced by one image and a four-word claim?

### 3.2 Dress up numbers
Never state a number without context the audience already understands.
Three criteria: specific (a count, not "thousands"), relevant (to their concerns), contextual (compared with something familiar).
Techniques:
- Convert a capacity to a concrete quantity (storage to number of items).
- Convert a total to a rate ("per day", "per household").
- Compare a share to a familiar benchmark.
- Use a scale analogy (a small population compared with a large one; a length compared with going round the globe).
- Use a physical demonstration (pull out or hold up the thing the number describes).
Coaching rule for technical numbers: state the figure, translate it into an analogy, then state what it means to the listener.

### 3.3 Plain, vivid language
Use direct, vivid, jargon-free words, chosen and scripted in advance, so that memorable phrases feel spontaneous and land as intended.

### 3.4 Share the stage
Include employees, partners, customers and endorsers: on stage, on video, or as quoted reviews. A reference is good; a customer or partner physically present is better. Thank the team publicly. Word-of-mouth and third-party endorsement overcome scepticism better than any slide.

### 3.5 Props and demonstrations
Hand objects to the audience; let them handle a sample in silence. Physical demonstration conveys what a specification cannot (a product that fits a pocket).

Demonstration criteria (Kawasaki):
1. Short.
2. Simple: one or two key messages.
3. Sweet: the hottest, most differentiating features (imagine the audience asking "so what?" after each).
4. Swift: no step longer than about fifteen seconds.
5. Substantial: solves a real problem.
A strong demo is under about two minutes, compares directly with the alternative, builds drama with silence and resolves a pain the audience has felt.

### 3.6 The planned "wow" moment
Design at least one moment that makes the audience react: a surprising reveal, a live demonstration, a unexpected number or object. Rules: earn it with the narrative before it; silence before the reveal matters as much as the reveal; make it the single most memorable moment; save the biggest announcement for last, and keep a signature "one more thing" only if it still surprises.

## 4. Phase 3: refine and rehearse

### 4.1 Presence
Non-verbal cues and voice carry most of the impression; words matter less (the frequently quoted percentages from Mehrabian's research apply to feelings and attitudes, not to all communication; treat them cautiously).

Body language:
- Eye contact: glance at the slide and return to the audience; associated with honesty and confidence; sustained by rehearsal and visual slides.
- Open posture: no crossed arms; do not hide behind a lectern; sit or stand so nothing blocks the view of the audience.
- Gestures: use more, not fewer, gestures that match the words; they aid clarity of thought.
- Failure pattern: hands in pockets, monotone, reading note cards word for word, no eye contact.

Voice:
- Inflection: raise or lower pitch on key words.
- Pauses: before the headline and before key reveals; script less than the time allows so there is room for pauses.
- Volume: drop it to build anticipation, raise it for the peak.
- Rate: normal for demonstration, slower for headlines and key messages.
Plan a vocal arc across the introduction of the main points, rising to the loudest peak at the central announcement.

### 4.2 Make it look effortless
Informality comes from long rehearsal. Rehearse every slide transition, demo step, pause and prop moment. Treat the keynote as a competitive asset. Minimal notes suffice when the script is internalised (a short list of demo steps can guide several minutes). Practice is what makes one good.

### 4.3 Costume
Appearance signals which tribe you belong to and what the audience can expect. Dress one level above the audience's norm, not two: formal for banks, professional for development funders, matching or slightly above the client for client meetings.

### 4.4 Toss the script: five-step memorisation
1. Write the script in full sentences in the notes field: complete ideas, at most four or five sentences per slide.
2. Underline the key word in each sentence and rehearse from those words.
3. Delete everything except key words; rehearse again.
4. Fix one key idea per slide ("what is the one thing the audience should take from this slide?"); the visual becomes the prompt.
5. Rehearse the whole presentation without notes, slides as the only prompt (about four passes per slide by now).
Notes may stay available for peace of mind, but never read whole sentences; keep notes on a lectern and do not stand behind it.

### 4.5 Enjoy it
Treat the presentation as informative entertainment. Planned, warm humour helps; unscripted humour in a bank meeting is high risk.

Recovery when things go wrong:
- Acknowledge briefly, without apology; smile and continue.
- Do not draw attention to a problem the audience has not noticed.
- Ask calmly for technical help if needed; fill a pause with a short relevant story.
- Return to the message; the audience forgives errors if you recover.

### 4.6 A spoken-only presentation
The same techniques work without slides: a three-part road map (three stories); the rule of three; repeated structure for emphasis (anaphora); a headline repeated three times in the close.

## 5. Slide design summary

| Principle | Rule | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| One idea | One theme, one visual | Clutter |
| Images over text | Photograph or strong visual | Bullets, text-heavy slides |
| Headlines | State conclusions | Topic labels |
| Typography | Simple, large, readable (choose typefaces via the design engine) | Multiple fonts and sizes |
| Colour | High contrast, limited palette | Competing colours |
| Data | Numbers with analogies | Raw figures |
| Words | Short, vivid, plain | Jargon, specifications |
| Slide count | As few as needed | More slides as a sign of thoroughness |

## 6. Preparation schedule

| Phase | Activity | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Research | Facts, market data, customer stories | Largest share of the total effort |
| Organise | Headline, problem, solution, road map, wow moment | Before software |
| Sketch | Slide flow and props on paper or whiteboard | Before software |
| Script | Full sentences per slide in notes | Iterative |
| Build | Minimal text, strong visuals | Last third |
| Rehearse with notes | Sentences to key words | Four passes per slide |
| Rehearse without notes | Slides as the only prompt | Until conversational |
| Technical run | Demos, props, video, transitions | Full run-through |

## 7. Application by audience

### 7.1 Investor pitch deck
| Technique | Use |
|---|---|
| Headline | One-line description of the business, including its distinctive position |
| Antagonist | Problem slide: vivid pain in numbers (spoilage, losses, inefficiency) |
| Hero | One clear visual, jargon-free, outcome-focused |
| Ten-word test | State what you do in ten words or fewer |
| Rule of three | Three reasons the investment succeeds; three market drivers; three defensible advantages |
| Numbers | Convert the market size into a comparison and show your share of the problem as the revenue target (using sourced figures) |
| Wow moment | Live demo, customer video or physical prototype |
| Share the stage | Customer, supplier endorsement or media quote |
| Road map | "Three things today: the problem, our solution, the returns" |

### 7.2 Bank loan presentation
| Technique | Use |
|---|---|
| Why should I care | Banks care about repayment: open with coverage ratio and collateral, not vision |
| Headline | "This loan generates [annual revenue], serviced by monthly cash flow of [amount]" (figures from the model) |
| Numbers | Ratios, comparisons and benchmarks the officer recognises, not raw tables |
| Antagonist and hero | The operational constraint limiting cash flow, and the asset the loan buys and how it changes the numbers |
| Evidence | Customer reference letters, supplier contracts, purchase orders on the table |
| Rehearsal | Rehearse every likely question; one-page summary mirrors the headline |
| Final move | Hand over a single-page summary: headline, amount, coverage ratio, collateral table, repayment schedule |
| Eye contact | Know your numbers cold |

### 7.3 Client proposal deck
Client problem in their words first; a passion statement for the specific problem; demonstrate, show examples or physical samples; one comparable-client reference; one message per slide; a call to action with start date and attached contract.

### 7.4 Board or governance presentation
Rule of three (three priorities, three risks, three decisions); ratios and trends, not tables; road map ("twenty minutes and three decisions"); slow down on key numbers and pause before the recommendation; one anchoring exhibit.

## 8. Business plan deck mapping

| Slide | Primary technique | Secondary |
|---|---|---|
| Cover and opening | One-line headline | Passion statement |
| Problem or market gap | Antagonist: vivid pain | Arouse interest, pose the problem |
| Solution | Hero: benefits, not specifications | Demonstration or prop |
| Market size | Dressed-up numbers | Three segments or drivers |
| Business model | One simple diagram | One-sentence explanation |
| Traction and evidence | Shared stage: customer quotes, media, data | Third-party endorsement |
| Team | Founder passion; three team strengths | Physical presence |
| Financial projections | Ratios not tables; coverage ratio | Industry benchmarks |
| Funding ask | Call to action: amount, use, return, timeline | Strong close |
| Closing | Repeat the headline; end on the mission | Memorable final line |

## 9. Client coaching protocol (first-time presenters)

Mindset: the client is not presenting a plan; they are telling a story about a problem that matters and why this solution deserves backing.

| Session | Length | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Headline | 30 min | Describe the business in one sentence as if texting a friend outside the industry; refine to under about fifteen words with a specific benefit; if impossible, the case is not yet clear |
| 2 Problem | 45 min | Who suffers, and what does a bad day look like for them; write it in the customer's language; test with a loan officer who reads it cold |
| 3 Numbers | 45 min | List every number; give each a familiar comparison; practise saying each with its context |
| 4 Physical delivery | 60 min | Record on a phone; review for reading, eye contact, crossed arms, monotone; fix the worst habit first; rehearse the opening minute |
| 5 Wow moment | as needed | Ask what they want discussed at dinner that night; build it in; rehearse the minute before and after |

Five rules for the day:
1. Know the first three sentences by heart.
2. Slow down and pause after the most important number.
3. Never apologise for slides, nerves or preparation.
4. If something fails, smile, acknowledge briefly and move on.
5. End by repeating the headline and stating the ask.

## 10. East Africa calibration

- Eye contact: sustained eye contact with a senior figure may feel presumptuous in some settings. Aim for warm, composed, respectful directness; total avoidance signals nervousness. Calibrate with a mirror or a trusted colleague.
- Passion: understatement to appear humble reads as uncertainty to investors. "I am excited about this because..." signals conviction, not boasting.
- Humour: use planned, warm, self-deprecating humour only; avoid jokes about competitors, the bank or regulators.
- Highest-value technique for bank and development-funder pitches: dressing up numbers. Find a vivid comparison (a building, a salary, a distance, a familiar quantity); make the figure specific and contextual; link it to the audience's own frame (a banker's coverage ratios, a development funder's employment multipliers, an impact investor's beneficiary counts). Example structure: sector-wide loss stated with a dated source, compared with a familiar public asset, then your share of that loss as the revenue target. Planning assumption: every figure comes from a dated, cited source or the plan's model; do not quote illustrative figures.
