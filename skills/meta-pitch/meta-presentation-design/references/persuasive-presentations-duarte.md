# Persuasive Presentation Design: Narrative Contrast, Audience Resistance and Slide Standards

Task reference for the Presentation Design meta-skill. Use it to build the narrative of a funding or sales deck, to analyse audience resistance, to design slides and data visuals, and to plan the whole persuasion timeline (before, during, after). It applies the "what is versus what could be" narrative model and visual grammar associated with Nancy Duarte, together with general slide-design and rhetoric practice.

## 1. Narrative model: alternate "what is" and "what could be"

Persuasive presentations create productive tension by moving repeatedly between the present reality (status quo, problem) and an improved future (opportunity, solution), rising to a final elevated future state. Draw it as a rising zig-zag line.

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| Beginning | Describe the audience's current world so they nod in recognition, then introduce what could be; the gap is why they care. Never propose the future before establishing the present |
| Middle | Alternate several times (typically three to five cycles): each "what is" makes the status quo less tolerable; each "what could be" makes the future more attractive |
| End | Stay at the elevated future ("new bliss"); do not fall back to the problem, and do not end on a list of actions |

It is not a single problem-then-solution turn, a flat fact list with a recommendation, or a data dump with a call to action appended.

Why it works: stories feature transformation; conflict earns engagement; contrast is cognitively compelling.

### Template (fill from the plan; illustrative structure only)
- What is: the current economics or constraint, with a sourced figure.
- What could be: the specific change the loan or investment enables, with the modelled result.
- Repeat for three further contrasts, for example loss or waste today versus the loss avoided; job or income insecurity today versus stability; import dependence or market gap today versus the market opened.
- New bliss: the business in three years (revenue, jobs, certifications, markets, repayment complete) as stated in the plan.
Planning assumption: every figure placed in the template comes from the plan's own model or a dated cited source; do not reuse illustrative numbers.

## 2. The big idea (central message)

Write one complete sentence containing a point of view and what is at stake, with the audience's role as subject where possible and an action verb.

| Topic only | Big idea |
|---|---|
| "Our quarterly financials" | "Your department will miss its targets unless we upgrade the workflow this quarter" |
| "The sector" | "We can multiply revenue without adding to the herd by processing our own output" |
| "Market expansion" | "The regional market is open; we have a limited window before a better-funded competitor takes the shelf space" |

People move away from pain and towards reward. Name the discomfort of staying put, then offer the reward for moving.

## 3. Audience analysis

### 3.1 Audience as hero
Position the audience as the hero and yourself as the guide: give them an insight, teach them a tool or approach, and help them past an obstacle.

### 3.2 Segmentation dimensions
| Dimension | Analyse |
|---|---|
| Politics | Who decides; influence; process |
| Demographics | Age, education, geography |
| Psychographics | Values, attitudes, personality |
| Firmographics | Organisation size, sector, locations |
| Ethnographics | Social and cultural needs (critical cross-culturally) |
Write mainly for the single most influential person, without alienating the rest.

### 3.3 Audience research questions
- [ ] What are they like (a day in their life)?
- [ ] Why are they here (willing or obliged)?
- [ ] What worries them most?
- [ ] How can you solve it?
- [ ] What do you want them to do?
- [ ] How might they resist (logical, emotional, practical)?
- [ ] How best to reach them (format, room, pre-reading)?

### 3.4 Transformation arc
Map the audience's current belief or behaviour to the desired one. Bank example: sceptical about repayment capacity becomes confident and ready to approve with conditions; uncertain about collateral becomes satisfied with cover; worried about management depth becomes reassured by named advisers.

### 3.5 Resistance types and responses
- Logical: they hold contrary data. Research the opposing view, acknowledge it, and show why your evidence outweighs it.
- Emotional: the idea offends a bias or value. Acknowledge the feeling directly.
- Practical: the change is hard or costly for them. Acknowledge the sacrifice, share the burden, show a manageable path.
- Show that you have considered opposing views; it invites the same openness in return.
- Pre-sell to the toughest critic privately before the meeting.

## 4. Resistance map by audience

### Bank or commercial lender (logical and practical)
Fears: default, collateral shortfall, weak management, optimistic projections.

| Resistance | Response |
|---|---|
| Projections too optimistic | Show conservative, base and optimistic scenarios; anchor the base case to cited benchmarks; do not over-explain |
| Similar businesses have failed | Acknowledge it; show how the structure differs |
| Collateral does not fully cover the loan | Show the full collateral schedule and coverage ratio against the lender's threshold (planning assumption: thresholds vary; obtain the lender's current one); offer additional security |
| Management too thin | Name advisers; supply CVs; show succession for key roles |
| Cash flow assumes constant growth | Present a stress test, for example survival under a stated revenue decline |
Tone: analytical first, emotional second; the story supports the numbers.

### Equity investor or venture capital (logical and emotional)
Fears: founder cannot scale; market too small; weak moat.

| Resistance | Response |
|---|---|
| Market too niche | Show total, serviceable and obtainable market with cited sources and a route to the larger market |
| A larger player could copy it | Name the moat (IP, relationships, first-mover, network effects, tacit knowledge; see the Teece business-model reference) |
| Doubts about the founder | Tell the founder story; show grit, domain expertise, coachability |
| Valuation too high | Have comparable deals and the method ready; be willing to negotiate |
Tone: balanced; investors buy the founder as much as the business.

### Development finance institution or grant funder (logical and ethical)
Fears: unmeasurable impact, misused funds, no additionality.

| Resistance | Response |
|---|---|
| How is impact real? | Theory of Change; each activity tied to an outcome indicator with baseline |
| What after the grant? | Sustainability pathway to self-funding |
| Thin beneficiary data | Primary research (surveys, interviews, field data); third-party validation |
| Others do similar work | State additionality |
Tone: evidence throughout, sourced data slides, honest limits.

### Client or customer (practical and emotional)
Fears: cost, disruption, supplier failure.

| Resistance | Response |
|---|---|
| Already have a supplier | Do not attack it; show specific gaps you fill |
| Your price is higher | Shift to total cost of ownership; quality, reliability, after-sales |
| We have been burned before | Social proof; references; offer a trial or pilot |
| Needs committee approval | Give your champion a one-page summary and the arguments |
Tone: emotional first (they must like and trust you); logic closes.

## 5. Story structure

Stories work because they feature transformation, have a familiar beginning-middle-end structure, and provoke physical responses. People retell stories, not bullet points.

Story template:
| Element | Question |
|---|---|
| Beginning | When, who, where |
| Middle: context | What was the situation? |
| Middle: conflict | What tension or problem existed? |
| Middle: proposed resolution | What action was taken? |
| Middle: complication | What made it difficult? |
| End: resolution | What happened and what changed? |
Personal stories told with conviction are strongest; showing challenge and vulnerability builds affection.

### Founder story (90-120 seconds)
Beginning: the moment you met the problem. Middle: the search for a solution, including failures and lessons. Complication: what made it harder or nearly ended it. End: how the business idea crystallised. Purpose: authenticity, domain knowledge and emotional connection before the analytical case.

### Metaphor
Develop metaphors from your own experience; avoid stock visual metaphors (handshakes, targets, racing cars); carry the metaphor as a thread; a single strong metaphor early can produce the first audience reaction.

## 6. STAR moment (something they will always remember)

Include one climactic moment near the peak of the final "what could be". Four methods:
1. Shocking statistic: amplify the number with a concrete comparison (for example, spread a national figure across households to show per-household scale). A number is only as strong as the comparison that makes it real.
2. Evocative visual: connects emotionally to the beat; specific and credible, not generic stock; passes the three-second glance test.
3. Memorable dramatisation: a prototype, sample, site photograph, customer letter or printed invoice from your largest sale; physical objects break slide rhythm.
4. Emotive anecdote: genuine stakes (livelihood, failure, loss) and a specific unexpected complication, ideally with a prop and a metaphorical link to the business. Generic "I worked hard and succeeded" is not enough.

## 7. Call to action

Answer: what exactly should each person do, and by when?
Audience roles: doers (take an action), suppliers (provide funds, resources, connections), influencers (advocate), innovators (co-create or extend).
Principles: be explicit; break into discrete tasks with dates; frame in terms of the audience's benefit; close by reminding them of the future state.
Bank example structure: the specific approval sought (amount, instruments, terms); the decision date and the reason it matters (a procurement or seasonal deadline); the outcome if approved (jobs, tax contribution, alignment with the lender's mandate).

## 8. Slide design standards

### 8.1 Glance test
A slide must be understood in about three seconds, like a roadside billboard. Slides with default text volumes across a long deck cost the audience a large share of the presentation in reading rather than listening.

### 8.2 Signal-to-noise
Remove anything not supporting the current statement: logos on every slide, decorative borders, bullets that repeat speech, 3-D chart effects, gratuitous animation. Learners do better when extraneous words, graphics and sound are stripped.

### 8.3 Five arrangement principles
| Principle | Rule |
|---|---|
| Flow | Place elements so the eye moves in one sweep, left to right and top to bottom, without zig-zags |
| Contrast | Use size, shape, colour and proximity to direct attention; on data slides render everything grey except the point discussed |
| White space | Space isolates what matters and signals value |
| Hierarchy | Large, medium, small map to primary message, supporting detail, context |
| Unity | One or two typefaces; three complementary colours plus one or two neutrals; consistent image treatment |

### 8.4 Typography and colour
- Set type as large as the back row requires; one or two typefaces; size creates hierarchy; no script or decorative faces for body text. For typeface choice use the design engine (design-system-skills).
- Colour: three complementary colours plus one or two neutrals; use it to draw attention, not to decorate; strong text-to-background contrast for room legibility.

### 8.5 Slide types
| Type | Use | Common mistake |
|---|---|---|
| Walk-in slide | Sets tone before starting | Generic corporate template |
| Title | Orients the audience | Too busy |
| Navigation | Marks major sections | Overuse; needed only in talks over about half an hour |
| Bullet | Clusters related ideas | Showing all bullets at once; use builds |
| Big word | Lands a single concept | Word too small |
| Quote | Adds credibility | Long quotes (over about thirty words) or several at once |
| Data | Evidence and performance | Raw tables instead of the key insight |
| Diagram | Shows relationships | Diagram more complex than the bullets it replaced |
| Conceptual image | When showing beats telling | Generic stock photography |
| Video | Testimonials, demonstrations, animated infographics | Poor audio; slow files |
| Walk-out | Call to action as people leave | Ending on a bare "Questions?" slide |

### 8.6 One idea per slide
Slides cost nothing; give each idea its moment. Storyboard on sticky notes or thumbnails first; if it will not fit a thumbnail, it is too complex. A deck with many simple slides is perceived as shorter than one with a few dense slides.

## 9. Data slides

- Pursue clarity above all. Readers scan title, then shapes in the data, then axes.
- Highlight what matters; grey out the rest.
- Remove gridlines, borders and 3-D effects.
- Do not distort axes: truncating exaggerates small differences, stretching hides large ones.
- Find the narrative: not only what changed but why, who was affected and what it means.
- Make large numbers concrete with a scale comparison.
- Chart choice: line for change over time; bar for category comparison; pie sparingly for part-to-whole; matrix for multi-variable relationships; a single huge number when one figure is the message.
- Sometimes the best chart is no chart.

## 10. Turning bullets into diagrams

Process: read the bullet slide, circle the nouns and verbs, identify the relationship, replace with the diagram type.

| Relationship | Diagram | Business example |
|---|---|---|
| Network | Hub and spoke | Stakeholders contributing to a plant expansion |
| Segment | Donut or pie | Product lines within a portfolio |
| Join | Overlap or hook | Farmer cooperative and processor partnership |
| Flow | Parallel arrows or process flow | Two teams working towards a launch |
| Stack | Layers or building blocks | Annual targets building to profitability |

## 11. Assertion-evidence structure

Replace topic headlines with a full-sentence claim the slide proves, and show visual evidence beneath it; the speaker adds nuance. Test: if people read only the titles, do they get the argument?

| Vague | Assertion |
|---|---|
| Market overview | We are level with an aggressive rival |
| Productivity gains | Production time fell from [before] to [after] days |
| Financial projections | Revenue doubles by year three even in the conservative scenario |
| Risk analysis | Three risks are manageable; one needs immediate mitigation |
(Figures in brackets come from the plan.)

## 12. Analytical versus emotional appeal

No presentation should lack emotional content. Use pain (discomfort, urgency, concern) and pleasure (aspiration, pride, relief).
- Bank credit committees: analytical lead, with emotional texture (jobs at stake, community served, founder's motivation).
- Investors: roughly equal balance.
- Technique: ask "why" repeatedly from a logical claim until you reach the people affected; lead with that human outcome and treat the mechanism as the means.

## 13. Delivery and presence

- Authenticity: openness earns trust more than polish; audiences forgive stumbles when the speaker is sincere. Match style to content and to your real self (an explosive style and a quiet, measured one can both work).
- Body language: open posture; gestures larger than in conversation, expansive for large numbers, close hands for small gaps; face the audience, never read from the screen with your back turned; use the space, moving towards the audience on key points and back when asking a question.
- Voice: vary volume (soft draws people in), pace (slow on key points), pauses (silence instead of fillers) and pitch. Record yourself to find verbal tics.
- Q&A: prepare for every question, including hostile ones; listen for the subtext beneath a surface question; never fake an answer ("I do not have that in front of me; I will send it within a day"); with hostile questioners, acknowledge, address the wider audience and move on; after the last question, deliver a brief closing that returns to the future state.

## 14. Presenting to senior executives (credit and investment committees)

- Get to the point at once.
- Put summary slides (about a tenth of the total) first, the rest in an appendix.
- Prepare a short version inside the long one: the three things they must hear.
- Set expectations ("five minutes on findings and the recommendation, the rest for your questions").
- Rehearse with an adviser experienced with executives: is the message clear in the first minute, are summary slides skimmable?

## 15. Business plan deck mapped to the narrative model

| Slide | Content | Narrative role |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Walk-in and title | Tone |
| 2 | Hook: the problem | What is |
| 3 | The vision | What could be |
| 4 | Market: size of the problem, who suffers, inadequate current solutions | What is |
| 5 | Market: addressable share, opportunity | What could be |
| 6 | Product or service | The tool given to the hero |
| 7 | Business model | Value created and captured |
| 8 | Traction and proof | What is already working |
| 9 | STAR moment | Peak |
| 10 | Financials: current performance | What is |
| 11 | Financials: projections and return to funder | What could be |
| 12 | Risk and mitigation | Answers resistance |
| 13 | Team | Credentials of the guide |
| 14 | The ask | Explicit call to action: amount and terms |
| 15 | New bliss | Future state, return, legacy |

Rules:
1. Never open with the company overview or history; open with the problem.
2. The financial slide is evidence mid-deck, not the climax.
3. One assertion per slide.
4. Place the team late, after the vision and market give context.
5. End on the future state, not "Questions?"; let it sit before opening Q&A.

## 16. Slide transformation guide (before and after)

| Problem slide | Why it fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Bullet dump (many statistics in tiny type) | Audience reads, no message lands | Option A: assertion title with one huge number; Option B: diagram of three named segments; Option C: clean bar chart, target group in colour, others grey |
| Raw data table (multi-year profit and loss) | Unreadable at distance | Assertion title (for example, the loan is repaid by month N even in the stress case); keep only the rows and columns that prove it; highlight the key figure; put the stress case in small grey text; full table as handout or appendix |
| Text wall (executive summary pasted in) | Audience stops listening | Split into three or four claims, one slide each, each with an assertion and one visual or statistic; circulate the summary as a document |
| Generic stock photo | No emotional response | Use a real photograph of your operation, product, customers or site; for abstract concepts use bold typography or a diagram; if stock is unavoidable, choose unexpected, specific images |
| Org chart | Shows structure, not capability | Portrait cards (name, photo, two-line credential); assertion title on relevant prior experience; org chart to appendix unless a funder requires it |

Cliché alternatives: goal (bull's-eye) becomes a maze with one clear path or a threshold being crossed; partnership (handshake and globe) becomes an ecosystem or a dance pair; security (lock and key) becomes a guard animal or a defensive object; growth (seedling) becomes your own real growth chart.

## 17. Practical rules

- Length: plan content for about sixty per cent of the slot; the rest is for discussion and the unexpected. Tolerance is roughly thirty to forty minutes; beyond that, change pace (video, guest, exercise). Finishing early is forgiven; overrunning is not.
- Preparation: distilling to a few takeaways takes more work than filling an hour. Rehearse, record, take honest feedback, and be able to present without slides.
- Two endings: build a false ending (summary, usable if time is short) and a real ending (the future state), so you can finish gracefully on either.
- Slideuments: slides that make sense without a speaker are documents; circulate them before or afterwards, do not project them.

## 18. Ethos, pathos, logos

- Ethos: state the one credential that qualifies you in the first two minutes; admit what you do not know; cite credible sources; well-prepared, error-free materials show respect.
- Pathos: personal story; statistics linked to human consequences; specific, sensory, active language; the STAR moment.
- Logos: explicit assumptions behind projections; sourced market sizes; verifiable competitive claims; risk statements that acknowledge uncertainty.
- "So what?" test: for each piece of evidence, state in one sentence why it matters to this audience.

## 19. Social proof

Use customer testimonials (quoted, video, live), reference letters, press headlines (not whole articles) and third-party test results. Local examples: a letter from the largest customer confirming an ongoing order outweighs projected sales; endorsement by a relevant regulator, standards body or export-promotion agency signals legitimacy; a quote from an employee or community member humanises impact.

## 20. Persuasion timeline

| Phase | Actions |
|---|---|
| Before | Send a clear agenda with a subject line stating what the audience gains; provide pre-reading and a short biography for external audiences; pre-sell the toughest critics privately |
| During | Hand out materials at the right moment (not all at once); for public events, publish slides with supporting media and use a hashtag |
| After | Personal thank-you (handwritten when stakes are high); follow up with useful supplementary material; for a bank, confirm availability for the committee's follow-up questions; book next-step meetings at once |

## 21. Quick reference

Audience first; one-sentence big idea; alternate what is and what could be; use contrast; story with transformation; one STAR moment; three-second glance test; assertion headlines; one idea per slide; highlight one data point; replace bullets with diagrams; match delivery to your true style; rehearse until technology failure cannot derail you; end on the future state.
