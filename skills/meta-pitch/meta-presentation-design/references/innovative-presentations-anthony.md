# Audience-Adapted, Engaging Presentation Design

Task reference for the Presentation Design meta-skill. Use it to decide how ambitious the presentation should be, how to adapt to the funder or buyer in the room, how to structure opening, body and close, how to handle questions, how to rehearse, and how to package the deck and proposal. It draws on general sales-presentation and persuasion practice, including the "innovative presentation" approach of Ray Anthony and Barbara Boyd.

## 1. Quality baseline and ambition level

### 1.1 Five-quality test (apply to every presentation)
Clear (no undefined jargon); concise (never padded to fill the slot); compelling (creates a reason to act); captivating (holds attention); convincing (moves the audience to commit).

### 1.2 Two measures of success
- Effectiveness: goals met or exceeded (approval, funding, contract).
- Efficiency: more communicated, in less time. A slide of text read aloud is the least efficient form.

### 1.3 Choosing the ambition level
Select a point on a scale from restrained to bold.
- Restrained: conservative audiences (bank credit committees, regulators). Differentiate through quality, evidence and tidy packaging.
- Moderate: most funder and client meetings; a story or a short proof video is enough.
- Bold: competitive situations where a conventional pitch will not win.
The reason to innovate is better results; if innovation adds risk without benefit, tone it down.

### 1.4 Four-step innovation process
1. Set objectives: analyse audience needs, competition, constraints, risk appetite; decide how distinctive the presentation must be.
2. Fix the required characteristics (for competitive funding pitches: captivating, compelling, highly credible; for an information briefing: clear, concise, convincing).
3. Match creative devices (analogy, prop, video, demonstration, format) to those characteristics.
4. Design content, visuals, handouts and delivery for the outcome in step 1.

## 2. Audience analysis

### 2.1 Four working styles
| Style | Traits | Presenting approach | Typical in |
|---|---|---|---|
| Director | Fast, results-driven, direct | Bottom line first; brief, data-backed; no small talk | Senior executives; some investors |
| Thinker | Analytical, detail-focused, risk-averse | Full evidence with sources; pre-answer objections; state risks and mitigations | Credit officers, analysts |
| Energiser | Enthusiastic, visionary, easily distracted | Lead with vision and story; energy; vivid visuals | Many angel and venture investors |
| Affable | Warm, consensus-seeking, risk-averse | Build rapport; testimonials; people impact | Development programme officers, NGO funders |

Style labels are heuristics; verify against what you observe. Mixed rooms: scope the room beforehand, warm up informally, and include a results summary and timeline (Director), evidence (Thinker), story and vision (Energiser), and testimonials (Affable).

### 2.2 Credibility triangle
Audiences commit when they like, respect and trust the presenter. Trust comes from consistency, openness and honesty; respect from competence, preparation and track record; likeability from warmth and rapport. Winning two usually brings the third.

### 2.3 Audience research checklist
- [ ] Organisation website and tone.
- [ ] Executive biographies.
- [ ] Current priorities and pressures.
- [ ] Their familiarity with your topic (set the depth of definitions).
- [ ] Decision process, who decides, who influences.

## 3. Strategy before slides

Seven-step planning process:
1. State the target goal.
2. Analyse audience, situation, competition and constraints.
3. Confirm or revise the goal.
4. Define strategy (persuasion, psychology, finance).
5. Design a format that serves the strategy.
6. Rehearse to a polished standard.
7. Deliver and adapt in the room.
Most weak pitches over-invest in tactics (slide design) and neglect strategy (positioning against the competitor and the audience).

## 4. Content development

### 4.1 Priority order
Present the most decision-relevant information first, then supporting detail, then secondary points. Decision-makers leave early, and attention peaks at the start.

### 4.2 Three content levels for variable time
- Summary level: core message if time is cut (prepare a fifteen-minute version).
- Planned level: the intended presentation.
- Extended level: extra depth for a highly engaged audience or long questions.

### 4.3 Content types to draw on
Facts with sources; examples; third-party references; stories; statistics (rounded to be memorable, for example "five in six" rather than a decimal percentage, always sourced); expert quotations (local authorities where possible); contrasts (before and after, with and without); comparisons showing why one option is recommended; demonstrations; definitions; rhetorical questions; explanations; explicit assumptions; testimonials (problem, decision, result); analogies and metaphors.

### 4.4 Feature-advantage-benefit (FAB)
State the feature, what it does better (advantage), and what the buyer gains (benefit, in productivity, profit or reduced risk). Express benefit in the audience's terms, financial where possible.

## 5. Seven laws of communication impact

| Law | Rule | Application |
|---|---|---|
| Primacy | The first material has disproportionate weight | Craft and rehearse the first two minutes; memorise the first two sentences |
| Emphasis | Key points must stand out | Contrast, change of vocal intensity, colour, a prop or demonstration at the exact moment; one distinct element among many |
| Engagement | Active audiences learn more | Questions, volunteers, live polls, invite opinions, move among the audience |
| Interest | Relevance sustains attention | Tie every section to their situation; use sector stories; use names |
| Effect | State the cause, then describe the result | Use in the call to action |
| Recency | The last thing heard is remembered | Plan the close as carefully as the opening |
| Repetition | Reinforce key messages | Repeat in different words and media (spoken, visual, handout, demonstration); avoid over-repetition |

## 6. Openings (first ninety seconds)

### 6.1 Building blocks (combine as needed)
Greeting; theme statement; team introductions; housekeeping (goals, agenda, timing, handouts); confirmation of findings; executive summary; quotation; media reference; striking fact or statistic; question; prop; demonstration; humour; story; transition to the body.

### 6.2 Opening options by ambition level
- Restrained: a short proof video or slide showing real customer results, then move to how the same results apply to this audience.
- Moderate: a story about organisations facing the audience's challenge, stopped before the ending, resolved at the close.
- Confirm-findings opening: restate what the audience told you they need and check accuracy, then preview the specific results you will deliver.
- Bold: a theatrical device that symbolises the theme. Use only when the audience and setting can carry it, and test it beforehand.

### 6.3 Confirm-findings technique
State the needs learned in pre-meeting research and ask whether the summary is right. It shows homework, targets their real problem, invites correction before you spend thirty minutes on the wrong path, and signals a systematic professional. Particularly effective for funder and client meetings.

## 7. Body structure

- Make the deck navigable, not only sequential: executives jump back and forward. Build links to hidden backup slides (deep data, diagrams, video) to answer questions without breaking flow.
- Change activity roughly every ten minutes (slide, demonstration, prop, story, question).
- Present from different positions in the room; use audience names; use physical boards to reset attention where the group is small.
- For sceptical or analytical audiences: organise more data clearly; cite a source for every key figure; name risks and mitigations; use rounded but precise figures; follow the logic problem, evidence, solution, proof; pre-answer likely objections; hold technical detail in backup slides.

## 8. Closing

Building blocks: theme callback; quotation; challenge; summary of three to five key points at most; humour; media reference; a striking fact saved for last; thought-provoking question; multimedia finale; resolution of the opening story; call to action.

Rules:
- The call to action is specific: what you ask, of whom, by when.
- Signal the end verbally, visually (callback to the opening image) and with energy.
- Never trail off, apologise for the time or say "one last thing" repeatedly.
- Close options: repeat the theme with a twist (a memento embedding the message); leave the audience with a positive impression after a precise summary; in competitive bids, a real, deliverable added-value offer (only where commercially sound and authorised).

## 9. Visual design principles

Shift from text slides read aloud to one strong visual per idea, with the presenter's words carrying the argument.
- One idea per visual; five bullets become five visuals.
- Visual metaphors (a labyrinth for complexity, a filling suitcase for services, a growing plant for revenue).
- Infographics for data; before-and-after comparisons that make the difference vivid.
- Colour for emphasis; one highlighted element.
- Typography: clear sans-serif or serif chosen deliberately, large sizes (about twenty-four points or more for slide text). For typeface selection, use the design engine (design-system-skills).
- Real-time pointing or annotation; animated builds so the audience does not read ahead.
- Avoid: text read aloud; charts needing minutes to decode; decorative animation; generic stock images; using slides as a crutch.

## 10. Props, boards and physical media

- Multi-sensory involvement speeds and strengthens persuasion; passing a physical object, prototype or sample makes claims tangible.
- Types: static props and models; mechanical demonstrations; product prototypes; themed packaging for proposals.
- Physical boards for groups under about twenty-five: large foam-core boards, tri-fold boards, storyboard panels revealed in turn; moving to the board resets rhythm.
- Flip charts: write key numbers live; pre-sketch lightly and trace to look spontaneous; use as a transition from the projector.

## 11. Presenting to executives, buyers and mixed panels

### 11.1 Executives and committees
Wants: big picture, recommendation up front, projected return early, brevity (often thirty minutes or less), and roughly equal time for discussion. If you have thirty minutes, present for about fifteen.

Seven-part content structure:
1. Problem or opportunity.
2. Proposed solution.
3. Goals, benefits, strategy.
4. Drawbacks, risks, limits (acknowledge them).
5. Cost and return, short and long term.
6. Resources and implementation requirements.
7. Justification for proceeding now.

Behaviour rules: do not ramble or improvise; welcome interruptions and answer at once; know the material without reading slides; keep backup slides; rehearse many times the running length for high-stakes meetings. Brief, relevant stories with video, metaphors and testimonials support intuition-led decision-makers.

### 11.2 Sales and funding audiences: opportunities, results, benefits
Open with the opportunity their situation presents; show the actual results achieved; frame everything as benefit, not feature; mention competitors only to show superior value; provide overwhelming evidence that value outweighs the risk of change. Borrow from direct-response practice: demonstration, before and after, sequenced testimonials, a clear call to action, risk removal.

## 12. Questions, resistance and hostile audiences

### 12.1 Seven-step question handling
1. Welcome the question.
2. Acknowledge its importance.
3. Clarify if multi-part or vague.
4. Repeat it for the room.
5. Pause.
6. Answer as a short structured presentation (evidence, example, enumerated reasons).
7. Check acceptance ("does that answer your question?").

### 12.2 Hostile or loaded questions
Stay calm; use humour to defuse; test whether others share the concern and offer to take a narrow issue offline; rephrase into the neutral underlying question; for a legitimate challenge, acknowledge it directly and set out evidence calmly.

### 12.3 Objections
Classify the resistance (intellectual, logical, emotional); give sincere answers; pre-empt objections inside the presentation ("you may be wondering about X"); offer proof rather than argument. Do not try to wear the audience down.

## 13. Rehearsal

Practice improves general speaking skill over time (clubs, classes, recordings). Rehearsal prepares a specific presentation until polished and timed.

Progression:
1. Rehearse the opening most.
2. Run the whole piece aloud five to ten times for important presentations.
3. Work in five-minute sections, then link them.
4. Memorise the opening and the key figures.
5. Time each section (talks usually run long).
6. Reserve at least a quarter of the slot for questions.
7. Record from the second or third rehearsal and review objectively.
8. For high-stakes pitches, total rehearsal time can be many multiples of the talk length.
9. Make the last two run-throughs the best; continue if not.

Team presentations: individuals prepare their own sections first; group rehearsals follow; structured written feedback after each; record from about the third run; final run-throughs with everyone present; a dress rehearsal is mandatory; cross-train an understudy for each section.

## 14. Technology and logistics

- Tool choice: prefer tools allowing non-linear navigation, live annotation and interactive elements (zoom-based, whiteboard-style or animated-data tools) over strictly sequential slides. Tool markets change; verify current options.
- Hardware: arrive at least an hour early; bring your own equipment and a connected backup; wireless headset or lapel microphone; projector brightness suited to the room (around two thousand lumens or more for groups; a small projector or tablet for one-to-one).
- Remote delivery: visuals must be stronger; use builds; minimal text in large type; limit segments to about twenty minutes with breaks or changes of speaker; record so presenter and slides are both visible.

## 15. Proposals and handouts

- The proposal is the first physical impression of how you work. A themed, well-made proposal (quality paper, tabbed sections with a benefit statement per tab, themed packaging) signals attention to detail and client focus. Packaging cannot replace targeted content and useful illustrations; do both.
- Handout design: landscape layout with two columns, colour print on good stock, personalised with the recipient's name, supplementary detail that deepens key points, creative items tied to the theme.

## 16. Elevator pitch (thirty to sixty seconds)

Answer in order: who you are; what you do (lead with a benefit or value statement, not a job title); who your customers are; what results you deliver; a small next step ("may I call you to discuss").
Formats: value-proposition opening; a relatable-problem story; a rhetorical question.
Test: does it make the listener say "tell me more" or "so what"? If the latter, rewrite.

## 17. Business plan deck (twelve to fifteen slides)

The usual failure is a deck built for the presenter's convenience (linear, exhaustive, speaker-dependent) rather than the audience's decision process (non-linear, evidence-seeking, time-pressed).

| Slide(s) | Content and technique |
|---|---|
| 1-2 Opening | Confirm-findings opening or one visualised statistic; optional thirty-second proof video; state the summary of results up front and the time you will take |
| 3 Problem | Image, not bullets; a real person's story; before-and-after contrast |
| 4-5 Solution | Live demonstration for a product; short customer video for a service; FAB for each feature |
| 6 Market | Infographic; rounded, sourced figures (planning assumption: figures from a dated, cited source) |
| 7 Business model | One visual of money flow; unit economics (cost, price, margin per unit) |
| 8-9 Financials | Key numbers as stand-alone visuals (three-year revenue, break-even month, debt service coverage); assumptions in backup slides; current versus year-three contrast; the funding ask on one slide (amount, itemised purpose, repayment or return) |
| 10 Team | Track record and relevant experience over titles; acknowledge gaps and mitigation (advisers, partners) |
| 11 Risk | Top three risks each with a named mitigation |
| 12 Call to action | Exact ask, disbursement schedule, milestones, repayment start; what happens in the first thirty days after yes; end on the strongest evidence |
| Backup | Monthly financial model; collateral schedule; regulatory compliance status; competitor comparison; team CVs; reference letters and supply agreements |

## 18. Audience playbooks

### Bank credit officer (Thinker and Director)
- Interests: repayment certainty, collateral cover, coverage ratios, management quality; fear of an approved loan going bad or of incomplete files.
- Approach: executive summary with key financials; confirm you understand their lending criteria; lead with evidence (trading history, statements, contracts) before projections; name weaknesses with mitigations; data-heavy backup slides; restrained creativity (a well-designed proposal and one-page summary); give about half the time to questions; close with a specific ask and a simple repayment schedule.

### Angel or venture investor (Energiser and Director)
- Interests: size of opportunity, founder quality, outsized return; fear of missing a winner or backing a weak operator.
- Approach: open with the vision of success; bold, image-led slides; demonstration or customer video before financials; market size and growth; returns and exit; founders as central characters; leave them wanting more at each stage; ask stated with amount and valuation basis.

### Development finance institution or donor officer (Affable and Thinker)
- Interests: development impact, organisational capacity, sustainability, mandate alignment; fear of public project failure and missed reporting.
- Approach: confirm understanding of the mandate and how the business serves it; testimonials and human stories; logical framework or theory of change in backup; demonstrate financial sustainability; state women's participation, environmental and community benefit explicitly; cite local data (national statistics bureau, sector studies); partnership framing rather than a request for support.

### Prospective client
- Interests: what problem this solves, cost, what they get; fear of the wrong decision, supplier failure, hidden costs.
- Approach: confirm-findings opening; results for similar clients; FAB for each offer element; demonstration or trial; pre-empt objections; a simple proposal with clear pricing; differentiate through packaging.

## 19. East Africa calibration

1. Restrained-to-moderate creativity is usually right. Where most pitches read tables from slides, a striking opening figure, a sixty-second customer testimonial or a live product demonstration stands out.
2. Confirm-findings works well in relationship-oriented settings: show you know the funder's mandate and criteria. Planning assumption: lender thresholds (for example minimum debt service coverage and collateral cover) vary by institution and product; obtain the current criteria from the lender.
3. The physical proposal matters: a bound, tabbed, clearly labelled document signals competence before any figure is read.
4. References and testimonials carry extra weight: a customer video or a letter from a respected business figure can outweigh several slides of projections.
5. Storytelling suits oral traditions; open with a locally grounded story before numbers.
6. Team slide: state what each person has built or run, not only qualifications.
7. Expect interruptions from credit and donor officers; treat them as engagement, keep backup slides ready.
8. Keep a fifteen-minute version for shortened meetings.
9. Cite recognised sources (for example national statistics bureau, World Bank, IFC) for market data, with dates.
10. Always close with a specific request: amount, purpose, timeline and next step.

## 20. Ten traits of a strong presenter (self-audit)

Coherence; curiosity about the audience; enthusiasm; generosity of attention; honesty about limits; humility; one fresh idea per presentation; preparedness; punctuality (finishing early is not a failure); rehearsal that makes delivery look effortless.
