# Pitch and presentation delivery standard

Parent: [Meta Presentation Design](../SKILL.md).

When to read: when preparing a live pitch, lender or credit-committee
presentation, board paper walkthrough, grant panel, tender oral or workshop
for a business or marketing plan. It covers the decision to present, goals,
audience analysis, message structure, data slides, room and logistics,
rehearsal, delivery, questions, debrief and follow-up. Visual formatting of
slides goes to the design-system engine; this file owns content and conduct.

Sources (methods paraphrased and reorganised by task): Harvard Business
Review (2014) *Presentations* (20-Minute Manager series), Harvard Business
Review Press; Kupsh, J. and Graves, P. R. (1993) *How to Create High-Impact
Business Presentations*, NTC Business Books (room, meeting and evaluation
practice); Lin, L. C. (2013) *Decode and Conquer*, Impact Interview (hostile
questions, steel-manning). Research percentages about visual learning quoted
in older presentation books are weakly sourced and are not repeated here.

## 1. Decide whether to present

Write the purpose in one short phrase (explain new data, gain buy-in, ask for
a decision, seek help, gather ideas). Then check the medium: a few people are
often better reached one-to-one; a simple message to many by email; a complex,
data-heavy case for a decision is where a presentation earns its time. Many
lenders and investors prefer a written plan first and a shorter question
session; confirm the format they want.

## 2. Set three layers of goals

1. **Objective:** one sentence beginning "I want [audience] to [change]",
   e.g. "I want the credit committee to approve a UGX 150m term loan."
2. **Outcomes:** two to four concrete results (approval in principle, agreed
   collateral structure, next meeting booked).
3. **Measure:** how you will know (a term sheet within 14 days, tasks
   completed by a date, feedback).

## 3. Analyse the audience (nine questions)

Size and who is absent (plan follow-up for absentees); roles and why the
message matters to each; what they already know and need to know; assumptions
to confirm or correct gently; their relationship with the presenter; conflicts
between attendees; the style they are used to (numbers first or story first);
whether attendance is voluntary; who holds them accountable afterwards.

Reader priorities: a bank committee weighs cash flow, debt service cover,
security and track record; an investor weighs growth, unit economics, team,
risk and exit; a grant panel weighs evidence of need, results, value for money
and sustainability; a board weighs choices, resources and owners.

For formal panels (tender orals, DFI or government committees), gather the
evaluation criteria, the time slot, adjacent presenters and any protocol
(order of introductions, titles, dress) before building the content.

## 4. Build the message

- **One core message** the audience must take away; every slide and section
  either advances it or goes.
- **Evidence for every assertion**, only the data that persuades this
  audience, and an honest reason why they should care.
- **Structure:** opening (attention, purpose, what is in it for them,
  credibility, preview) → the need or problem (tight, specific, with urgency
  and outside evidence) → the solution in their terms, sized to the problem →
  the call to action (restate problem and solution, the specific decision
  sought, commitments and assignments, what happens next).
- **Steel-man:** state the strongest argument against the recommendation and
  why you still hold it; decision-makers trust a presenter who has done this.
- **Invite contribution:** decision-makers respond better when they help shape
  the idea; ask named individuals specific questions rather than "Is everyone
  following?" Decision-makers also judge the presenter's ability to deliver,
  not only the idea (Elsbach (2003) "How to Pitch a Brilliant Idea", *Harvard
  Business Review*, as cited in the HBR guide).
- **Stories:** use a short, light-detail story to move people to act; choose
  the story type for the purpose, and keep it true to the teller, the
  audience, the moment and the mission (Denning (2004) and Guber (2007) in
  *Harvard Business Review*, as cited in the HBR guide).
- **Style awareness:** conversational style affects who is heard and credited;
  review hedging, apologies and turn-taking in rehearsal, especially in mixed
  panels (Tannen (1995), *Harvard Business Review*, as cited in the HBR guide).
- **Authentic delivery:** rehearsing gestures does not produce authenticity;
  rehearse with the intention of openness, connection, passion for the
  content and listening, and let body language follow (Morgan (2008), *Harvard
  Business Review*, as cited in the HBR guide).

## 5. Present financial and plan data

- One idea per slide; headline the conclusion ("Revenue grows 34% on product
  mix"), not the category ("Revenue").
- Show history and projection differently (for example solid versus dashed)
  and state the assumptions behind projections.
- Show the range (base, downside, upside) and keep a back-up slide with the
  stress test.
- For mixed audiences: the conclusion first, one or two anchor charts, detail
  in the appendix or handout. Point to numbers and interpret them; do not read
  them aloud.
- Keep related charts on the same scale; do not size pictograms or areas out
  of proportion to the values.
- Every figure on a slide matches the plan and model exactly; labels show
  evidence class where it matters (actual, estimate, projection).

## 6. Room, equipment and logistics

Visit the room or ask for its layout: seating and sight lines, the furthest
viewer, whether a microphone is needed, lighting (can the screen be seen
without darkening the room?), noise, heat and ventilation, power points and
the backup if power fails (common for events outside major towns: carry
printed copies and a charged laptop or offline file). Test the projector,
cables, adaptors, clicker, network log-ins and any video link before the
audience arrives. Budget time for questions; decide in advance what to cut if
the slot shrinks. Tape down cables; know the exits. Blank the screen when it is
not in use so attention returns to the speaker.

Handouts: give a one-page summary or leave-behind after the session unless the
audience needs to follow detailed figures during it; senior officials often
prefer a short summary to a slide printout.

## 7. Rehearse

Best: a live run with people like the real audience; next: video or audio
recording; least useful: a mirror. Rehearse with the real equipment and, if
possible, in the room; run end to end for timing; know the content well enough
not to read, but do not memorise word for word; remove jargon; rehearse
answers to the hardest questions, including tone. List the twenty questions
you least want to be asked and answer each in writing. Ask what the worst
failure could be and how you would recover.

## 8. Deliver

Conversational, moderate pace; deliberate pauses instead of filler words; no
hedges ("sort of"); eye contact across the room; watch for disengagement and
respond by changing pace or position, asking a specific person a specific
question, giving a concrete example or story, or restating what the point
means for them. Treat surprises and equipment failures calmly; the message
matters more than the medium.

## 9. Handle questions

Declare the format at the start (questions at the end or throughout at planned
pause points). Repeat each question for the room and answer to the whole
group. For hard questions: anticipate them; stay gracious, not defensive;
name the scope and park what is outside it; answer honestly, and when you do
not know, say so, write the question down visibly and commit to a date for the
answer. If the question rests on a wrong premise, clarify terms, say so
constructively, offer a better frame, and still answer the original question.
Close with next steps: how feedback reaches you, what will be sent, and the
next meeting.

## 10. Debrief and follow up

Within one to two hours, list content flaws (unreadable slide, missing step,
data gap) and fix master files within days. Classify delivery problems as
minor, technical (learn the tool or assign an assistant) or chronic (coaching).
Ask colleagues for specific feedback in a short one-to-one meeting. Keep every
promise made in the room, correct errors, thank key attendees, send a short
note restating the core message and agreed next steps, and book the follow-up
meetings.

## 11. Meeting discipline (workshops, board and client meetings)

Meet only when discussion or decision needs people together; send an agenda
with time boxes, owners and expected outputs; state ground rules; keep time;
record decisions and actions with owners and dates; circulate minutes within
48 hours; review the action log at the next meeting. The cost of a meeting is
roughly the attendees' hourly cost multiplied by its length; say so when a
meeting is proposed without a decision to make.

## 12. Evaluation rubric (use after rehearsals and real sessions)

Score 1–5 on each: goal and core message clear; audience fit; structure
(opening, problem, solution, call to action); evidence and accuracy (figures
match the plan); data slides readable and honest; visual clarity; delivery
(voice, pace, presence); handling of questions; time control; room and
technology readiness. Record the three highest-value fixes before the next
session.

## 13. Pre-presentation checklist

- [ ] Purpose in one phrase; live presentation confirmed as the right medium.
- [ ] Objective, two to four outcomes and success measure written.
- [ ] Nine audience questions answered; panel criteria and protocol known.
- [ ] Core message; four-part structure; steel-man prepared.
- [ ] Data slides headline conclusions and match the model.
- [ ] Room, power, equipment and backup checked; cuts planned.
- [ ] Rehearsed with a test audience; twenty hard questions answered.
- [ ] Question format decided; parking list ready.
- [ ] Follow-up note, leave-behind and next-step meeting prepared.
