# Commercial Persuasion Patterns

Use these patterns when writing persuasive business copy, sales sections, website pages, landing pages, proposals, lead magnets, investor narratives, and premium-service positioning.

## Core Sequence

High-performing commercial writing usually follows this order:

1. Reader: who this is for and what they already care about.
2. Problem: what is costing them money, time, confidence, status, or opportunity.
3. Consequence: what happens if the problem remains unresolved.
4. Promise: what changes after the offer, plan, or recommendation is adopted.
5. Mechanism: how the change happens.
6. Proof: why the reader should believe it.
7. Fit: who should act, and who should not.
8. Next step: what the reader should do now.

For formal plans and investor copy, keep the sequence restrained and evidence-led. For sales pages and promotional copy, make the sequence more explicit.

## Reader Research

Before writing, answer:

- What does this reader already know?
- What do they fear wasting: money, time, reputation, opportunity, control, or social standing?
- What alternatives are they comparing against, including doing nothing?
- What words would they use to describe the problem?
- What proof would reduce their perceived risk?
- What objection will stop them if it is not answered?

Use the reader's real buying context. A bank officer, procurement manager, founder, parent, enterprise CIO, farmer, and affluent consumer do not buy the same way.

## Differentiation Angles

Use one or more earned differentiators:

- Narrow market focus: built for a specific buyer, sector, location, or use case.
- Better process: diagnosis, planning, implementation, review, or aftercare.
- Better proof: track record, pilot, data, customer outcomes, third-party validation, or compliance readiness.
- Better economics: lower total cost, higher margin, shorter payback, reduced waste, or better risk-adjusted return.
- Better access: scarce expertise, distribution, relationships, location, supply, technology, or licences.
- Better worldview: a sharper point of view about what the market gets wrong.
- Better service experience: speed, discretion, customisation, communication, accountability, or assurance.

Do not claim difference because the business is "passionate", "quality-focused", or "customer-centric" unless the claim is proven through behaviour.

## Offer And Value Framing

For each feature, write:

`Feature -> buyer benefit -> measurable or felt outcome -> proof`

Examples of acceptable benefit types:

- More revenue or better margin.
- Less waste, delay, error, exposure, or stress.
- More confidence, control, reputation, access, or convenience.
- Better compliance, readiness, resilience, or decision quality.

Premium offers need a value frame before a price frame. Explain what the buyer gains, what risk is removed, and what ordinary alternatives fail to provide.

## Proof Stack

Use a proof stack instead of one unsupported claim:

- Mechanism proof: explain how the result is produced.
- Numerical proof: use credible figures where available.
- Social proof: testimonials, logos, referrals, repeat purchase, or adoption.
- Demonstration proof: sample output, pilot, prototype, trial, or before/after.
- Authority proof: credentials, licences, partnerships, certifications, expert background.
- Risk-control proof: warranty, SLA, escalation path, quality checks, compliance, insurance, governance.

If proof is missing, write the gap plainly and recommend what to collect.

## Objection Handling

Treat objections as decision risks, not interruptions.

Common objections:

- Price: show value, payback, alternatives, scope, and consequence of inaction.
- Trust: show proof, process, credentials, references, and risk controls.
- Timing: show why now matters, but avoid false urgency.
- Complexity: show the implementation plan, responsibilities, and support.
- Fit: state who the offer is and is not for.
- Internal approval: provide numbers, business case, comparison table, and decision summary.

Use honest qualification. Premium buyers trust copy that excludes poor-fit buyers.

## Headline And Lede Patterns

Use headlines that carry a real claim:

- Outcome for segment: "A Working-Capital Plan for Schools That Collect Fees Termly"
- Problem and consequence: "The Cash-Flow Gap That Stops Profitable Retailers From Expanding"
- Method: "A Four-Step Market Entry Plan for Regional Food Processors"
- Comparison: "Bank Loan or Investor Capital: Which Fits This Expansion?"
- Proof-led: "How a Pilot in Gulu Validated Demand Before Equipment Purchase"

Avoid cute headlines that hide the topic. Avoid generic headlines that could describe any business.

Open with the strongest useful point: a problem, result, contrast, number, named buyer situation, or decision context. Do not start with history, definitions, or "in today's market".

## Calls To Action

Every commercial artifact needs a next step matched to the reader's readiness.

- Low readiness: read guide, complete checklist, request diagnostic, join webinar.
- Medium readiness: book consultation, request quote, download proposal pack, compare options.
- High readiness: approve budget, sign term sheet, submit application, place order, schedule kickoff.

Use one primary CTA. Secondary CTAs are acceptable only when they support the same path.

## Premium Tone

Premium writing is specific, calm, and selective.

Use:

- precise claims
- qualified confidence
- clear process
- visible expertise
- proof and boundaries
- restrained urgency

Avoid:

- luxury adjectives without substance
- discount-led framing
- crowded offers
- vague "bespoke solutions"
- guarantees the business cannot support
- over-familiar or over-excited tone
