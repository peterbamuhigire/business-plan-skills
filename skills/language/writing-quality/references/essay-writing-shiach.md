# Persuasive Structure for Business Writing: Argument, Paragraphs and Transitions

Use for proposals, executive summaries, investment cases, grant narratives and any section that argues a case. Principle: relevance is everything; every paragraph, sentence and example must serve the question asked or the proposition argued.

## 1. Argument structure
- Three parts in every persuasive document (proportions vary): an opening that engages, states the position and maps the ground; body paragraphs that develop the case point by point with evidence; a conclusion that summarises, delivers judgement and leaves something to remember.
- Plan first: identify the key words of the brief (what exactly is asked, or what the funder's criteria require); jot notes; impose an order; decide which point comes first and why. In business terms, draft a short outline before writing.
- Misreading the brief is the classic failure: a document that describes the business well but does not answer the funder's criteria.

## 2. Openings
Jobs of the opening: grab attention, engage the topic specifically, map the ground (about four or five sentences).
- Avoid waffle: generalised statements that fit any topic ("The business environment is complex and competitive and many businesses face challenges"). Say something specific from the first sentence.
- Use key words from the brief in the first sentence to signal relevance; use signposts ("This proposal will focus on...").
- Test: does it address the question at once, contain real content, map what follows and avoid waffle?

## 3. Body paragraphs
- One key point per paragraph.
- Formula: key sentence (main point, usually first); development (two to four sentences of examples, evidence, illustration); closing link (an interim conclusion or pointer to the next paragraph, often signalled with "Thus" or "This shows that").
- Assertions need evidence in the following sentences; an unsupported claim is an opinion. The formula parallels state the issue, give evidence, draw the implication.

## 4. Conclusions
- Purpose: round off emphatically, deliver the judgement the body built towards, leave the reader with something to think about.
- Must not: repeat the opening, introduce new arguments, or end weakly.
- Must: signal its function ("In conclusion...", "The evidence therefore points to..."), refer back to key points in fresh language, deliver a specific judgement or recommendation, and end on a sentence of finality (in a plan, the sentence that crystallises the investment case or the ask).

## 5. Transitions and coherence
Linking devices by function: continuing (furthermore, moreover, in addition); counter-argument (however, nevertheless, on the contrary); conclusion within the body (thus, therefore, hence); evidence (for example, to illustrate); moving on (having analysed this aspect, another essential feature is); qualifying (while it can be argued that X, it is also true that Y); emphasising; summarising.
- Place a linking device in the first sentence of each new paragraph; it need not open every sentence. Mid-sentence "however" takes commas on both sides.
- Continuity test: each opening sentence should pick up a thread from the previous closing sentence; if paragraphs could be reordered without consequence, the argument lacks structure.

## 6. Clarity and precision
- Be specific: avoid waffle words (many, various, some people feel, it has been suggested) unless followed by specifics.
- Evidence over assertion: not "the market is growing" but the rate, period and named source.
- The business equivalent of close textual reference is specific data, named sources, concrete examples and dated facts.
- Concision: each sentence advances the argument; cut restatements and removable phrases; choose the shorter word.

## 7. Application to plan sections
| Technique | Where |
|---|---|
| Three-part structure | Every section: executive summary, market analysis, financial narrative |
| No waffle in the opening | Executive summary opens with the proposition |
| Key sentence first | Market, competitive and risk paragraphs |
| Evidence for assertions | Market claims, financial assumptions, risk assessments |
| Linking words | Long narrative sections (operations, implementation) |
| Conclusion delivers judgement | Funding request closes with the ask and repayment rationale |
| Final sentence with finality | Executive summary close; cover letter |
| Key words of the brief | Answer the funder's stated criteria precisely |
| One point per paragraph | Financial narrative, structured responses |
| Map the ground | Section introductions say what the section covers |

## 8. East African application
- Answer exactly what was asked: banks and development finance institutions publish criteria; underline requirements and structure the response around them.
- Avoid generic openings about the economy or population; name the business, the claim and the evidence in the first sentence of every section.
- Label estimates, cite dated sources for market data and separate proven facts from projections.
- Oral and narrative traditions often reach the conclusion last; lenders need the conclusion first, then evidence. The three-part structure bridges both.
- Grant narratives responding to criteria: opening that maps the ground, body that treats each criterion with evidence, conclusion that summarises the case.
- Formal local writing uses "furthermore" and "in addition" freely but rarely the concluding signals ("thus", "therefore", "the evidence demonstrates"); add explicit interim conclusions.
