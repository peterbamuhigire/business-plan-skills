# Article and Blog Phrase Guidance

Method source: the sentence-job idea (fact → why it matters → implication) is adapted from Debelak, D. (2006) *Perfect Phrases for Business Proposals and Business Plans*, McGraw-Hill. All patterns and examples here are original; figures are illustrative.

Boundary: full article production, SEO and publishing belong to `blog-writer`, `content-writing` and `premium-commercial-writing` (SEO and AI-search reference). This file only covers wording discipline.

## 1. What carries over from plan writing

| Plan discipline | How it adapts to articles |
|---|---|
| Power paragraph (fact → why it matters → emphasis → implication) | The opening three or four sentences of the article, and the lead sentence of each section |
| Keywords per section | The reader's own terms for the problem, used naturally in the headline, lede and subheads; never stuffed |
| Slot-templates filled with verified facts | Headline and lede patterns filled with a named place, a number with its source, and a dated event |
| Evidence labels (fact, assumption, estimate) | Attribution in the sentence: "according to [source, date]", "in our work with [n] clients", "we estimate" |
| Before→after rewrites | The self-edit pass on every paragraph before publishing |
| Red-flag phrases | The anti-AI-slop banned list plus the genre tells in section 7 below |

Articles differ from plans in three ways: the reader chooses to read (so the first two sentences must earn the third), the register may be warmer and use "you", and the ending asks for an action rather than a decision.

## 2. Headline patterns

Good headlines name the reader, the problem or the result, and a specific detail.

- **Result + reader + condition:** "How [reader type] in [place] can [result] without [cost / risk]"
- **Number + specific object:** "[n] [mistakes / checks / questions] [reader] should [act on] before [event]"
- **Dated change + consequence:** "[Rule / event, date]: what it means for [reader]"
- **Question the reader already asks:** "[Exact question in the reader's words]?"
- **Named case:** "What [anonymised or consented business] learned from [specific event]"

Before → after:
- Before (wrong): "Unlocking the Power of Digital Transformation for SMEs" → After: "Five questions a Kampala bank will ask about your cash-flow forecast"
- Before (wrong): "Navigating the Evolving Landscape of Agribusiness Finance" → After: "Why SACCO loans for dairy farmers stall at the collateral stage, and what to bring instead"
- Before (wrong): "The Ultimate Guide to Business Plans" → After: "What a credit officer reads first in your business plan, and what they skip"

## 3. Lede patterns (first two or three sentences)

Open with the fact, the scene or the problem. Never open with the state of the world.

- **Driver lede:** "[Dated change]. It is forcing [readers] to [action]. Most are responding by [costly workaround]."
- **Scene lede:** "[Named role] in [place] [specific moment with a detail]. [What it cost them]."
- **Number lede:** "[Specific figure with source]. [Why that figure matters to this reader]."
- **Contradiction lede:** "Most [readers] assume [common belief]. In [n] [plans / audits / projects] we reviewed, [what actually happened]."

Before → after:
- Before (wrong): "In today's fast-paced business world, having a solid business plan is more important than ever." → After: "A credit officer at a Kampala bank told us she decides in the first five minutes whether a plan deserves a full read. Those five minutes go on the summary and the cash-flow table (illustrative)."
- Before (wrong): "It is important to note that many businesses struggle with cash flow." → After: "A hardware wholesaler in Mbale made a profit every month last year and still missed two supplier payments. The profit was sitting in 90-day credit to contractors (composite example)."
- Before (wrong): "Let's dive into the world of marketing plans." → After: "If your marketing plan lists five channels and no targets, a lender will read it as a wish list."

## 4. Evidence phrasing in articles

- Attribute in the sentence, with a date: "According to [source] ([month year]), …"
- Distinguish your own observation from research: "In the [n] plans we prepared last year, …" is honest; "Studies show" is not.
- Label composites and anonymised cases: "(composite example drawn from several clients)", "(name changed with permission)".
- Give ranges when precision is false: "between UGX 2 and 3 million a month" rather than "UGX 2,437,000".
- Put volatile facts (rates, rules, platform features) as dated statements or as checks: "Confirm the current rate with [authority] before you file."
- Never invent a statistic to strengthen a point. If none exists, write the point as experience or argument.

## 5. Transitions that do not sound machine-made

Machine-made prose links paragraphs with stock connectors ("Furthermore", "Moreover", "Additionally") and announces what comes next. Human writers link by content: the next paragraph picks up a word, a question or a consequence from the last.

| Instead of (wrong) | Use a content link |
|---|---|
| "Furthermore, it is important to consider pricing." | "Price is where most of these plans fail." |
| "Moreover, marketing plays a key role." | "The forecast only holds if customers hear about the shop, which brings us to the marketing budget." |
| "Additionally, there are several risks to consider." | "Two things can break this model." |
| "Let's now turn to the financials." | "The bank will check all of this against one table: the monthly cash flow." |
| "In conclusion, …" | End on the action or the implication; no signpost needed |

Also vary paragraph length, allow one short sentence for emphasis, and keep em-dashes to one per paragraph at most.

## 6. Closings with a concrete next step

Close by telling the reader what to do next, how long it takes, and what they will have when they finish.

- "Before your next lender meeting, [specific action] ([time needed]). You will then have [result]."
- "Take last month's sales and [calculation]. If the figure is below [threshold], [action]."
- "If you want a second pair of eyes on [specific artefact], [contact route]." (Only where a call-to-action is appropriate; avoid pressure tactics.)

Before → after:
- Before (wrong): "In conclusion, a well-crafted business plan is a testament to your commitment and will help you achieve your goals." → After: "Before you submit, print the cash-flow table and mark the lowest month. If you cannot explain that month in one sentence, the credit officer will ask, so prepare the answer now."
- Before (wrong): "We hope this article has been helpful. Feel free to reach out!" → After: "Check your plan against the five questions above this week; each one takes less than an hour to answer from your own records."

## 7. Genre tells to remove

- Rhetorical openers ("Have you ever wondered…?") unless the question is the reader's own.
- Listicle padding: numbered points that repeat each other.
- Symmetrical "not X, but Y" constructions used for drama.
- Every section ending with a one-line moral.
- Adjective stacks and triplets used as rhythm rather than meaning.
- The words and phrases in the `anti-ai-slop` banned list.
- Advice with no place, number or example ("Businesses should focus on customers").

## 8. Worked example (illustrative)

**Headline:** "What a credit officer reads first in your business plan"

**Lede (driver pattern):** "Most SME loan applications reach a Kampala credit desk with a plan of 40 pages or more. The officers we interviewed read three parts first: the summary, the monthly cash flow and the owner's contribution. If those three do not agree, the rest is rarely read (interviews with four officers, [month year]; composite)."

**Section lead (power-paragraph pattern):** "The owner's contribution is the part applicants underplay most. Officers read it as a measure of commitment and as the first loss buffer. A plan that says 'the owner has invested significantly' tells them nothing; a plan that says 'the owner has put UGX 45 million into stock and equipment, receipts in Appendix D' answers the question before it is asked."

**Close (next-step pattern):** "Open your plan and find the sentence that states your own contribution. If it has no figure and no evidence, rewrite it before your next meeting; it takes ten minutes and removes the first question you would otherwise face."

A non-Ugandan adaptation needs only the local reader and evidence: for a Nairobi audience, name the lender type and use KES; for a Kigali audience, use RWF and the relevant institution; keep the same sentence jobs.

## 9. Pre-publish checklist

- [ ] Headline names the reader or the result and contains a specific detail.
- [ ] The first two sentences contain a fact, scene or number, not a generalisation.
- [ ] Every statistic has a source and date, or is labelled as experience, estimate or composite.
- [ ] Transitions link by content; no stock connectors in a row.
- [ ] The close gives one concrete next step.
- [ ] No banned vocabulary; British English; no unfilled `[slots]`.
