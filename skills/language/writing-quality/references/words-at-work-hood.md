# Plain-English Workplace Writing

Use for reports, letters, emails, memos and any plan section that must get a reader to act. Most failures come from applying academic or literary habits to workplace writing. The fix: plain English, reversed structure, relentless reader focus.

## 1. Core principles
- Complexity trap: longer, more formal words do not improve writing. Test: would I say this to the person sitting opposite me? If not, do not write it.
- Inverted triangle: academic writing builds from evidence to conclusion; workplace writing leads with what the reader must know or do, then explains.
- Plain-English checklist: everyday English; sentences of two lines at most; headings and lists to direct the eye; tables; technical terms explained; active voice ("we did it"); direct speech ("we will", not "the organisation shall"); generous white space (the document's body language); concise; positive ("when you send the form we will...", not "we cannot assist until you return the form").
- Every document needs a clear purpose, audience and context.

## 2. Sentence craft
One idea per sentence; long multi-idea sentences signal unclear thinking. Keep to two lines and about fifteen to twenty words on average. Prefer the active voice; use "you" and "we" rather than third-person constructions ("it is recommended..."). Avoid split infinitives only when they create ambiguity. Read aloud: if you stumble or paraphrase, rewrite.

## 3. Paragraphs
One idea each; three to four sentences in letters and memos; open with the main point; leave white space; use headings so scanners see structure.

## 4. Document structures
- Four kinds of writing: academic (show reasoning), evidentiary (tell exactly as experienced), literary (entertain), workplace (get things done). Use the workplace kind.
- Reversed-triangle architecture: (1) what the reader must know or do, stated immediately; (2) key supporting points in logical order; (3) background and detail only as needed.
- Reports: lead with an executive summary (one to three pages) because few readers read a whole report; include key information, possible impacts, conclusions or options and recommendations; tier the document (summary, main body of key points, attachments for detail). Reports fail when they inform without recommending or ignore the audience.
- Report-writing stages: why am I writing and what do I want to happen; who is the primary audience and who are stakeholders; what do I know, who else knows, where is the information; mind-map the structure and check with the manager; draft, circulate, revise; write the executive summary last (why, how, what); plan the finished product (format, copies, binding, distribution).
- Letters: two pages or fewer; the first paragraph states what the reader needs to know or do; the middle covers points, answers and questions in logical order, one issue per paragraph; move complex detail into an enclosure; close with a next step (phone, email, deadline).
- Email, seven habits: keep it simple (one topic); make the subject line relevant; limit text to one screen and attach longer material; treat it as a business document; do not assume instant reply and use the phone for urgency; manage notifications and batch-read; when upset, draft and wait ten minutes before sending.

## 5. Tone and reader focus
- Central question: what do I want my reader to know or do? Keep it visible while writing. Readers ask "Do I have to do anything?"; answer early.
- Write plainly for the specific audience, considering what they know, how much detail they need, what language they understand and how they will respond. Dense pages say "I don't want to share my content"; short paragraphs, headings and white space say "read me".
- Positive framing ("Once you sign the form we can pay you"). Avoid unexplained jargon. Use inclusive language (check gender, culture, disability and age). Consider the reading environment (one document among twenty versus a quiet meeting).

## 6. Common mistakes and fixes
| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Long multi-idea sentences | One idea, two lines |
| Passive throughout | Name who did what |
| Buried main point | First paragraph |
| Inflated formality | Ask whether you would say it aloud |
| Unnecessary words | "in consideration of the fact that" becomes "because"; "we made the decision to" becomes "we decided" |
| Unexplained jargon | Define at first use or replace |
| Dense unheaded text | Headings and shorter paragraphs |
| No white space | Margins, lists, spacing, ragged right |
| Sensitive content by email | Use an attached letter or memo; emails are permanent and shareable |
| Academic structure | Lead with the conclusion |
| Telling everyone everything | Match detail to audience |
More substitutions: "a survey of volunteers in regional locations was conducted" becomes "we surveyed regional volunteers"; "they came to the conclusion that" becomes "they concluded"; "henceforth" becomes "from now on"; "herein enclosed is" becomes "I have enclosed"; "pursuant" becomes "as we agreed"; "written notification shall be made to this office in the event that ownership has been transferred" becomes "you must notify us in writing if you transfer ownership".

## 7. Self-editing checklist
1. Have I said early what the reader must know or do? 2. Did I plan with a mind map or key-point list? 3. Are there useful headings, tables and lists? 4. One idea per sentence, and can any be shorter? 5. Right amount of information? 6. Is everything relevant and logically ordered? 7. Has a fresh reader seen the draft? 8. Does the layout invite reading? 9. Does the start orient the reader? 10. Active voice, "you" and "we"? 11. Plain language for this audience? 12. If a decision is needed, is it clearly stated? Then read aloud.

## 8. East African notes
- Oral culture: write what you would say to the person opposite.
- Over-formal habits ("herein enclosed please find", "kindly be advised that") are an antidote target: respect shows through clarity, not complexity.
- Academic training rewards building to a conclusion; lenders, investors and officials need the ask or recommendation first.
- Plans read by banks and development finance institutions are more likely to be read with clear headings, bullet lists and an executive summary; dense text signals poor preparation.
- For readers using English as a second or third language, keep sentences short and vocabulary simple; two lines is a minimum standard.
- Memos in organisations, NGOs and government: always state who must do what by when.
