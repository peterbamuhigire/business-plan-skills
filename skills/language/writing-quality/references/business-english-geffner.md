# Business English: Style, Tone and Document Conventions

Use when drafting or editing any business-plan text, cover letter, memo, email, report or proposal.

## 1. Style principles
- Sound natural, not "businesslike". Stilted phrases add nothing. Replace "As per your request, please find enclosed herewith a cheque in the amount of X" with "As you requested, I am enclosing a cheque for X".
- Four tests for every piece: complete (the reader has what is needed to act), accurate (facts and figures verified), clear (meaning immediate), concise (no padding or redundancy).
- Organise before writing: note the main point and every supporting fact, reason, explanation and appeal, then arrange logically.
- Stop when finished: do not add a paragraph to a short message, repeat thanks already given, or tack on an unrelated second message.

## 2. Grammar that matters in business documents
- Active voice by default (subject acts): "We received your order." Use passive only when the actor is unknown, unimportant or deliberately withheld.
- Parallel structure: items of equal weight take equal grammatical form in lists, headings and series ("market size, competitive analysis and pricing strategy", not "...and how we will price"). Also keep both halves of not only/but also, either/or, both/and parallel. In plan bullet lists, make every bullet the same pattern (all noun phrases, all verb phrases, or all sentences).
- Misplaced modifiers: place the modifier next to the word it modifies ("On Monday, he explained why the company was in trouble").
- Dangling modifiers: the implied subject must appear ("Having reviewed the financials, we believe the projections are conservative").
- Subject and verb agreement: with "or" or "nor", agree with the nearer subject; collective nouns (team, board, management) take singular verbs in formal writing.
- Pronouns: "I" for the signer, "we" for the company; avoid the company name or "our company" in place of "we"; do not overuse the reader's name in the body.

## 3. Document conventions
- Letter: opening states the main idea, middle develops it, close states the conclusion and any recommendation. Formats: full-blocked, blocked, semi-blocked, square-blocked, simplified (no salutation or complimentary close).
- Replace archaic formulas: "please find enclosed herewith" with "I am enclosing"; "pursuant to our conversation" with "as we discussed"; "I beg to inform you" with "I am pleased to tell you"; "the undersigned" with "I"; "in compliance with" with "as you requested". Never end with a fragment ("Hoping to hear from you"); write "I hope to hear from you soon."
- Memo: To, From, Date, Subject; direct but courteous; itemise data.
- Email: apply the four tests; keep to about one screen; put subject and required action first; never send what you would not want forwarded or printed.
- Formal report, nine components: title page; contents (prepared last); introduction (purpose, scope, method); summary; body (organised data with subheadings, charts, tables); conclusion (objective); recommendations (following from the conclusion, based on facts); appendix; bibliography. Rules: cite sources, date the report, keep a copy.
- Proposal ("a sales pitch for an idea"): define the idea (purpose, scope, limits); persuade with specific reasons and benefits in logical order; anticipate objections (cost, credentials, alternatives); explain how to proceed (action, by whom, by when). Include a terse clear title, headings, budget or cost analysis and credentials; for long proposals add summary, appendices and cover. Logic should predominate, with visible enthusiasm and a sense of urgency.

## 4. Tone
- Fit tone to relationship (colleague, senior lender, unknown regulator), purpose (request, refusal, complaint, collection) and subject; all tones should sound natural.
- Positive outlook: stay courteous and tactful even with bad news; use "please" and "thank you"; replace impersonal statements with reader-aware ones ("Thank you for your recent order" for "We have received your order").
- Deliver bad news diplomatically: attack the situation, not the person. "Because the balance on your account is now over ninety days past due, your credit rating is in jeopardy" rather than accusing the reader of refusing to pay.
- "You approach": keep the reader's interests and perspective central without avoiding "I" and "we".

## 5. Word choice
- Replace: according to our records (our records show); acknowledge receipt of (thank you for); as per (as); at this time (now); deem (believe); due to the fact that (because); enclosed please find (I am enclosing); forward (send); herewith, heretofore, hereby (omit); in accordance with (as you requested); in the amount of (for); in the event that (if); kindly (please); pursuant to (following or as agreed); said as an adjective (the or this); same as a noun (it or them); subsequent to (after); up to this writing (until now).
- Remove redundancy: at this point in time (now); cooperate together; each and every; free gift; in spite of the fact that (although); past experience; repeat again; whether or not (whether).
- Prefer strong verbs and concrete nouns: decide, not make a decision; consider, not give consideration to; order, not place an order for; analyse, not perform an analysis of; believe, not be of the opinion. "Revenue grew by 34 per cent in Year 1" beats "We achieved significant financial improvement".

## 6. Positive versus negative framing
Frame messages positively without hiding facts: "We can ship from 15 March" for "We cannot ship before 15 March"; "Please include your purchase order number so we can process your request promptly" for "You failed to include a purchase order number"; "We offer full refunds within 30 days" for "We do not offer refunds after 30 days". In plans, state risks and limits honestly, then pivot to mitigation or upside ("Although the market is competitive, the company targets an underserved niche in which the three largest players have no presence").

## 7. Revision checklist
- Style and tone: natural; stilted phrases removed; tone fits audience and purpose; reader-oriented; bad news framed diplomatically.
- Completeness and concision: all needed information; padding and repetition removed; redundancies cut; stops when finished.
- Grammar and structure: active verbs; parallel items; modifiers placed correctly; no dangling participles; agreement; pronouns used sparingly and correctly.
- Format: logical opening, middle, close; itemised lists and data; sources cited; dated; proofread for spelling, capitalisation and punctuation.

## 9. East African register
- Formal English is expected for banks, government bodies and institutions (revenue authority, central bank, city authority), but formal does not mean stilted.
- Salutation: "Dear Sir/Madam" for institutions, "Dear Mr/Ms Surname" when known. Close "Yours faithfully" after "Dear Sir/Madam" and "Yours sincerely" after a name.
- Professional titles (Dr, Eng, Prof) carry weight; use them in correspondence and management sections.
- Government and legal documents favour the passive; acceptable in regulatory-compliance sections, but executive summaries and funding requests should be active.
- The "you approach" fits local culture: show awareness of the lender's security, investor's return or funder's social objectives.
- Avoid formulaic flattery ("I humbly submit", "your esteemed organisation"); sophisticated readers see it as padding.
- Common fixes: "I beg to submit this plan for your favourable consideration" becomes "I am pleased to present the company's business plan for your consideration"; "due to the fact that" becomes "because"; "the said project will commence" becomes "the project will begin"; mixed bullet forms become parallel; passive throughout becomes active except in compliance sections.
