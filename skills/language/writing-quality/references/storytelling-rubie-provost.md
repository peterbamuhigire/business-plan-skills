# Storytelling Technique for Business Plans and Pitches

Use for executive summaries, founder narratives, problem statements, pitches and case studies. Narrative structure is a discipline for making facts memorable; it never replaces evidence.

## 1. Story structure
- Compact model: someone faces a disruption, sets a goal, devises a plan, meets opposition, has much at stake, reaches a low point, learns something important, and must make a decision that resolves the underlying need. It applies to narrative non-fiction, including plans and pitches.
- Eight elements and their business equivalents: inciting incident (the market problem or founder's moment of realisation); goal (the opportunity pursued); strategy (business model and go-to-market); opposition (competition, regulation, scepticism); stakes (size of the unsolved problem, investor return, social impact); lowest point (a setback that proves resilience, if real); revelation (the insight that makes the business uniquely qualified); decision (the call to action: invest, lend, partner).
- Start at the disruption, not the history: open with the problem that created the business, not the founding date. Describe the world as it is (the status quo), then show how the business disrupts it.

## 2. Choose the protagonist
- Founder narrative or executive summary: the founder (journey, conviction, qualification). Market problem section: the customer (frustration, gap, relief). Funding request or pitch: the business (goal, opposition, stakes).
- Do not mix protagonists within one document; focus is lost.
- Outer character (CV, qualifications) versus deep character (what the founder did under pressure, what drove them to leave employment, the problem they could not stop thinking about). Deep character persuades more than credentials.
- Treat competitors as rational actors with coherent strategies, then show specifically why their strategies leave a gap; an accurately drawn opponent makes the win more convincing.

## 3. Conflict and stakes
- Conflict is the engine: without tension a plan is a collection of facts that the reader skims. Use external conflict (competition, regulation, supply constraints), internal conflict (growth speed versus quality, debt versus equity) and philosophical conflict (the status quo the business challenges). Do not sanitise the plan to look risk-free; readers invent worse conflicts from gaps.
- Stakes: scale of the problem unaddressed, urgency (why now, what changed), and consequences of failure for entrepreneur, team and customers.
- Ticking clock: a real deadline or window (a technology, competitor or regulatory change) turns a passive proposal into a live opportunity.
- "No to yes" journeys: sceptics, a first bank refusal, early customer pushback, each converted to yes, show resilience more persuasively than a list of qualifications; explain what caused each conversion.

## 4. Show, do not tell
- Specificity: give a named person and concrete facts rather than a generalisation. Instead of "there is significant demand for affordable housing", describe a specific tenant's rent, rent increases and waiting time, then present the market data.
- A scene has a specific time and place, a character with a goal, opposition, and movement (something changes). Apply to case studies, testimonials and problem statements ("The morning I realised our bookkeeping system had failed...").
- Show versus tell by section: market problem (a named individual with concrete details versus "there is a gap"); founder (a specific moment and obstacle versus "15 years' experience"); projections (named customers, signed memoranda, unit costs versus "revenue will grow"); competition (named rivals with prices and weaknesses versus "the competition is weak").
- Description depends on viewpoint: choose whose eyes the reader looks through (customer, founder, investor) and stay consistent within a section.

## 5. Hooks
- A hook signals a specific, original focus, shows the writer knows the kind of story, and makes the reader want more; go straight in with no wind-up.
- High-concept hook: one sentence that summarises the proposition, immediately understood, surprising, exciting. Business forms: "[Unmet need] is a [market size] opportunity that [rivals] have declined to address"; "What if [group] could [benefit] without [barrier]?"; "We are building the layer that [platform] cannot provide."
- Weak opening: "This business plan presents a proposal for the establishment of a poultry processing enterprise." Hook opening: a specific import bill in the category and a stated plan to displace it within a set period (use a current sourced figure).
- Query-letter test: if the executive summary does not hook a lender in the first paragraph, the rest is read with scepticism. The summary is the query letter and must do its job in one page.

## 6. Voice and authenticity
- Corporate voice (impersonal, passive, abstract) removes people; readers invest in people, not institutions. Write with conviction and specific knowledge: replace "management possesses extensive expertise" with what a named person has done and what it taught them.
- Keep voice consistent across sections; a conversational summary, bureaucratic finance section and hyperbolic marketing section signal inconsistent preparation.
- Earned enthusiasm: passion supported by specific data, named customers and demonstrated knowledge; unearned claims such as "will revolutionise the industry" read as inexperience.

## 7. Emotional engagement
- Anchor each statistic to a human situation; the number alone is forgettable.
- Founder motivation must be believable and rooted in lived experience (a recurring problem observed over years) rather than constructed ("inspired at a conference").
- Backstory as engine: the one experience that explains why this founder cannot stop, not a biography.
- Theme coherence: operations, HR and risk sections must reinforce the central proposition (a premium health-food plan shows premium quality standards, relevant expertise and knowledge of food-safety regulation). Sections that do not reinforce the theme are noise.

## 8. Application to plan sections
| Technique | Section | How |
|---|---|---|
| Inciting incident | 01, 04 | Open with the disruption or problem |
| High-concept hook | 01 | One sentence proposition |
| Protagonist | 01 | Choose founder, customer or business and stay consistent |
| Deep character | 09 | Show conduct under pressure, not only qualifications |
| Stakes | 04, 11 | Quantify the unsolved problem and what the funder gains |
| Conflict | 06, 12 | Describe opposition accurately, then the advantage |
| Ticking clock | 11, 13 | State why now |
| Show, do not tell | 05, 03 | Named customer scenarios, unit economics, real conversations |
| Lowest point and resilience | 09 | One brief setback overcome |
| Revelation | 01, 02 | The insight competitors missed |
| Theme coherence | All | Every section supports the proposition |
| Status quo disruption | 02 | "The world was like X. Then Y changed. We exist because of Y." |
| Call to action | 11 | The decision requested and its gain |

## 9. East African application
- Specificity needs research: naming the first ten prospective customers, their workarounds and stated willingness to pay makes a plan both story-driven and bankable.
- Founder origin stories (rural-to-urban journeys, informal market experience, remittances, family obligation, farming backgrounds) are evidence of market knowledge; use them honestly and specifically, not sentimentally.
- Oral storytelling traditions transfer to the page: hook, protagonist, conflict and stakes are familiar; the task is translation into a written form.
- Face-saving culture must not remove conflict: omitting competitors, risks or challenges lowers credibility; naming conflict and showing how it is overcome is what persuades.
- Stakes often include community, family and national development; state them specifically (jobs, community, measurable social outcome), which resonates with development-finance and impact funders.

## 10. Content angles (for the blog generator)
A plan missing a protagonist; the one-sentence high-concept hook with worked examples; show-do-not-tell as market-research discipline; the origin story a plan is hiding.
