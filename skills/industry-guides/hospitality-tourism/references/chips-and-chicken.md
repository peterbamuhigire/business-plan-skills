# Chips and Fried Chicken Cafe

Use this reference when a plan covers a fixed fast-food outlet frying potato chips (from raw potatoes or frozen) and roasting or frying chicken, with optional accompaniments (savoury snacks, sausages, soft drinks). Scale ranges from a basic kiosk to a full restaurant; profitability varies sharply with scale, location and management. Figures are planning assumptions from an older regional case; verify every price, fee and yield with a dated local source.

## 1. Market and customer

Chips and chicken cafes are common across urban East Africa; the format is mature and crowded, with outlets opening and closing regularly. Customers: workers at lunch, students, residents in estates, night-shift and nightlife crowds (extended hours raise revenue). Formal fast-food chains compete in city centres; informal cafes dominate residential estates. Local variants matter: chips folded into a chapati or omelette are popular in some countries.

Success depends on foot traffic and a price point matching local purchasing power. Do the site work: count passers-by by hour, list competitors within walking distance, and check what they charge per plate and per chicken portion.

## 2. Operating model and tiers

Five tiers from basic kiosk to full restaurant. State the tier and the seating, opening hours (near 24 hours in busy areas) and staffing. Staff by station: fryer, chicken, front counter and cashier, cleaning.

## 3. Inputs and suppliers

- Potatoes (wholesale market or farm supply; seasonal price swings, so budget an off-season price spike). In Uganda, sources include south-western and eastern highland regions; sweet potato is sometimes substituted in low-income variants.
- Frozen chips: more consistent quality and less labour, higher input cost.
- Chicken from wholesale markets or slaughterhouses (check inspection and cold-chain).
- Cooking oil (a major cost and quality driver; plan oil change intervals), flour or batter, sausages, snacks, soft drinks.
- Cooking sequence: peel, slice, fry; two fryers allow simultaneous batches and shorter waits.

## 4. Equipment and capex (categories)

Fryers (single, double or locally fabricated; test welds, stainless steel quality and power consumption before buying), potato peeler (pays back through labour savings at several sacks a day), cutter, warmer, freezer, chicken rotisserie (aroma outside the shop is a marketing tool), kitchen utensils, cash register, furniture and interior, refrigerator, signboard, fire extinguisher. Capital structure for the plan: licences; equipment; opening stock; premises (deposit and first rent, renovation); working capital (about three months of salaries, utilities and miscellaneous). Budget for a basic outlet with second-hand equipment separately from a fully equipped one, and obtain current quotes for each line.

## 5. Unit economics drivers

- Chips per sack: plates per sack = sack weight / plate weight, less peeling and frying loss. A commonly observed yield is on the order of 150 plates from a 75 kg sack; measure your own.
- Break-even on chips alone: plates needed to recover the sack cost = sack cost / (plate price). Then add plates to cover oil, fuel and overheads. Example pattern from the source: at a low plate price, about 70 plates recover the sack and roughly 30 more cover oil, power and overheads, so a basic outlet needs about 100 or more plates a day before chicken revenue.
- Chicken: wholesale cost per kg / portions per kg gives cost per portion; compare with the retail portion price; add cooking fuel and wastage.
- Case pattern: a large central outlet sells many chicken pieces and several sacks of potatoes a day with high rent and dozens of staff; an estate outlet sells far less with low rent. Build both scenarios (volume x margin vs rent and payroll).
- Accompaniments raise ticket size; track attach rate.

## 6. Pricing

Set plate and portion prices from local benchmarks and target contribution; keep a sensible price ladder (plate, quarter chicken, combo, drink). Reprice with the potato season.

## 7. Regulation and licensing checks

Business permit, public health licence, signboard permit, staff medical certificates, fire safety certificate, music and performance rights licence if music is played, and waste management permit. Verify each with the city or county and national bodies (in Uganda, city authority health inspection is required; check the national tax and environmental authorities). Keep records, dispose of waste properly and hold all employee medical certificates because several agencies inspect.

## 8. Risks and controls

Potato price seasonality, oil quality and cost, hygiene and food-safety incidents, staff theft and under-recording (daily stock reconciliation, one cashier), competition, rent escalation, power outages, and location failure. Controls: supplier diversification, fixed portion sizes, daily plate and chicken reconciliation, generator or gas backup where feasible.

## 9. KPIs

Plates and chicken pieces per day; potato sacks per day; gross margin per plate and per portion; wastage; ticket size; labour cost as a percentage of sales; rent as a percentage of sales; customer count by hour.

## 10. Plan-section prompts

- Which tier and hours, and what footfall supports them?
- What are the plate and portion yields and the break-even plates per day?
- What is the fallback plan for potato price spikes?
- Which licences and inspections apply, and who holds the records?
- How will the outlet differentiate (taste, speed, accompaniments, delivery)?

Sources consulted: Crack A Business Kenya, chips and chicken guide (2017), and general fast-food operating practice. Prices, fees and capital sums were removed or relabelled as assumptions to re-verify.
