# Chapati Production: Wholesale and Street Retail

Use this reference when a plan covers flatbread (chapati) production sold through wholesale distribution, roadside retail, hawking or cook-for-hire. It is a low-capital, high-churn street-food business where location, differentiation and distribution decide survival. All figures below are planning assumptions from older regional market observation; replace them with a dated local survey and current input prices.

## 1. Market and customer

- Chapati is a staple across East Africa, eaten as a snack or a full meal. Demand is stable and broad; the mass market is price-sensitive, while mid-income customers respond to quality and distinctive variants.
- Customer segments: office and shop workers, transport workers and bus-stage crowds, schools and institutions, households buying for occasions.
- Competition is dense and loyalty is low. A new entrant must win on location or offer a product distinctive enough to make customers switch. Female vendors are often perceived as better cooks; test perception locally rather than assuming it.
- Survey method for the plan: count vendors within a fixed radius of the proposed site, record new entries and closures over the past year, and ask why vendors left (losses, better alternatives, regulation). Entry and exit are both high because there is little equipment or stock to lose; expect a customer-building period of a few weeks.

## 2. Operating models

| Model | Description | Capital profile |
|---|---|---|
| Roadside retailer | Cooks and sells from a stall, kiosk or table | Very low: flour and basic set-up |
| Mobile hawker | Buys from a producer and resells to offices, shops and drivers, possibly on commission | Minimal |
| Producer with salespeople | Produces in volume and sends commission sellers to distant locations | Low: rent and equipment |
| Cook-for-hire | Visits households or institutions and charges for labour only | Nil (customer supplies materials) |
| Group producers | Several people share bulk production for schools, institutions and events | Shared contributions |

Distribution matters most for producers: those who send salespeople out grow faster than those waiting for walk-ins.

## 3. Products and differentiation

Plain chapati versus differentiated variants (egg, pumpkin, milk, lemon) that command premiums and referrals. Bundles with an accompaniment (vegetable stew, tea) earn materially more per customer than chapati alone; measure the uplift on a pilot. In Uganda the egg-and-vegetable rolled chapati ("rolex") is a leading street food and typically sells at a large multiple of a plain chapati for a modest added cost; test the price and margin locally. Other local variants: chips omelette or sesame flatbread in Tanzania.

## 4. Inputs and suppliers

Wheat flour is the key variable cost; quality of flour and dough preparation drive product quality. Also: cooking oil or fat, salt, fuel (charcoal, gas or electric), egg and vegetable fillings if used. Track flour price volatility and set a re-pricing rule.

## 5. Equipment and set-up (categories)

Griddle or flat pan, stove or charcoal burner, rolling boards and pins, mixing basin, storage and display covers, hygienic serving items, hand-washing point, signage; for producers add a bigger cooker and transport containers; for institutional supply add packaging and delivery bags. Obtain current quotes; minimum entry can be very small for a roadside stall.

## 6. Unit economics drivers

- Revenue per day = chapatis sold x price.
- Contribution per chapati = price - flour, oil, fuel and filling cost per piece.
- Salesperson commission model: commission as a percentage of the sale price (a commonly observed range is one fifth to three tenths), paid at day end; compute margin after commission for the wholesale route.
- Waste is the main profit killer: overestimating demand leaves unsold stock that keeps poorly; blending stale with fresh damages reputation. Plan production by a demand forecast per location and time of day, with a cut-off rule.
- Break-even: fixed daily costs (rent, labour, licence amortisation) divided by daily contribution; pilot for two to six weeks in a competitive location and record daily sales to validate.

## 7. Pricing

Set price by local benchmark, ingredient cost and target contribution; wholesale discounts to hawkers should leave the hawker a margin (commonly a discount from retail), while protecting yours. Bundle prices and variant premiums should be tested.

## 8. Regulation and licensing checks

Trade licence, food-handler medical certificates, premises health inspection and food-safety standards apply. Small mobile and roadside vendors often operate informally, but a fixed, visible business faces real inspection and health risk; plan for formal compliance. Check the county or city authority, the national standards body and local hygiene rules (Kigali enforces hygiene more consistently than many cities).

## 9. Risks and controls

Low barriers to entry (many rivals), price sensitivity, flour price shocks, wastage, hygiene incidents, weather for outdoor sales, loss of a good location, staff leaving to start their own stalls. Controls: forecast-based production, cut-off times, clean presentation, commission incentives, differentiation.

## 10. KPIs

Chapatis per day by location; sell-through and wastage percentage; contribution margin per unit; sales per salesperson; repeat customers; bundle attach rate; days to break-even in a new location.

## 11. Plan-section prompts

- Which model, which locations and why (footfall evidence)?
- What is the differentiator and its tested price premium?
- What is the demand forecast and wastage assumption?
- What licences and inspections apply, and when will they be obtained?
- How will the business grow: more locations, more salespeople, institutional contracts?

Sources consulted: Crack A Business Kenya, chapati market survey (2013), and general street-food practice. Figures were removed or relabelled as assumptions to be re-verified.
