# Pork Butchery and Eatery

Use this reference when a plan covers a raw pork butchery combined with a sit-down eatery serving cooked pork with a staple and greens. Profitability is location-driven: high-footfall sites near markets, schools, offices and hospitals give the most consistent revenue. Figures are planning assumptions from an older regional case; verify prices and fees.

## 1. Market and customer

- Two customer types: home cooks buying raw pork by the kilogram, and lunch workers and passing trade buying cooked portions.
- Market days multiply revenue; note market day frequency in the site assessment.
- Religious and cultural acceptance varies: pork is widely eaten in much of Uganda and Rwanda but not in Muslim-majority areas; site selection must account for this. In Tanzania, pork is strongest in Christian-majority districts and highland towns; grilled-meat rivals are significant. In Rwanda pork is the most widely consumed red meat.
- Count competing pork eateries in the area; clusters exist near markets and financial or government centres.

## 2. Operating model and layout

- Combined butchery and eatery: butchery counter near the entrance so raw buyers do not pass through the eatery; kitchen at the rear behind a wall; hand-washing tap near the entrance.
- Scale down (a small seating area, minimal equipment) and scale up as revenue grows.
- Staffing by function: butcher, cook, waiter or waitress, cashier, cleaner.

## 3. Sourcing (legal and quality critical)

All pork sold to the public must be veterinary-inspected and stamped at a licensed slaughterhouse; unstamped pork is illegal and creates poisoning liability. Sources:
1. Licensed slaughterhouses (buy from farmers and brokers at the market).
2. Brokers who deliver to the premises at an agreed price.
3. Own animals taken to a licensed slaughterhouse for slaughter and stamping (highest margin if manageable). Buying live pigs directly earns the best margins, and experienced buyers can estimate live weight visually.
Confirm the inspection requirement and the licensed slaughterhouses near your site with the local veterinary authority.

## 4. Equipment and capex (categories)

Digital scale, knives, axes, chopping boards, hooks, aprons, cooking apparatus (large pots, stove), cooking utensils, customer plates and cups, sink or tank with a tap, chairs and tables, signboard and advertising board, fan, receipts and padlocks, cleaning items, fire extinguisher; refrigeration if held stock or a cold display is planned. Plan: licences; equipment; initial stock (kilograms x cost per kilogram); premises (deposit, first rent, renovation); three months of wages, power and miscellaneous. Obtain current quotes.

## 5. Unit economics drivers

- Raw gross margin per kg = retail price per kg - wholesale price per kg (planning at the higher end of the wholesale range). The margin must cover fuel, water, labour and waste from fat and bone that customers reject.
- Cooked portions add volume rather than large margin: the cooked premium over the raw equivalent is consumed largely by labour, fuel and inputs; net margin on cooked operations is modest (an older benchmark is around 12 percent; measure your own).
- Mix effect: overall margin depends on the ratio of raw to cooked sales, portion control and waste management.
- Some eateries serve slightly smaller portions on wide plates; disclose portion sizes honestly, since customers notice and it damages loyalty.
- Break-even: fixed costs (rent, wages, licences, utilities) / blended contribution per kilogram equivalent.

## 6. Pricing

Retail per kg by cut (lean pork commands a premium), cooked portion prices by size with staple and greens, low-end small portions in small eateries. Prices have tended to rise slowly each year; index them to your wholesale price.

## 7. Regulation and licensing checks

Business permit, public health licence, signboard permit, staff medical certificates, fire compliance, veterinary inspection and stamp on all carcasses. Country notes: Uganda (city authority trade licence, public health permit, national standards food handling, stronger enforcement in the capital than rural areas); Tanzania (food and drugs authority, business registration, slaughterhouse stamp); Rwanda (biomedical and veterinary inspection, district environmental health officers). Verify each.

## 8. Operations and controls

- Daily reconciliation of kilograms sold versus cash on hand versus staple (flour) used.
- One cashier receives all payments; waiters issue receipts; customers pay on exit.
- Fixed daily cooking quantities when the owner is absent, to prevent over-cooking and under-reporting.
- Consistent cooking quality (variance destroys loyalty), speed of service for lunch-break customers.

## 9. Risks

Kitchen theft, meat quality or spoilage, unstamped meat (legal exposure), price changes at the slaughterhouse, competition in tight clusters, disease outbreaks in pigs (for example a notifiable swine disease), cultural mismatch of the site, refrigeration failure.

## 10. KPIs

Kilograms sold per day (raw and cooked); wastage percentage; margin per kg; portions per day; average ticket; yield per carcass; days of stock; complaints.

## 11. Plan-section prompts

- Site: which markets, schools and offices support the volume, and how many competitors?
- Sourcing route and inspection compliance?
- Margin structure of raw versus cooked lines?
- Controls for theft and portioning?
- Cultural and religious fit of the location?

Sources consulted: Crack A Business Kenya, pork butchery and eatery guide (2016), and general butchery and eatery practice. Figures were relabelled as assumptions to re-verify.
