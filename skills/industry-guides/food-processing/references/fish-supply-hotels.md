# Fresh Fish Supply to Hotels and Restaurants (B2B): Plan-Section Reference

Use when the plan covers a small trading business buying fresh tilapia (and similar fish) and delivering it to mid-level hotels and restaurants. Individual suppliers, not registered companies, typically win these accounts; success rests on freshness, reliability and price.

## Market and customer
- Target: mid-level hotels and dining halls that serve fish daily. Run a short buyer survey in the target area before planning volumes: species bought, daily fish count, supplier type, delivery frequency, payment terms, willingness to switch and switching reasons.
- Patterns seen in an urban survey, to be re-tested locally: most hotels buy tilapia only; most buy modest daily volumes; most use individual suppliers; delivery is commonly twice weekly with deep-freezer storage between; payment is a mix of cash and short credit; most buyers say they would switch supplier for price and quality.
- Implication: the market is accessible but customers leave easily. Hotels usually keep two suppliers, so aim to be the preferred primary supplier through consistency, not exclusivity.
- Budget conservatively for volume per account (start from the low end of the survey range, not the high).

## Operating model
- Start with one species (tilapia); add others once supply is reliable.
- Sourcing ladder: main wholesale fish market (earliest morning purchase, usual entry point); processors of frozen product (cheaper but some hotels reject frozen); smaller city markets (top-ups); direct from the lakeside landing (best quality, higher logistics, needs refrigerated transport). Wild lake fish is generally preferred for taste and weight over pond-raised fish.
- Delivery: public transport for small volumes; motorcycle or hired vehicle once five or more accounts share a route. Use insulated boxes and ice.
- Entry sequence: sign up about three hotels in a tight cluster; buy small batches at market price; deliver twice weekly; build trust for four to six weeks; add accounts as working capital grows.

## Rules that protect the account
1. Freshness is the product.
2. Reliability beats price: a missed delivery costs the hotel revenue and costs you the account.
3. Agree credit terms in writing; never let outstanding credit run beyond one cycle unaddressed.
4. Track each account's price, volume and payment history.

## Unit economics drivers
- Gross margin per fish = delivered price minus (market purchase price + ice, transport and losses).
- Contribution per week = fish delivered x margin minus delivery cost; the main capital need is working capital to fund credit terms (days of credit x daily purchases), not equipment.
- Sensitivity: purchase-price swings by season and lake catch, spoilage rate, bad-debt rate.

## Regulation and licensing checks
Food-handler health certificate; tax identification for invoicing larger hotels; business registration as the business grows; fish-trade permits where required (Uganda: fisheries authority under the agriculture ministry; Tanzania: TFDA and fisheries authorities; Rwanda: NAEB and RDB). Confirm current requirements.

## Localisation
Species and markets differ: Nile perch and tilapia from Lake Victoria landings and Kampala wholesale markets (Uganda); Lake Victoria tilapia and sangara with Kariakoo as hub (Tanzania); Lake Kivu tilapia and Kigali markets (Rwanda). Hospitality growth raises demand for fish in formal dining.

## Risks
Supply shortages and price swings, spoilage in transit, customer credit default, easy supplier switching, regulatory tightening on fish handling.

## KPIs
Fish delivered per week, on-time delivery rate, gross margin per fish, days sales outstanding, accounts retained, spoilage percentage.

## Plan-section prompts
Which hotels and how many fish per day each (evidence)? Which sourcing channel? What credit terms and what working capital do they imply? How is freshness assured through the cold chain?
