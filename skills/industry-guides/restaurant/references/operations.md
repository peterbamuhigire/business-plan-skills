# Restaurant Operations Reference

Use this reference for the operations section of a restaurant business plan: start-up scope, front-of-house design, kitchen and equipment, purchasing and stock control, staffing, food safety, responsible alcohol service and marketing.

Equipment costs, wage rates and temperature or licensing rules vary by place and date. Treat every figure as a planning assumption; re-verify with dated local quotations and the current public-health rules before it enters a plan. Verify payroll, tips and tax treatment with the Chwezi finance engine.

## 1. Start-up scope and staging

Cost three blocks: (1) initial planning (legal and accounting help, market research, design, cost estimates); (2) construction and acquisition (fit-out, equipment, opening inventories, operating supplies); (3) pre-opening expenses and working capital (marketing, recruitment and training, clean-up, cash to meet payroll and bills until cash flow is self-sustaining).

Decision rule: open with what can be funded and earn a reputation first; upgrade in stages. Do not size the first outlet on the "dream" concept.

## 2. Front of house

### Space and layout

- Plan aisles for movement and safety: a wide main aisle from entrance through the dining room (about 54 in), narrower access aisles to service stations (about 36 in), and chair-back service aisles of about 18-24 in. Verify against local building and fire codes.
- Size service stations for roughly 18 seats each.
- Set table sizes from the expected party-size mix: seats allocated to each party size / seats per table = tables. Use expandable tables for flexibility.

### Service system

Walk the whole guest journey and assign an owner to each step: arrival and greeting; seating and drinks; order taking (POS, slips or verbal); transmission to kitchen; pick-up and service; bill and payment; departure. Use a buddy system so staff cover adjacent stations when busy.

### Service failures to design out

Late opening or early closing; impersonal greeting; dirty tables visible to waiting guests; worn menus; chipped glassware; long waits; food held under heat lamps; flat drinks or stale bread; advertised items unavailable; unfilled water glasses; marked bills; no thanks at departure.

### Take-away as a profit centre

Adds volume without major investment, attracts new customers, offsets slow table turnover and uses spare kitchen capacity. Suits pizza, Asian and increasingly upscale cuisine. Plan packaging, a separate collection point and delivery partners.

## 3. Back of house

Size the kitchen from menu complexity, production methods, expected volume, service style and storage needs. Prepare an equipment schedule with quotations for each group.

- **Kitchen:** walk-in cold room, freezers, dishwasher, fryers, ovens, ranges, extraction hood and fire suppression, grill, mixer, coffee urn, ice maker, griddle, toaster, stainless prep tables, waste disposal, reach-in refrigerators.
- **Bar:** keg refrigeration and taps, cocktail stations, bar sinks and speed racks, post-mix soda dispensing, bar refrigerators, glass froster, bottle cooler, front and back bar.
- **Dining room:** chairs, booths, tables, bar stools, screens, POS system.
- **Leasehold improvements:** HVAC, electrical, plumbing, floors and finishes.

Decide buy versus lease: leasing preserves working capital; buying builds assets. Compare total cost, tax effect (verify with the Chwezi finance engine) and equipment life.

## 4. Purchasing and stock control

### Principles

- Purchasing savings drop straight to profit: at a net margin of about 10%, each shilling lost through poor buying needs about ten shillings of extra sales to recover.
- Buy the right quantity, grade and ripeness; avoid slow-moving lines; compare several suppliers.
- Write purchase specifications (supplier, brand, size, minimum and maximum stock).

### Dead-stock causes

Over-buying; ingredients bought for dishes that did not sell; irregular stock counts; no rule forcing use of older items.

### Receiving

Count and inspect every delivery before signing; check quantity, quality and price against the specification; check chilled and frozen temperatures; reject damaged packs and swollen or dented cans.

### Storage

- Put away immediately: frozen, then chilled, then dry goods.
- Store raw below cooked, drip-prone items wrapped on lower shelves, everything off the floor.
- Keep the store locked, limit keys, authorise withdrawals and record all of them.
- Planning storage temperatures (verify locally): meat and poultry about 0-2 degrees C; fish about -1 to 1 degrees C; dairy about 3-4 degrees C; most fruit and vegetables about 4-7 degrees C, with higher humidity for produce.

### Standard recipes and portion control

Write a standard recipe for every dish (ingredients, method, yield, portion). Fix portion sizes and issue scales, numbered scoops and ladles. This makes cost and quality predictable.

### Simple inventory control

Needs three records: periodic stock counts (at least monthly), purchases, and sales. Cost of food sold = opening stock + purchases - closing stock.

### Control loop

Purchasing (specifications) -> receiving (count, inspect, price check) -> storing (secure, immediate) -> issuing (record) -> stock count (monthly) -> standard recipes -> portion control -> cashiering (every item served is accounted for).

## 5. Staffing

Build a staffing table by kitchen, dining room, bar and other, giving full-time or part-time status, weekly hours, rate and weekly pay; sum to weekly and annual payroll and express as a percentage of forecast sales. Owner-managers who take salaries must appear in the table. Rates are local and time-bound; source them from the current labour market.

### Chef (head of kitchen) responsibilities

Menu planning; costing and pricing; portion standards; competitive purchasing; cost control (labour, food, beverage, supplies, utilities); function and banquet coordination; supervising and motivating staff; cleanliness and equipment checks; rush-period control; hiring, discipline, appraisal and scheduling; back-of-house safety and security; sanitation compliance; stock counts and food cost %.

### Induction checklist

History and structure; hours and roster location; leave, overtime and holiday rules; breaks and smoking rules; pay, deductions, pay day and tip reporting; benefits; staff meals; absence and lateness; emergency call-in; probation and warnings; training plan; appraisal and advancement; bill, complaint, breakage and portion policies; dress and appearance; facility tour; dismissal offences; safety and fire procedures.

### Four-step training

1. Show the task.
2. Explain what and why.
3. Let the trainee do it under supervision.
4. Check back periodically.

### Tips

Follow the statutory reporting and allocation rules applicable to the business; confirm with the tax authority and the Chwezi finance engine.

## 6. Sanitation and food safety

- Apply HACCP: map each process from raw material to service, identify critical control points, set controls, and specify minimum cooking times and temperatures.
- Temperature planning points (verify against current local rules): hold hot food above about 60 degrees C; hold cold food at about 4 degrees C or below; wash dishes hot and sanitise at a higher temperature or with an approved chemical; hold draught beer at about 3-6 degrees C.
- Programme steps: buy from inspected suppliers; check deliveries; store correctly; train staff in hygiene and glove use; cook in small batches, minimise holding times and check temperatures each meal period; clean and sanitise on schedule.
- Fire prevention: clean extraction hoods and ducts regularly (the commonest fire source); maintain electrical equipment, switch off when idle and report defects.

## 7. Responsible alcohol service

- Train staff to recognise four stages of intoxication: relaxed inhibitions, poor judgement, impaired reactions, loss of coordination.
- Intervene by serving snacks that do not create thirst, delaying reorders, not pushing refills, offering weaker or non-alcoholic options, and staffing peaks adequately for checking age and monitoring.
- Keep an incident log, post the house policy on menus and table cards, check identification of anyone who may be underage, avoid promotions that encourage excess, offer coffee and help arrange safe transport.
- Confirm the licence conditions and legal age with the licensing authority.

## 8. Marketing and promotion

### Marketing plan steps

1. Set an objective.
2. List strengths and weaknesses.
3. List alternative strategies (expand, convert space, raise turnover).
4. Select the best.
5. Detail an action plan.
6. Implement to a timetable.
7. Evaluate against the objective.

### Market information

Demographic (age, income, occupation), geographic (where people live, work and eat) and psychographic (lifestyle, brand consciousness, peer influence).

### Budget and life cycle

Budget about 2-3% of forecast sales (assumption; re-verify). Advertising follows four stages: awareness, trial, maintenance, rejuvenation.

### Media selection

| Medium | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Radio | Targeted, drive-time audience | Audio only, not saved |
| Print | Timely, lower cost | Short life |
| Television | Sound and vision | Cost, lead time |
| Magazines | Targeted, long life | Lead time, cost |
| Outdoor | Directions, reminders | Short messages |
| Online and social | Local targeting, measurable | Needs constant activity |
| Direct mail and coupons | Personal, selective | Low response, cost |

### Menu as a sales tool

A menu informs, prices and promotes high-margin dishes. Box key items, place them in high-visibility positions, use clear descriptions and train staff in suggestive selling. Display wine to lift wine sales.

### Launch and retention

Hold rehearsal services before opening; invite media, community leaders and event planners; issue a press release; build word of mouth from day one. Retain guests through personal rapport, feedback handling, loyalty offers, theme nights for slow days, early-evening specials and a lighter late menu.

## Plan-section prompts

- Describe the layout, seat count and table mix with the reasoning.
- Attach an equipment schedule with dated quotations.
- Set out the purchasing-to-cashiering control loop and who owns each step.
- Present the staffing table and payroll as a percentage of sales.
- List licences, food-safety and alcohol controls with the authority for each.
- Give the launch plan, marketing budget and retention measures.
