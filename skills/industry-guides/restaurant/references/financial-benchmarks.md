# Restaurant Financial Structure, Benchmarks and Pricing Methods

Use this reference to build the financial section of a restaurant business plan: the income-statement template, start-up budget headings, funding structure, daily break-even, operating ratios, and menu and beverage pricing methods.

All percentages below are planning ranges, not facts about a specific market. Before they enter a plan, re-verify them against a dated local source (a comparable operator's accounts, a trade association, or a lender's sector data) and label them as assumptions. Any monetary figure must be re-costed at current prices. Verify tax and accounting treatment with the Chwezi finance engine.

## 1. Income statement template (percentage of total sales)

Build the projected income statement in this order and express every line as a percentage of total sales.

| Line | Planning range or note |
|---|---|
| Food sales and beverage sales | Forecast separately; state the food/beverage split (commonly food-led, with beverage the higher-margin minority) |
| Cost of food (as % of food sales) | About 30-40% for table service; lower if cooked from scratch, higher if using convenience foods |
| Cost of beverages (as % of beverage sales) | About 20-30% |
| Gross profit | Sales less blended cost of sales |
| Payroll and employee benefits (labour) | Largest controllable cost; total labour often around one-third of sales |
| Direct operating expenses, advertising, music and entertainment, utilities, administration, repairs and maintenance | Controllable; advertising budget commonly 2-3% of sales |
| Profit before occupancy | Controllable profit; the manager's own performance measure |
| Occupancy (rent, property taxes, property insurance) | Verify against the local lease market; state whether rent is gross or net of taxes and insurance |
| Interest, depreciation | Outside the manager's control; link to the funding and asset schedules |
| Net profit | Typically single digits to low teens as a percentage of sales for a well-run table-service operation |

Sanity-test every plan: if prime cost (cost of sales plus labour) falls outside the plausible range, or net margin is far above the sector norm, revisit the assumptions.

## 2. Start-up investment budget headings

Cover each heading; omitting one is the commonest cause of under-funding.

1. Working capital (cash to meet payroll and bills until cash flow is self-sustaining).
2. Leasehold improvements (HVAC, electrical, plumbing, floors and finishes).
3. Licences and permits.
4. Opening inventories (food, beverage, supplies).
5. Furniture, fixtures and equipment (see `operations.md` for equipment groups).
6. Opening expenses (insurance, permits, recruitment and training, launch marketing).

State each as a share of the total and show the funding mix: owners' equity, loan (amount, term, fixed or variable rate, collateral) and the resulting debt-to-equity ratio. Test debt service against the break-even sales level, not the base case.

## 3. Sales forecast build

Forecast by meal period, not as one annual number.

```
Weekly sales = customers per period x average guest check   (sum lunch, dinner, bar-only)
Annual sales = weekly sales x trading weeks
```

Calculate the average guest check per meal period and track it separately. Derive customer counts from seats x seat turnover x days open; see `restaurant-financial-basics.md`.

## 4. Daily break-even

1. Total monthly fixed costs (rent, salaried staff, utilities, insurance, taxes, depreciation).
2. Divide by trading days to get daily fixed costs.
3. Add daily variable costs (one day's food and liquor supply, additional staff essential to service).
4. Convert to the daily sales needed to break even, using the contribution-margin method in `restaurant-break-even-menu.md`.
5. Compare with the forecast sales per day and state the margin of safety.

## 5. Operating ratios and how to calculate them

| Ratio | Formula | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Food cost % | Cost of food sold / food sales | Range 30-40% for table service |
| Pouring cost % | Cost of beverages sold / beverage sales | Range about 20-30% |
| Labour cost % | (Payroll + benefits) / total sales | Varies with service style, seasonality, convenience foods and labour market |
| Net profit % | Net profit / total sales | |
| Return on investment | Net profit / total investment | |
| Current ratio | Current assets / current liabilities | Aim for roughly 2:1 or better |
| Acid-test ratio | (Cash + receivables + marketable securities) / current liabilities | About 1:1 acceptable |
| Working capital | Current assets less current liabilities | |
| Average guest check | Sales / guests served | Calculate per meal period |
| Seat turnover | Customers / seats / days in period | Dining room and bar separately |
| Labour turnover | Leavers / jobs x 100 | Track quarterly |
| Covers per employee, sales per employee, sales per hour worked | Weekly, from sales, covers, headcount and hours | Spot over-staffing in weak weeks |

Cost of food sold (periodic method): opening inventory + purchases - closing inventory, then adjust for staff meals and garnishes issued to the bar.

Customer lifetime value: weekly visits x average spend x 52 x expected years. Use it to justify retention spending; do not present it as revenue.

## 6. Balance-sheet template

Current assets (cash, bank, receivables, food, beverage and supplies inventory, marketable securities, prepaid expenses); fixed assets net of depreciation (FF&E, leasehold improvements); current liabilities (payables, taxes collected, accruals, current portion of loan); long-term loan; owners' equity. Check that total assets equal liabilities plus equity.

## 7. Menu pricing methods

Choose one primary method, cross-check with a second, and record the result as a price band.

1. **Cost-plus target method.** Food cost % + all other costs % + desired profit % = 100%. Solve for the food cost % the business can afford, then price = raw ingredient cost / food cost %. Round to a sensible price point.
2. **Markup on ingredient cost.** Price = ingredient cost / target food cost %. Derive the percentage from the actual cost structure; an arbitrary target underprices dishes.
3. **Market-bearing pricing.** Suits a unique concept with strong demand and little competition; treat as short-term and watch consumer price limits.
4. **Competition-based pricing.** Use as a cross-check; do not follow blindly, since competitors may have better purchasing power or efficiency.

Cost every plate from its full recipe, including accompaniments (bread, starch, vegetable, soup, salad); price a set-lunch special on total plate cost divided by the target cost %.

### Beverage pricing

- **Wine:** use flexible markups. Give cheaper wines a higher percentage markup and dearer wines a lower percentage that still yields the larger absolute gross profit.
- **Draught beer:** cost per ounce = keg cost / ounces per keg; cost per glass = cost per ounce x pour size (allow for head and wastage); price = glass cost / target pouring cost %; profit per keg = glasses x price - keg cost.
- **Spirits and mixed drinks:** salable ounces = bottle ounces less spillage; drinks per bottle = salable ounces / standard pour; sales yield = bottle cost / target pouring cost %; price per drink = yield / drinks per bottle; add a small allowance for garnishes and mixers.

## Plan-section prompts

- State the assumed food/beverage split, cost percentages and labour percentage with their dated sources.
- Show the start-up budget by heading and the funding mix.
- Show the year-one income statement as amounts and percentages.
- Show daily break-even and the margin of safety.
- Show the pricing method for at least three sample dishes and one beverage.
- List the ratios the owner will report monthly.
