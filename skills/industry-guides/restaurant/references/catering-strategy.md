# Catering Strategy: Adding Catering as a Revenue Channel

Task reference for the market, model, pricing, marketing and financial sections of a restaurant plan that adds catering. Staffing, menu, scaling and KPIs are in `catering-operations.md`.

Figures are illustrative planning assumptions in UGX; re-verify each against a dated local source.

## 1. Why consider catering

- Catering is a business within a business: it layers revenue on assets already paid for (premises, equipment, labour, supplier terms, brand) with few incremental costs.
- It uses idle capacity, such as early mornings and off-peak production, and needs little new capital.
- Demand is mostly corporate meetings, training events and group orders, with private gatherings secondary. Test the actual split in the target town.
- Cross-pollination: people who eat catered food become walk-in customers, and walk-in customers learn of the service.
- Independent caterers, takeaways and supermarkets often take this spend by default; the plan should identify who currently supplies the target accounts.

## 2. Business model

- B2B: the contract is with the organisation, but the buyer is the individual order-placer.
- Requires invoicing, credit accounts and receivables control, a shift from cash retail.
- Relationship selling is the core method.

## 3. Pricing

- Price catering above retail: the buyer pays for delivery, set-up, convenience, presentation and reliability, and spends a corporate budget.
- Consider upscale or healthier versions of existing items.
- Avoid discounts and coupons as the catering incentive; use sampling instead.
- Price by platter with size bands; recommend the right quantity and never undersell.
- Formula: platter price = (ingredient cost + packaging + delivery share + labour share) / target cost percentage, then compare with local competitors.
- Margin logic to model: catering can supply a larger share of gross margin than of sales because of premium prices, bulk production and leveraged assets. Compute it from the plan's own numbers.

## 4. Order workflow

1. Pre-production the day before: portion, slice and measure next-day materials; prepare shelf-stable items; date-label; counter staff prepare drinks and boxes.
2. Morning production: start before opening; concentrate on assembly and packaging, not cooking from scratch; produce by item in bulk, then pack per order against packing slips.
3. Distribution timing: work back from the delivery time (breakfast orders ready early; lunch orders loaded in time for on-time arrival).
4. Delivery management: route plan the day before; vehicle safety check each shift; labels, packing slips and invoices with each driver; own drivers, third parties or both.
5. Collection point: keep a separate, marked catering pick-up area from takeaway.

Principle: run catering as a high-volume production line, not made to order.

Food safety: control temperature from production to delivery; plan refrigeration and holding times; cold programmes are easier than hot. Verify licence and hygiene requirements with the local authority.

## 5. Marketing and sales method

1. Qualify likely corporate buyers.
2. Telephone to introduce the service.
3. Offer a free sampling delivery (for example a platter for five) in off-peak hours, with full branded packaging and materials.
4. Follow up repeatedly; keep prospects on task-based follow-up.
5. In the outlet: make catering highly visible on walls, tables and menu boards; a board of the day's delivery clients builds awareness; train all frontline staff to route catering enquiries.
6. Assets: business cards, a consistent sampling package, branded packaging, catering-specific print separate from retail.
7. Channels: cold calling, referrals, walk-in enquiries, telephone campaigns, staff as brand ambassadors, and relationship targets such as sales representatives in pharmaceutical and similar sectors.
8. Segments: corporate meetings, training courses, informal group lunches, boardroom orders, tour groups, private events.

## 6. Financial framing

Build the projection from drivers: orders per day x average order value x trading days x weeks, minus incremental cost. Show conservative, target and stretch cases. Track order size in value and headcount, menu mix (a small share of items produces most revenue), dormant clients, lead conversion time, service performance and complaints, order frequency and rotation position.

Sources consulted: Dardick, E., *Get Catering and Grow Sales!* (2011).
