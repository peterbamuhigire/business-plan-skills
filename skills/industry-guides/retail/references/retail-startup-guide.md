# Retail Startup Guide

Use this reference to plan the opening of a shop: readiness, structure, finance, premises, cost by format, fittings, records and tax, insurance, buying an existing business, mistakes, and the pre-opening timeline. All currency amounts are Ugandan-shilling planning assumptions; re-verify each with a dated local quotation. Tax, statutory rates and thresholds change: verify with the Chwezi finance engine and the tax authority before use.

## 1. Owner readiness

Traits that predict success: self-discipline (long weeks), people skills, numeracy (margin, cash flow, break-even, VAT), resilience, and organisation (stock, rotas, records, tax deadlines).

Screening questions:
1. Can I decide carefully under stress?
2. Will I give up evenings, weekends and holidays, especially in the first two years?
3. Does my family understand the time and money demands?
4. Do I have, or can I obtain, marketing, accounts and personnel skills?
5. Have I identified a gap in the market this shop fills?

Prior industry experience materially improves survival odds; the source research cited a large gap between experienced and inexperienced founders. Treat any survival percentage as dated and verify it.

## 2. Steps to start

1. Define the idea: what, to whom, and why customers choose you.
2. Research the market: demographics, foot traffic, competitors, price expectations.
3. Choose the legal structure.
4. Write the business plan: summary, business analysis, marketing, management, and projections (12-month cash flow, profit and loss, balance sheet).
5. Secure finance.
6. Secure premises.
7. Fit out: fixtures, signage, till or POS.
8. Source stock and negotiate supplier credit.
9. Hire and train staff.
10. Register for tax promptly after starting (revenue authority).
11. Insure.
12. Open, launch marketing and monitor cash weekly.

## 3. Legal structure and naming

| Structure | Features | Liability | Registration (Uganda) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Simplest and cheapest; owner keeps profit and bears debt | Unlimited personal | Business name with the registry; trading licence; tax identification number |
| Partnership | Shared capital, profit and debt; written deed covering contributions, profit ratios, exit | Unlimited for ordinary partners; limited for limited partners | Partnership deed with the registry; each partner registers for tax |
| Limited company | Separate legal entity; more compliance (annual returns, audited accounts) | Limited to share value | Incorporate with the registry; tax, VAT and PAYE registration |

Naming: easy to pronounce, distinctive, says what you sell; check availability with the registry; add a tagline if needed; consider a trade mark.

## 4. Financing

Plan enough finance to cover bills and stock for at least the first two years; many failures stem from working-capital shortage, not a poor idea.

| Source | Notes |
|---|---|
| Personal savings | Full control, no interest |
| Family and friends | Put repayment terms or equity share in writing |
| Bank loans | Small retail is high-risk to banks; expect a strong plan, forecast and collateral; typical terms one to five years |
| Overdraft | For temporary cash gaps and running costs only |
| Microfinance | More accessible, shorter cycles, higher rates |
| SACCOs | Member savings history needed; lower rates than microfinance |
| Angel investors | Equity plus mentoring; expect a demanding return target |
| Grants and schemes | Rarely cover full cost; need detailed plans |

Amount ranges by source vary widely and date quickly; source current ranges from the lender.

Prepare before approaching funders: 12-month cash-flow forecast; balance-sheet forecast; concise plan; personal budget (owner drawings); identification and tax certificate. Lenders look for a sound plan, financial controls, personal commitment and a researched opportunity.

## 5. Premises

Location factors: foot traffic (count at different times); visibility from the road; accessibility (transport stages, parking, delivery access); neighbouring businesses that draw traffic; competition (avoid clustering for convenience goods, cluster for specialty goods); demographic match; safety.

Lease checklist: length (short for flexibility, long for security); payment timing and any rent-free fit-out period; rent-review mechanism; break clause; repair responsibility; restrictions on signage, hours and goods; dilapidations at exit. Have a lawyer review the lease and check hidden charges (security, service charges, insurance, local rates).

Fit-out heads: painting, flooring, electrics and lighting, plumbing (food retail), signage, security (grilles, CCTV), counter and service area.

## 6. Startup cost model by format

Build a cost schedule per format with the same heads, quoted locally, using the format's floor area:

| Head | General shop | Supermarket | Specialty store | Convenience store | Franchise |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rent deposit | yes | yes | yes | yes | to franchisor spec |
| Fit-out | yes | yes | higher finish | yes | franchisor spec |
| Fixtures | shelving | shelving, gondolas | showcases, mannequins | shelving, cooler | franchisor spec |
| Refrigeration | optional | major line | optional | cooler | as required |
| Initial stock | yes | largest | yes | yes | yes |
| POS | yes | multiple tills | yes | yes | systems |
| Security | basic | CCTV, trolleys | tags, CCTV | basic | as required |
| Signage, licences, insurance | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Franchise fee, training, royalty | no | no | no | no | yes (royalty as a percentage of turnover) |
| Working capital (three months) | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |

Typical floor areas: general shop 20 to 60 sq m; supermarket 150 to 500 sq m; specialty 30 to 100 sq m; convenience 15 to 40 sq m. Indicative totals in the source ranged from tens of millions of shillings for a small shop to hundreds of millions for a supermarket or franchise; re-quote every line.

Typical cost mix for a small business, as a planning assumption to test: wages are the largest outgoing (roughly a quarter or more), then stock and materials, tax and utilities.

## 7. Fittings and equipment

| Item | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Wall shelving, gondolas | Display packaged goods |
| Glass showcases | High-value items |
| Counter or service desk | Transactions |
| Clothing rails, hangers, mannequins | Apparel |
| Upright cooler, chest freezer | Cold drinks, dairy, frozen goods |
| POS with software, barcode scanner | Sales, stock tracking, speed |
| Digital scales | Fresh and bulk goods |
| CCTV, convex mirrors, anti-theft tags | Theft deterrence |
| Signage, baskets and trolleys, pricing guns | Traffic and convenience |

Buying tips: buy from wholesalers with full range, price guarantee and delivery; consider leasing costly equipment to preserve cash; check specifications, power needs, warranty and local spares; read maintenance contracts closely.

Floor layouts: straight (most shops); diagonal (self-service, better flow); angular (high-end specialty); geometric (clothing, flexible); mixed (larger multi-category stores).

## 8. Records and tax

Minimum records: cash book; sales ledger; purchase ledger; petty cash book; bank statements and paying-in slips; stock records (quantity, cost, selling price); staff records (deductions, contracts, attendance); till receipts and daily sales summaries. Retain records for the statutory period (verify locally).

Tax obligation checklist (Uganda; verify current rates and thresholds with the revenue authority and the Chwezi finance engine): tax identification number; income tax (presumptive regime for small turnover, returns for others); corporation tax for companies; VAT registration above the turnover threshold, with monthly returns; PAYE on employee pay; social security contributions above the employee-count threshold; local trading licence renewed annually; withholding tax on certain payments such as rent and professional fees.

Accounting tools: cloud accounting packages, modular accounting software, or a well-structured spreadsheet for the smallest shops.

Break-even formula: break-even sales = fixed costs / gross margin percentage. Worked pattern: fixed costs of 3 million per month at a 25 per cent gross margin need 12 million of monthly sales.

## 9. Insurance

| Type | Covers | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Public liability | Customer injury on premises or by products | Essential |
| Employers' liability | Staff injury or illness | Mandatory with staff |
| Stock and contents | Theft, fire, flood damage | Essential |
| Fire | Premises and contents | Essential |
| Business interruption | Lost income during closure | Highly recommended |
| Money | Cash in till, safe, transit | Recommended |
| Goods in transit | Delivery loss | If transporting stock |
| Professional indemnity | Advice or installation services | Situational |
| Motor | Delivery vehicles | Mandatory with vehicles |

Tips: compare quotes or use a broker; bundle policies; disclose fully on proposal forms; review cover annually; pay annually where instalments carry interest.

## 10. Buying an existing shop

Advantages: immediate income, known turnover, established customers and suppliers, fittings in place, easier finance. Disadvantages: hidden debts, declining trade, inherited reputation and stale stock, professional fees, unfavourable lease.

Due-diligence checklist:
- Financial: several years of accounts or tax returns; sales verified against bank and till records; debts, loans and claims; tax compliance; seasonal cash pattern; stock value (current, in date, saleable).
- Legal: lease term, rent reviews, break clauses, repairs; assignability of the lease; licences valid and transferable; pending litigation or regulatory action; staff contracts inherited.
- Operational: premises inspection (structure, plumbing, electrics, damp, pests); fixtures condition and life; key suppliers willing to continue; neighbour and area reputation; the owner's reason for selling.
- Valuation: professional valuation of the business, not only property; separate asset value from goodwill; compare with recent sales; set a maximum offer before negotiating.

Sequence: register interest, preliminary due diligence, offer, legal due diligence, then announce the change of ownership positively.

## 11. Common mistakes and controls

| Mistake | Control |
|---|---|
| Undercapitalisation (profit typically takes months) | Budget at least six months of operating costs beyond fit-out and stock; add a contingency (about 15 to 20 per cent, to test); keep a personal emergency fund |
| Wrong location | Count traffic before signing; match location to customer; check planned roads or competing malls |
| Poor stock selection | Start focused; expand from sales data; weekly stock check; do not stock what you would not buy; follow trade trends |
| Ignoring the numbers | Record from day one; weekly cash review; monthly budget-versus-actual variances; margin by category |
| Inadequate market research | State a unique selling proposition; visit competitors; talk to prospective customers; test the market |
| No business plan | Concise living plan, reviewed quarterly, shared with an adviser |
| Doing everything alone | Outsource weak areas; find a mentor; join a trade association |
| Neglecting service | Train staff on greeting and complaints; act on feedback; know regulars |
| Over-investing in fit-out | Prioritise function; consider second-hand fixtures; fit out in stages |

## 12. Startup timeline

| Timeframe | Key actions |
|---|---|
| Six to four months before opening | Concept, market research, plan, explore finance |
| Four to two months | Secure finance, sign lease, register with registry and tax authority, begin fit-out |
| Two to one month | Complete fit-out, order stock, install POS and security, hire and train, insure |
| Opening month | Soft launch, adjust stock to early sales, set daily cash and stock routines |
| Months one to six after | Weekly cash review, actuals versus budget, supplier relationships, stock-mix review |

## 13. KPIs and plan-section prompts

KPIs: sales per square metre; gross margin by category; stock turn; shrink; average basket; footfall-to-sale conversion; weeks of cash runway.

Prompts: describe the concept and gap; show the location count and lease terms; present the format cost schedule; show funding sources and the two-year cash cover; list licences and tax registrations with dates; state insurance in place; give break-even and the controls for section 11.

Always seek advice from a qualified accountant and lawyer before legal or financial commitments.

Sources consulted: Penrith, D., Start and Run a Shop (How To Books, 2009); Berman, B. and Evans, J., Retail Management (2013); Tiwari, R., Retailing (2008); general retail practice. Figures are dated planning assumptions.
