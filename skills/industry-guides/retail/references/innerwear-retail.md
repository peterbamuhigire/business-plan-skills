# Innerwear Retail

Use this reference to plan a shop or stall selling panties, bras, boxers, vests, socks, lingerie and swimwear. All figures are planning assumptions to re-verify with current wholesale price lists.

## 1. Market and customer

- Women's and children's segments are the most active; men's innerwear is less contested because female vendors dominate most markets, so it is a niche opportunity (test with a street-level trial). Children's innerwear supports better margins where parents buy on quality rather than price.
- Buying behaviour to design around: purchases are impulse-driven for a majority of buyers, loyalty to a particular vendor is low for most, and replacement on wear-out is the main secondary trigger. Location, variety and price therefore drive sales; open display for easy browsing helps. Treat survey percentages as directional and re-survey locally.
- Entry is possible at street-vendor level with very small capital; a fixed shop needs several times more.

## 2. Operating model

Choose the format: roadside or market stall (lowest cost, daily-profit model), small shop, or specialist shop (women's, children's or men's). Stock by segment; the number of garments owned per customer is rising, supporting repeat demand. Use stock display that suits local norms: men's lines are often displayed less openly than women's; coastal and modest-dress areas favour full-coverage and shaping styles over lingerie.

## 3. Sourcing

Main sources: wholesale clusters in the capital and border markets (Kenya: Moi Avenue lingerie wholesalers, Kamukunji, Eastleigh; Uganda: Owino and Kikuubo; Tanzania: Kariakoo; Rwanda: Nyabugogo and Kimironko). Imports come mainly from China and Turkey (mid-range). Prices for the same SKU vary between suppliers, so compare at least three and negotiate volume discounts. Minimum lots are typically a dozen (a few pieces for some lines).

## 4. Unit economics

- Margin per item = retail price - wholesale price / pieces per lot. Planning assumption: gross margins of about 20-50%, with lace, seamless and premium lines higher and plain cotton basics and socks lower.
- Daily profit model for a stall: sum(units per day x margin per unit) - transport and market fees.
- Shop model: daily sales x gross margin - rent - wages - utilities; compare rent per month against contribution. Test with a scenario for each format.

## 5. Capex template

Licences (business permit, signboard, fire clearance, worker medical certificates, registration); equipment (display shelves and racks, counter and glass, signboard, furniture); opening mixed stock; premises (rent deposit plus first month, renovation and branding); three months of wages and utilities. Keep at least one restocking cycle in reserve.

## 6. Regulation checks

Local trade licence and tax registration; textile import standards apply technically but retail enforcement is inconsistent in some markets; check current import duty under the regional tariff. Tax: verify with the Chwezi finance engine.

## 7. East Africa adaptation

Uganda: mass market served by market wholesalers and Chinese imports, lower rents than Nairobi. Tanzania: Kariakoo hub, Zanzibar swimwear for tourists, modest-wear in coastal areas. Rwanda: modesty norms make the lingerie niche small relative to cotton basics.

## 8. Risks and KPIs

Risks: price undercutting, fashion cycles and dead stock, supplier quality variation, theft, low loyalty. KPIs: units per day by line, margin per dozen, stock turn, rent-to-sales ratio, conversion of passers-by.

## 9. Plan-section prompts

Segment choice with evidence; supplier comparison table; pricing and margin per line; capex; daily sales assumptions with a stated basis.

Sources consulted: Crack A Business Kenya, innerwear retail quick guide (2017); general retail practice. All price lists are dated and must be refreshed.
