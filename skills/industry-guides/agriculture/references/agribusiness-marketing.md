# Marketing and Sales for Agricultural Businesses: Plan-Section Reference

Use for the marketing section of a plan for an input supplier, processor, distributor or commodity marketer.

## Marketing orientation
Choose and state the orientation: product-driven (build then sell), sales-driven (push what is made) or market-driven (start with customer need and work backwards). Plans should be market-driven and show evidence of customer need.

## Marketing planning sequence
1. Situation review (marketing audit).
2. Opportunities and threats (SWOT).
3. Objectives (specific and measurable).
4. Strategy: segmentation, targeting, positioning.
5. Marketing mix: product, price, promotion, place.
6. Implementation and evaluation.

### Segmentation and adoption
- Segment consumer markets by geography, demographics, lifestyle and behaviour; segment farm (business) markets by location and soil, farm size and enterprise, management style and technology adoption, and purchase frequency and service needs.
- Apply the diffusion-of-innovations adopter categories (Rogers): innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards. Target early adopters first for new inputs or practices and use them as reference farmers.
- Positioning statement: what it is, who it is for, why it is better than alternatives.

## Marketing mix
### Product
- Total product concept: generic, expected, value-added (soil testing, agronomic advice, delivery) and potential product.
- Customer value = benefits received / price paid. Build advantage by adding benefits (service, information, trust, brand) rather than cutting price.
- Product life cycle: match tactics to introduction, growth, maturity and decline.
### Pricing
- Floor is the price that covers cost; ceiling is the price at which customers switch to substitutes. Choose within the range using cost-plus (commodities), value-based (differentiated), competitive, penetration (entry), skimming (novel) or loss-leader (traffic) approaches.
- Advertising budget: set as a percentage of sales (local farm-focused firms spend a small percentage; consumer-facing firms more) and repeat exposures because prospects need several contacts.
### Promotion
- Mix: advertising, personal selling, sales promotion, public relations, digital. Personal selling is often the most important tool in agribusiness because products are technical, purchases are large, and trust and after-sales support matter. Sales steps: prospecting, pre-approach research, approach, presentation, handling objections, closing, follow-up.
### Place
- Channels for inputs: direct from manufacturer, dealer-distribution (most common in East Africa: agro-dealers, farm stores, co-operatives), distributor systems (merchant distributors take title; agents earn commission).
- Channels for food: to supermarkets, via wholesalers to independent retailers, via food-service distributors, and direct to consumers (farm shops, markets, online).
- Choose on cost, customer access, timeliness (critical for perishables), control and freshness (cold chain).

## Decision tools
- Marketing audit: analyse success by product, review market conditions, identify distinctive competencies, evaluate plan and objectives, review people and structure.
- Sales forecasting methods from simple to complex: sales-force composite, customer survey, trend analysis, moving average, expert opinion (Delphi), regression. Match to data available.
- Competitor grid: rate your firm and rivals on quality, price, distribution, technical support, brand and finance; note advantages and vulnerabilities.
- Customer research: focus groups, surveys, in-depth interviews, observation, market mapping.
- Gap analysis: performance gap = expectation score minus actual score; prioritise large negative gaps on factors customers rate as important.

## Agricultural market characteristics to reflect in the plan
Seasonality, perishability, biological variability (weather, pests), derived demand for inputs, price volatility, and government intervention (subsidies, trade policy).
- Supply shifters: technology, input prices, weather, alternative products, subsidies or taxes, number of suppliers.
- Demand shifters: income, tastes, expected prices, number of buyers, prices of substitutes and complements.
- Cross-price elasticity: negative implies complements, zero independent, positive substitutes; use it to design promotions and bundles.

## Plan-section prompts
Which orientation and segments? What is the positioning statement? Which channel and why? What is the promotion mix and budget? How is the forecast built and reviewed?

Sources consulted: Barnard, Akridge, Dooley and Foltz, Agribusiness Management, 4th edn (Routledge, 2012); Rogers, Diffusion of Innovations. Local figures must be re-verified.
