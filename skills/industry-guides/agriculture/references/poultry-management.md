# Poultry Enterprises (Layers, Broilers, Dual-Purpose): Plan-Section Reference

Use for a poultry business plan: enterprise choice, housing, feeding, health, records, economics, free-range and organic positioning, and waste use. Veterinary schedules and drug decisions must be confirmed with the district veterinary officer or a veterinarian. Prices, feed costs and benchmarks are planning assumptions to be replaced by dated local quotations (feed suppliers, hatcheries, poultry farmers' association).

## 1. Choose the enterprise first
Match breed to purpose: layer breeds lay well but are uneconomic as meat birds; broiler breeds grow fast but lay too little to justify feed. 

### Enterprise comparison (layers versus broilers)
| Factor | Broilers | Layers |
|---|---|---|
| Time to first income | Weeks (short cycle, then batch sale) | Several months of rearing, or buy point-of-lay pullets at higher cost |
| Revenue pattern | Lump sum per cycle; downtime for cleaning between batches | Daily or weekly egg income |
| Capital recovery | Fast, repeated each cycle | Slow; capital tied up for the laying period |
| Price risk | Acute at sale time (a batch cannot wait) | Lower, steadier |
| Disease exposure window | Short | Long, cumulative |
| Housing | Simpler | Nest boxes, roosts, lighting, egg handling |
| Fit | Capital-constrained start, learning quickly | Steady household income, working capital for the rearing gap |
- Strategy: for a first-time farmer with limited capital, one or two broiler cycles allow fast learning and capital recovery; farmers with stable alternative income can move to layers if they can fund the rearing period. Running both at once doubles management load and is not a starting position.
- Broiler risk control: pre-sell to a committed buyer with an agreed price formula or floor before placing chicks; time batches for seasonal demand peaks (festive periods).
- Layer risk control: hold a feed reserve of a few weeks, line up buyers before hens come into lay, consider a standing supply contract with a hotel or institution.
- Profitability check: build a cycle model for each option: chicks or pullets, feed (kilograms x price), vaccines and litter, utilities, labour; revenue = surviving birds x weight x price (broilers) or eggs x price (layers). Feed cost is the dominant cost; at local feed and egg prices layers can be unprofitable unless lay rate is high, feed is mixed on farm, product earns a premium (free-range, direct supply) or scale absorbs overhead. Recalculate with actual prices before committing capital.

## 2. Breed selection
- Layers: high-output light breeds (for example White Leghorn, needs confinement and calm management) versus hardier warm-climate breeds (Australorp, Rhode Island Red, Plymouth Rock, Sussex) that suit semi-intensive smallholder systems.
- Broilers: fast commercial hybrids (need imported-quality feed and controlled housing) versus slower-growing coloured or improved local birds (lower input cost, better disease resistance, flavour premium in some markets). Heritage meat breeds are not recommended commercially.
- Dual-purpose (for example sex-linked crosses, Rhode Island Red, Barred Plymouth Rock): moderate egg output plus usable cockerels; suits mixed-income smallholders selling eggs and surplus male birds. Sex-linked crosses allow sexing at hatch.
- Free-range breeds: active foragers, calm, heat-tolerant, disease-resistant; East African improved local and hybrid village-system birds (for example Kuroiler and improved kienyeji types) suit free-range systems. Confirm current availability from national breeding centres and hatcheries.
- Typical benchmarks to test against supplier data: layer output in the first laying year, peak lay several months after hatch, replacement at about 72 weeks; broiler market weight at about six to eight weeks for fast hybrids, mortality target under about 5 per cent, layer mortality under about 10 per cent a year.

## 3. Housing and space
- Space standards (guide): deep litter about 0.2 to 0.3 square metres per layer; semi-intensive adds a run; free-range needs indoor space plus a substantial outdoor range with vegetation; broilers about 0.07 to 0.1 square metres each in confinement. Roost space about 15 cm per hen and one nest box per four to five hens. Overcrowding is the commonest cause of disease, feather pecking and poor performance: stock to the standard, not to fill the house.
- Ventilation: ammonia from manure is the main respiratory risk. Use open eaves covered with mesh on both long sides, a ridge vent, windbreaks against cold draughts, orientation across the prevailing wind, and test by smell at bird height.
- Litter: deep litter (wood shavings, rice or coffee husks, dry grass, chopped banana leaves) at adequate depth; top up when damp, stir weekly, replace completely between flocks; compost spent litter.
- Heat: heat stress cuts feed intake and lay and can kill fast-growing broilers. Use insulated roofing or false ceilings, shade trees east and west, damp hessian on vents, extra water, and feed in the cooler hours.

### Flooring options
| Floor | Strengths | Weaknesses | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concrete plus deep litter | Durable, disinfectable, rodent-resistant | Cost; foot and breast lesions if litter is thin or wet; slippery | Commercial houses with all-in all-out |
| Earth plus deep litter | Cheap, soft, warm | Cannot be disinfected fully; coccidiosis risk when wet; rodents burrow | Small, capital-limited flocks |
| Plastic slats | Droppings fall through; dry, easy to wash | Cost; UV brittleness; gap size matters | Commercial broilers, brooders |
| Wooden slats | Cheap, local | Rots, mites, termites, splinters; short life | Small breeder or brooder use |
| Wire mesh | Drainage and airflow | Foot injuries in heavier birds | Cages, first weeks of brooding |
| Expanded metal | Strong, drains | Highest cost | Specialist breeding |
Decision guide: small layer flocks on earth plus litter; medium layer farms and commercial broilers on concrete plus litter (or slats); brooders on concrete plus litter or slats. Always avoid standing water, keep litter depth, replace litter each cycle, and add a concrete apron around house walls against rodents.

## 4. Feeding and water
- Feed by stage (protein content and form): chick starter, grower, layer developer, layer; broiler starter and finisher with higher protein. Do not feed layer feed to chicks (excess calcium harms kidneys) or starter to laying hens (calcium too low). Give layers free-choice oyster shell or limestone grit.
- Water intake is roughly double feed weight; interrupting water in heat reduces lay immediately.
- Feed sources: commercial pelleted feed (most reliable), on-farm mixed rations (maize, soybean meal, premix and limestone; cheaper but needs consistent ingredients and mixing), and foraging supplements for free-range birds (cost savings and better yolk colour).
- Aflatoxin: mouldy maize-based feed suppresses immunity and vaccine response. Buy from reputable millers, store sealed and raised for a few weeks only, discard suspect feed.
- Layer lighting: about 14 to 16 hours of light for peak lay; add pre-dawn light with low-wattage LED bulbs; increase gradually at point of lay; never reduce light hours during lay (triggers moult).
- Flock replacement: lay rate declines in the second year while feed use stays similar; replace when feed cost approaches egg value (about 12 to 14 months of lay). Forced moulting extends lay for months at lower efficiency and is only worth it when replacement pullets are prohibitively costly.

## 5. Eggs: grading, handling, marketing
- Grades from clean firm-shelled premium eggs down to stained or cracked eggs sold at a discount or used at home. Smallholder markets sort visually by size and shell; frequent collection (two to three times daily) and clean nest boxes are the best quality control.
- Storage: unwashed eggs keep for weeks at ambient temperature; wash only eggs for immediate use; pack in 30-egg trays.
- Pre-investment market checklist: who buys (households, restaurants, institutions, traders); farm-gate price per egg or tray; weekly quantity by buyer type; tray versus retail price gap; premium for free-range, organic or village eggs; competition.

## 6. Biosecurity
- All-in all-out per house; controlled entry with footbaths and dedicated boots and coats; visitor log; quarantine new birds for two to four weeks; rodent and wild-bird control; prompt burning or deep burial of dead birds.
- Weak points: attending livestock markets and returning without changing clothes; traders visiting several farms; workers keeping village chickens at home. Train every worker (with illustrated instructions in local languages) and reward early reporting of unusual bird behaviour.
- Clean and disinfect between flocks: remove litter, scrub with detergent, rinse, disinfect, dry, rest the house for at least two weeks, fresh litter. Wash drinkers daily and scrub feeders weekly; control flies by managing litter moisture.

## 7. Vaccination and health programme (confirm with a veterinarian)
- Priorities: Newcastle disease (highest; live vaccine first dose in the first ten days by eye drop, second dose by drinking water, boosters every six to eight weeks); infectious bursal (Gumboro) disease (live intermediate vaccine from about two weeks, not earlier because maternal antibodies interfere, second dose two weeks later); Marek's disease (applied at the hatchery on day one; confirm before buying chicks); fowl pox (wing-web at six to eight weeks; confirm take after about a week; mosquitoes spread pox near wetlands); infectious bronchitis (strain-matched live vaccine; multiple strains circulate); fowl cholera where endemic (killed vaccine); infectious laryngotracheitis only where confirmed locally; coccidiosis via vaccine or anticoccidial medication at the age of risk, with dry litter as the main control; avian influenza policy is set by government, so consult the district veterinary officer and report sudden unexplained deaths.
- Consolidated schedule: day one hatchery vaccines; first Newcastle dose in the first ten days; Gumboro from about day 14 to 21 and repeat two weeks later; second Newcastle dose about day 21 to 28; fowl pox at six to eight weeks; Newcastle boosters throughout life.
- Cold chain: keep live vaccines refrigerated (about 2 to 8 degrees Celsius) and in a cool box with ice while in use; use reconstituted vaccine within hours; use clean, chlorine-free water for water administration and withdraw water beforehand; wash equipment with hot water only (no disinfectant residues); buy dose sizes matching flock size; record vaccine, batch, date, flock and administrator.
- Vaccine failure causes: broken cold chain, maternal antibody interference, wrong strain, immune suppression (stress, aflatoxin, coinfection), missed birds. Diagnose before blaming the vaccine.
- Withdrawal periods: record treatments and do not sell meat or eggs until the label withdrawal period ends.
- Daily health observation: walk the house before feeding; count and record deaths; treat falling feed or water intake, any sustained fall in lay, lethargy, abnormal droppings, respiratory signs, swollen faces or joints and rising daily mortality as triggers to investigate. Build a relationship with a veterinarian or extension officer in advance; keep a basic first-response kit.
- Handling and stress: catch birds in low light, hold by the body, use ventilated crates for transport in the cool of the morning, keep feeding and lighting schedules consistent, and remove dead birds promptly.
- Pests: rodents (concrete apron, sealed gaps, bait stations outside, metal feed bins), predators (weekly fence checks, latched pop-holes), external parasites (weekly red-mite check at night, treat with approved dust or spray).

## 8. Records to keep (eight types)
1. Flock inventory: date, event (purchase, sale, death, cull), quantity, running total; mortality percentage = deaths / starting flock x 100; investigate sustained rises.
2. Feed: date, type, quantity, supplier and batch, cost; feed conversion = feed used per dozen eggs or per unit of weight gain; a rising ratio signals a feeding, health or management problem.
3. Egg production: daily count, breakages, lay rate = eggs / hens x 100 (a sudden fall is an early disease or predator signal).
4. Health and treatment: date, signs, birds affected, treatment (drug, dose, route), veterinarian, outcome, withdrawal date.
5. Breeding and hatchery (if applicable): eggs set, fertility, hatch rate = chicks hatched / eggs set x 100; persistent low hatch signals male fertility, egg storage or incubator issues.
6. Financial: itemised income and expenses (chicks, feed, vaccines, litter, labour, repairs, utilities, transport, licences; eggs, live birds, manure); monthly profit check. Lenders usually want a year or more of production and financial records.
7. Environmental: house temperature, humidity, ammonia smell, litter condition, ventilation; brooder temperature judged by chick behaviour (huddling means too cold, avoiding the heat source means too hot).
8. Sales: buyer, product, quantity, price, payment method, outstanding balance; maintain a verified customer list.
- Tools: ledger books for small flocks; phone apps or spreadsheets for larger flocks; photograph pages for backup. Record at the time of the event, train staff, review weekly, back up monthly.

## 9. Economic efficiency metrics
Feed conversion, annual output per hen, mortality, culling rate, break-even price per egg (monthly cost / monthly eggs sold), and feed cost as a share of revenue (feed usually takes well over half of revenue). Scale: a household flock of tens of birds, a primary-income flock in the hundreds to low thousands, and commercial scale in the thousands with professional management; test that the chosen scale supports a full-time owner after labour and overheads.

## 10. Free-range and organic positioning
- Free-range: daily outdoor access with vegetation, more space (indoor plus a substantial range per bird), no routine antibiotics; birds return to secure housing each night. Organic: certified organic feed, no synthetic medicines (vaccines allowed), outdoor access, third-party certification before using the label.
- Range design: divide the range into at least two or three paddocks and rest each for several weeks; grow forage and shade trees; provide shade, dust-bathing areas and water points on the range; avoid bare compacted soil (parasite and coccidiosis risk). Electric poultry netting allows moving paddocks; a fixed strong perimeter fence is essential.
- Predator protection: fence buried at the base, overhead netting or dense shade against raptors, secure night housing with predator-proof latches, guard dogs or geese, and a predator-specific response table (monitor lizards, mongooses, raptors, stray dogs, snakes, rats).
- Welfare: no beak trimming where possible, monitor feather condition, shade and dust baths.
- Certification decision: for export or high-end urban retail, certification is essential; for informal and rural markets, position as free-range or village-style and invest in certification only when a specific buyer requires it. Certification bodies and fees vary; get quotations (Uganda: national organic certification bodies).
- Premium pricing: compare farm-gate prices by system (intensive, semi-intensive, free-range, certified, village eggs; commercial broilers versus coloured or village birds) using current local quotations, and quantify the premium against the extra land, labour and predator-control cost.
- Sales channels for premium product: upmarket residential customers via messaging orders, health-focused restaurants and cafes, weekend farmers' markets, health-food retailers, and hotels (need consistent volume and quality).

## 11. Poultry waste as an asset
Safety first: fresh manure carries Salmonella and other pathogens, breeds flies and burns roots; compost, digest or otherwise process before use, never apply fresh manure to vegetables or grazed pasture, and comply with environmental authority rules on waste and runoff. Options in priority order:
1. Biogas (200 or more birds): fixed-dome, floating-drum or tubular digesters; gas for cooking or brooder heating and digestate as liquid fertiliser; obtain two or three quotes and check builder quality.
2. Composting (any scale, lowest risk): mix litter with carbon material (about one part manure to two or three parts carbon), keep moist, turn every week or so, ready in weeks when dark, crumbly and cool; sell bagged compost to market gardeners and agrovets or use on farm.
3. Fish-pond fertilisation: apply small controlled manure amounts, watch water colour and oxygen, never use manure from birds treated with antibiotics, stop before harvest.
4. Mushroom substrate: composted litter as a nitrogen supplement to straw or sawdust; separate enterprise, add only once poultry is stable.
5. Manure tea and direct soil amendment: compost soaked and diluted; apply to soil, not to watercourses.
Compute the value of each option (saved fertiliser, fuel or feed, plus sales) from local prices. Start with composting for every farm; add biogas when capital allows.

## 12. Common mistakes checklist
Weak biosecurity; overcrowding and poor ventilation; nutrition errors and aflatoxin; poor hygiene and continuous flocks; irregular health monitoring; rough handling and stress; rodents, predators and parasites; missing records; heat stress; untrained staff.

## Plan-section prompts
Broilers or layers, and why, with a cycle-level cash model? Which breed and source? What house, floor and stocking density? Which feed route and cost per kilogram? What biosecurity, vaccination and record system? Which markets, with pre-sales? What is the waste use plan? Which licences and veterinary support apply?
