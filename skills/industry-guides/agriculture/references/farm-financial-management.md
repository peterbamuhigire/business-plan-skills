# Farm Financial Management (Enterprise Profitability and Controls): Plan-Section Reference

Use for the financial-management and controls part of a farm business plan, especially for a diversified vegetable or mixed farm. Tax and accounting treatments: verify with the Chwezi finance engine.

## Core logic
- Profit = income minus expenses; overall farm profit is an average of enterprises. Some enterprises earn well, some break even, some lose money; profitability improves by identifying which is which. Gross sales and net profit are largely unrelated.
- Plan profit deliberately rather than treating it as what is left. Set a multi-year path for gross sales and net profit; gross sales typically need to be a multiple (roughly two to three times) of desired net profit.

## Enterprise and crop budgets
- Budget each crop with variable costs (and allocated overhead) so crops can be compared side by side.
- Determine before budgeting: all-in hourly labour rate, the farmer's own hourly rate (costed even though no wage is paid, so profit is not overstated and replacement labour is affordable), total overhead, nursery or greenhouse cost per unit area, machinery cost per hour, irrigation cost per unit area, delivery costs, and market fees.
- All-in labour rate = base wage + employer social-security contribution + insurance + allowance for unassigned time.
- Machinery hourly cost = (purchase price / years of life + annual repairs + annual fuel) / hours used per year; implements exclude fuel. Low annual hours make hourly cost high.
- Overhead: allocate every overhead cost to some enterprise by share of area or sales; otherwise off-farm income subsidises the farm.
- Market-day cost: cost of staff time, loading and travel per market day is fixed; a market whose sales fall below it loses money.
- Index of profitability: rank crops by net return per unit area (not gross sales); expand top earners, scrutinise price and cost of bottom crops, drop persistent losers, and challenge loss-leader habits. Low-value staples may be unprofitable on small acreage once all costs are counted; high-value vegetables generally earn more per acre.

## Financial statements
- P&L: income by channel (wholesale, farm-gate, market, contract) and expenses by category over a period.
- Balance sheet: net worth = assets minus liabilities, with short-, medium- and long-term assets and liabilities; track net worth year to year. A capital purchase can reduce current profit while increasing net worth.
- Cash-flow projection: multi-year, with the proposed changes (equipment, expansion, new markets). Farm cash flow is lumpy (early costs, late income), and a profitable farm can fail for lack of cash; plan a credit line, savings-group access or reserve.

## Tax awareness (verify locally)
- Understand which taxes apply (income tax, PAYE, employer social-security contributions, withholding tax, VAT above the registration threshold, agricultural exemptions). Rates and thresholds must be verified with the finance engine and the revenue authority.
- Saved-expense rule: because of tax, one unit saved in expenses is worth more than one unit of extra revenue; unrecorded expenses cost extra tax.
- Caution on capital purchases made only to cut tax: assets that rust, rot and depreciate are expenses, not investments.

## Retirement and reserves
Set aside savings for the owner, not only the business: compare deposits, government securities and pooled funds after inflation, use mandatory and voluntary pension schemes, and consider farmland as a long-term asset. Do not give investment advice in the plan; state the policy only.

## Preventing financial leaks
- Sales leaks: no receipt written, lost invoices (use duplicate or triplicate books), arithmetic errors (use software or mobile apps), over-generous measure at market (weigh and count against what is charged), estimated instead of measured weights (use certified digital scales; the scale usually pays for itself quickly).
- Expense leaks: unrecorded expenses, no vehicle mileage or fuel log, no bookkeeper. A part-time bookkeeper commonly recovers the cost in errors and tax savings.
- Cash versus accrual: cash is simpler and mirrors a bank or mobile-money account; accrual with inventory gives a truer yearly picture (for example when pre-buying fertiliser before year-end).

## Chart of accounts (starter)
Income: wholesale, farm-gate, market, contract. Expenses: advertising and marketing, donations, fertiliser and soil amendments, fuel and oil, nursery and greenhouse supplies, insurance, interest, internet and mobile-money charges, livestock expenses, office, payroll, professional services, land rent, repairs and maintenance (by asset), seeds and seedlings, packaging supplies, taxes and licences, transport and delivery, utilities, miscellaneous.

## Plan-section prompts
What net profit path is planned and what gross sales does it require? What is each crop's net return per unit area? What hourly labour and machinery rates are used? What controls prevent leaks? What is the seasonal cash plan?

Sources consulted: Wiswall, The Organic Farmer's Business Handbook (Chelsea Green, 2009). Local tax rates, wage rates and prices must be verified with dated sources.
