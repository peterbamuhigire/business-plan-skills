# Farm Resource Management: Plan-Section Reference

Use for the land, water, machinery, labour, technology and environmental-compliance parts of a farm plan. All prices and rates below are drivers to be replaced with dated local quotations.

## Land and soil
- Assess land by capability class: the best classes suit annual crops; steeper, wetter or stonier classes suit pasture, perennials, forestry or conservation. Farm the best land for crops.
- Soil texture: sandy (drains fast, low nutrient retention), clay (drains slowly, compacts, high retention), loam (ideal). Test pH (target around 6 to 7 for most crops), organic matter (about 3 to 6 per cent), major nutrients, and cation exchange capacity. Test annually and adjust amendments.
- Sustainable practices: cover crops, crop rotation, conservation tillage, compost, rotational grazing, farmscaping (habitat and windbreaks). Cost each and note the benefit (erosion control, fertility, pest break, input savings).

## Water and irrigation
- Compare systems (hand line, K-line, wheel line, solid set, drip or micro) on capital per unit area, water efficiency, labour and best use; drip is most efficient for high-value rows and berries, solid set for orchards.
- Water budget: daily crop need = evapotranspiration rate x area (with unit conversion), divided by system efficiency to get water applied. Compute monthly volume and check source capacity, pumping cost and storage.
- Water rights: abstraction and borehole permits, customary or riparian access, metering; treat secure water access as a key asset. Confirm with the national water authority.

## Machinery
- Annual ownership cost = fixed (depreciation, interest on average investment, insurance, housing) + variable (fuel and lubricants, repairs that rise with age, operator labour). Divide by hours to get cost per hour.
- Buy versus lease or hire: buy when annual hours are high, cash or finance is available, and maintenance skills exist; lease or hire when use is low, capital is tight or technology changes quickly. Rule: if hire rate x hours of use is below annual ownership cost, hire.

## Labour
- True labour cost = wages + payroll taxes and contributions + benefits + workers' compensation + training and supervision; the multiplier over base wage is commonly above one tenth and can approach one fifth or more.
- Productivity benchmarks to set locally: transplanting, weeding, mechanical cultivation, harvest by crop type, washing and packing, and market sales per hour. Use local time studies.
- Practice: written job descriptions, documented training (food safety, equipment), regular check-ins, fair pay to reduce turnover, employment contracts and statutory registrations, and records for casual labour.

## Season extension
Row covers, plastic mulch, low tunnels, unheated high tunnels and heated greenhouses give increasing temperature gain, cost and life span. Match to crop hardiness groups and the local climate; in warm climates the value is often rain, pest and heat protection rather than cold protection. Evaluate with NPV and payback.

## Production economics
- Net return per unit area = yield x marketable share x price minus (variable + fixed costs).
- Profit drivers in order to test: price received (direct markets often earn well above wholesale), yield, marketable share (post-harvest handling, grading, storage), labour efficiency, input costs.
- Ecosystem cycles (carbon, nitrogen, water) and the integrated pest management ladder: prevention, cultural, biological, mechanical, then chemical controls (least toxic and targeted first).

## Supply chain and marketing channels
- Marketing mix: product, price, promotion, place; customer-centred equivalent: solution, information, value, access.
- Channels compared on price premium, volume, labour, and relationship: farmers' market, box subscription (CSA), farm stand or pick-your-own, restaurants, grocery and retail, wholesale, online delivery.
- Subscription share price = (total farm costs + owner compensation + target profit) / number of shares; weekly value = share price / season weeks.

## Technology adoption
Assess precision technologies (GPS guidance, soil mapping, drones, weather stations, farm-management software, automated irrigation) by cost, payback timeline and staff skills; start with those with the fastest payback.

## Environmental compliance and sustainability
- Check regulation areas: water quality, pesticide control, worker safety, food safety and standards, organic certification, animal disease and welfare, wetlands, and environmental impact assessment for large clearing or processing. Examples in Uganda: national environment authority, agricultural chemicals control, occupational safety, standards bureau, organic standard bodies.
- Practices with joint environmental and economic benefit: cover cropping, rotation, conservation tillage, composting, rotational grazing, buffer strips, integrated pest management. Frame as triple bottom line (profit, environment, community).
- Energy: fuel (consolidate field passes), electricity (cold rooms are usually the largest draw), and renewables (solar, biogas, biomass); evaluate payback and any partner grants.

## Plan-section prompts
Which land class and soil tests? What water source, system and budget? Buy or hire machinery? What true labour cost per hour? Which channels earn the highest net return? Which permits apply?
