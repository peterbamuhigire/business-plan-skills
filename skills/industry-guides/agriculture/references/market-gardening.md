# Intensive Market Gardening (Micro-Farm Model): Plan-Section Reference

Use when the plan covers a small plot of intensively managed vegetables sold directly. The model works because of intensive land use, direct marketing, tools matched to bed scale, high-value crop choice and biological fertility. Revenue per unit area can far exceed conventional grain or wholesale vegetable growing; test this with local numbers, not with cold-climate benchmarks.

## Business model drivers
1. Multiple crop successions per bed per year; no idle land.
2. Direct-to-consumer sales (subscription boxes, markets); no middlemen.
3. Human-powered or small-engine tools for bed width, avoiding heavy machinery capital.
4. High-value, high-yield crops.
5. Compost-based fertility in place of repeated purchased fertiliser.
Peri-urban plots of about half an acre to three acres near large cities and secondary towns are the natural fit.

## Site selection checklist
Deep, well-drained loamy soil with adequate organic matter; reliable irrigation source; flat to gentle slope; full sun (six or more hours); vehicle access; short distance to market; wind protection. Also check year-round water (borehole, stream, tank, mains), a laboratory soil test before purchase or lease, flood risk in the rainy season, travel time to the main market, and land history (avoid former industrial use or heavy pesticide use).

## Permanent raised bed system
- Standard bed about 75 cm wide with 45 cm paths (reachable from both sides, wheelbarrow-wide paths); length commonly about 30 m; surface a few centimetres above path for drainage and warmth. Productive share of land in this layout is roughly 60 per cent: compute = bed width / (bed width + path width).
- Benefits: no compaction, fertility concentrated on beds, tools sized to the bed, and numbered beds for crop history and yield data.
- Establishment: cut vegetation, apply a few centimetres of compost, loosen deeply with a broadfork without inverting layers, rake level and plant. Afterwards use only shallow surface cultivation to protect soil biology.

## Tools and equipment
- Broadfork (loosening without inversion), wheel hoe (inter-row weeding, large labour saving), seeders matched to seed size (larger seeds, fine seeds, dense small seeds), collinear hoe, transplant dibble board, garden fork, harvest knives and reusable stackable crates. Tools may be fabricated locally.
- Investment order: broadfork and wheel hoe; seeder matched to main crops; drip irrigation; low tunnels or greenhouse for high-value crops; refrigerated storage later.

## Crop planning and successions
- Select crops by market demand, revenue per bed length, ease of production and bed turn rate.
- Succession logic: work backwards from market day: kilograms sold per market day, days from sowing to harvest, yield per bed, then how many beds must be planted at what interval for a continuous supply. Keep a direct-seeding table (seeder, seed spacing, row spacing, depth) for each crop.
- Compare crops by yield per bed, revenue per bed and number of successions per year (salad mixes and leafy greens turn quickly; tomatoes give one high-value crop per season).

## Season extension
Low tunnels (hoops plus row cover: cheapest, some temperature gain and rain and pest protection), caterpillar tunnels (walk-under, for tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers), and hoophouses (passive, permanent; in tropical climates the main value is rain protection and disease reduction). Justify with premium crop prices and payback.

## Soil fertility
- Compost as the main input: apply a consistent depth across beds each season; on-farm compost from crop residues, manure and dry carbon material. Local sources: poultry and cattle manure, market waste, municipal compost.
- Vermicompost: worm bins fed on scraps and aged manure, harvested periodically; surplus can be sold to urban gardeners.
- Green manures during bed rest (sunn hemp, mucuna, cowpeas suit East Africa; small grains suit cooler areas).

## Pest and disease management
Hierarchy: prevention through healthy soil, cultural controls (rotation, intercropping, sanitation), physical controls (row covers, traps, hand removal), biological controls (predatory insects and mites, released early), then botanical or soap sprays as a last resort. Rotate plant families through beds on a three-year cycle (for example nightshades, then brassicas, then alliums; cucurbits, then legumes, then roots).

## Records
Bed map, planting log, harvest log (yield per bed to compute revenue per bed), sales log by channel, and input log with monthly cost summary. An exercise book updated weekly is enough to begin; move to a spreadsheet as complexity grows.

## Set-up cost checklist
Land preparation and initial compost, drip irrigation (gravity-fed reduces cost), first-season seed for a range of varieties, tools, low tunnels, compost inputs, packaging. Obtain local quotes and treat year one as the highest cost; later years fall as assets are amortised and on-farm compost matures.

## Plan-section prompts
Which site and what evidence of soil and water? How many beds and what revenue per bed? Which succession calendar? What tools and irrigation, in what order? How is fertility supplied and pests managed? What records support pricing?

Sources consulted: Fortier, The Market Gardener (New Society Publishers, 2014), based on cold-climate conditions; local adaptations and figures must be verified. Techniques not supported by evidence are excluded.
