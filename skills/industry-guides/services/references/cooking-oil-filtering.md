# Cooking Oil Filtering Service

Use this reference when a plan proposes a mobile B2B service that filters used frying oil for restaurants and cafes with a portable filter, extending oil life and cutting the client's oil bill.

## 1. Business model and value logic

1. Deep-frying degrades oil through heat, food particles and oxidation.
2. Filtering removes particles and some degradation by-products, so oil can be reused for additional cycles (planning assumption: 20-40% oil saving per filtering cycle; verify by a demonstration on client fryers).
3. Client saving per litre = oil price x saving percentage. Set the service charge below that saving so the client's net benefit is positive.
4. Provider costs are mainly transport, plus return on the filter investment; the filter uses the client's electricity.
5. Charge per visit rather than per litre for simple pricing, with a per-litre ceiling below the client's saving; route clustering cuts transport cost per visit.

## 2. Target market

| Segment | Fryer volume | Filter class | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small fast-food (chips, doughnuts, fritters) | small | small portable | high potential, price-sensitive |
| Medium fast-food and cafes | medium | mid-size | best target: high volume, open to savings |
| Large restaurants and caterers | large | large | good potential; may buy their own filter |
| Upscale hotels | internal filtration | n/a | limited opportunity |
| Very low-volume street vendors | very small | n/a | not viable |

Field surveys show very few small operators have any filter, so the market is largely untapped but slow to convert.

## 3. Adoption barriers and accelerators

Barriers: awareness gap; scepticism about effectiveness; reluctance to pay for a "new" service; existing free manual filtering with sieves or cloth.

Accelerators: free or discounted first demonstration; express the benefit as money saved per month; bundle fryer cleaning; professional branding (uniform, business cards, branded vehicle) for credibility.

## 4. Equipment and operations

- Buy filter capacity matched to the largest fryer segment served; obtain distributor quotes including tax and warranty, and date them.
- Clean the filter thoroughly after each use; recurring cost is low; check warranty terms.
- Route design: cluster clients geographically to minimise travel time and cost.
- Staffing: if employing an operator, set a daily minimum revenue target with the operator keeping the excess, or use commission versus retainer; compare against fraud risk.
- Food safety: hygiene practice on client premises, cleaning and disposal of filtered residue and spent oil.

## 5. Unit economics drivers

- Weekly revenue = clients x visits per week x fee per visit.
- Weekly net = revenue - transport per day x working days - operator cost - consumables.
- Payback (weeks) = filter cost / weekly net; compute base, slow-adoption and fast-adoption cases (planning assumption: payback in the order of a year at moderate client counts).
- Sensitivity: oil price (higher prices strengthen the pitch), client count per cluster, visit frequency, churn as clients buy their own filters.

## 6. Feasibility assessment

Market exists (large, growing fast-food sector); problem is real (oil is a material share of frying-restaurant production cost); solution works; adoption is slow and needs demonstration and persistent selling; competition is low (pioneer opportunity); exit risk is that clients eventually buy their own filters; capital is moderate. Success depends on (1) a dense enough cluster of suitable restaurants, (2) persistent sales and demonstration effort, and (3) an honest market test before committing capital.

## 7. Regulation and licensing checks

Local authority business licence; food-handler medical certificate; food-safety standards body rules (for example Uganda health certificate, TBS in Tanzania); waste-oil disposal rules. Verify tax treatment with the Chwezi finance engine.

## 8. Risks

Slow adoption; client scepticism; clients buying their own filters; transport cost creep; cross-contamination or hygiene complaints; oil price fall reducing the saving; equipment failure.

## 9. KPIs

Clients signed and retained; visits per day; revenue per route day; transport cost per visit; demonstration-to-contract conversion; churn.

## 10. Localisation

- Uganda: deep-fried street food and roll snack culture; dense fryer-heavy sites in central Kampala; very low awareness of filters.
- Tanzania: mandazi, samosa and chips shops in Dar es Salaam and Mwanza; demonstration-led selling essential.
- Rwanda: growing formal fast-food sector; operators receptive to documented cost-saving.
- Price in local currency: the charge must remain below the saving generated for the client.

## 11. Plan-section prompts

Cluster map and client count; demonstration plan; client saving calculation at current local oil price; pricing; route and cost model; payback scenarios; exit-risk response.

Sources consulted: Crack A Business Kenya (CAB), feasibility of a cooking oil filtering service, 2014; general food-service practice.
