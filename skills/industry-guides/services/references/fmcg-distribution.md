# FMCG Small-Scale Distribution

Use this reference when a plan proposes buying fast-moving consumer goods from manufacturers, importers or large distributors and selling to retail shops (kiosks, small shops, supermarkets) in a defined area. Margins are thin, so volume, route density and supply reliability decide the outcome.

## 1. Choose the operating model

| Model | Description | Strengths | Weaknesses | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Informal mobile distributor | No fixed premises; loads goods on bicycle, motorcycle or vehicle and visits shops | Low overhead, flexible, wide coverage, often cash terms | Small inventory, harder to build corporate supplier relationships, seen as less professional | Start-up, testing a product, dense shop clusters reachable on foot |
| Formal premises-based wholesaler | Shop or store; retailers come to you and you also deliver | Visibility and credibility, easier variety management, permanent presence | Rent, renovation and licences; risk of waiting rather than selling | Growth stage after the product is proven; areas without an established wholesaler in range |

Capital is small for the informal model (from very little for a single product) and rises with premises, licences and stock in the formal model. Treat capital as an assumption derived from your stock plan and dated quotes.

## 2. Product selection

Test for a gap before choosing a product:

- Demographic gap: a population segment not yet served with a product it uses.
- Knowledge gap: a product succeeding elsewhere but unknown locally.
- Supply gap: demand exists but the incumbent supplier has withdrawn or cannot supply.
- New-product gap: early-mover advantage before competitors arrive.

Checklist: is demand exceeding supply locally; what start-up capital does the product need; how perishable is it (bread and milk demand daily discipline; dry goods are forgiving); what is the unit margin and therefore the units per day needed; how many distributors already serve the area (saturation squeezes margin); can you buy broken cartons for low-capital entry.

## 3. Location and market selection

- Population and shop density is paramount: thin margins need many outlets on the route; a handful of shops is not enough.
- Match product price point to local purchasing power: low-income areas favour price over brand; higher-income areas the reverse.
- Existing successful distributors signal a working market; too many squeeze margin.
- Two routes to a decision: find the market then the product, or find the product then the market. In either case, visit the area and speak to several shop owners before committing capital, and verify margins with a sample of retailers and wholesalers.

## 4. Suppliers

- Price matters at volume; reliability matters equally, since running dry costs you retailer trust.
- Prefer suppliers who allow break-bulk.
- Terms: most require cash up front; perishables (bread, milk) may allow one to three days' credit.
- Sources: large wholesale distributors, manufacturers or importers directly (better price at higher volume), secondary distributors (easier access, slightly higher price, more flexibility).

## 5. Unit economics drivers

- Daily contribution = units sold x unit margin - delivery cost - shrinkage and expiry.
- Units needed for a target daily net = (target + fixed costs) / unit margin; convert to shops per day x units per shop to test route feasibility.
- Margin ranges depend on category (perishables, baked goods, snacks, staples, soaps and detergents); compile a dated margin table from local retailers and wholesalers.
- Working capital = stock days x cost of stock + retailer credit outstanding; set credit limits early.
- Transport: bicycle or foot for dense areas, motorcycle or vehicle for spread routes; group deliveries geographically to cut backtracking; hiring motorcycle couriers for last mile reduces vehicle capital.

## 6. Regulation and licensing checks

Local authority trade licence for formal setups; business registration and tax number where trading formally; food-safety and standards-body rules for food lines; weights and measures where goods are sold by weight. Verify tax treatment with the Chwezi finance engine.

## 7. Risks

Thin margins; supplier stock-outs; retailer credit and bad debts; perishable-goods waste; theft; saturation by other distributors; price wars; transport costs; over-expansion before one product and area work.

## 8. Critical success factors

Know margins and break-even units per day before starting; build in one product and one area, then scale; reliability plus price wins retailers; transport efficiency; credit discipline.

## 9. KPIs

Units per day; shops served per day and drops per route hour; average drop size; gross margin per unit; debtor days and bad-debt rate; stock days; delivery cost per unit; fill rate.

## 10. Localisation

- Uganda: informal-to-formal progression; wholesale hubs in central Kampala; mobile money reduces credit risk; local trade licence for formal setups.
- Tanzania: Kariakoo (Dar es Salaam) is the principal wholesale hub; Swahili branding preferred; company registration and tax number for formal distributors.
- Rwanda: formal retail grows faster; small-scale distribution is more viable in secondary towns; tax compliance strictly enforced.
- East Africa: motorcycle couriers increasingly serve last-mile delivery.

## 11. Plan-section prompts

Model chosen and why; product and gap analysis; route and shop count; supplier terms; margin table with dated sources; credit policy; break-even units; downside case.
