# SACCOs and Chamas: Cooperative Finance for Business Plans

Use this reference when a business plan uses member-based finance (savings and credit cooperatives, rotating savings groups) as a funding source, or when the plan evaluates starting one. Rates and statistics are planning assumptions to be re-verified with the national cooperative regulator.

## 1. What a SACCO is
- A member-owned, member-governed financial cooperative. Members contribute shares or savings regularly; the SACCO lends to members at below-bank rates and returns surplus as dividends and interest on deposits.
- Typical mechanics: monthly contribution; loan limit as a multiple of accumulated savings (commonly around 3 to 4 times, some higher); guarantors from other members or the member's own shares as security; a flat annual or monthly interest convention.
- Governance: elected board, one member one vote, a cap on any single member's shareholding.

## 2. SACCO versus commercial bank

| Feature | SACCO | Commercial bank |
|---|---|---|
| Loan pricing | Often quoted flat and fairly stable | Tracks central bank policy; usually higher |
| Ownership | Members, equal voting | Shareholders, votes by shares |
| Collateral | Guarantors and member shares | Property or securities |
| Surplus | Distributed to members as dividends | To shareholders |
| Approval | Faster, member-knows-member | Stricter credit scoring |

Compare cost properly: a flat rate is not comparable with a reducing-balance rate. Convert both to an effective annual rate before comparing (for a flat-rate loan repaid in equal instalments, the effective rate is roughly nearly double the nominal flat rate).

## 3. Front-office services and loan products
- Deposit-taking SACCOs run a banking-like front office (cash withdrawals, ATM or agent access, mobile-money integration, salary processing, same-day loans).
- Common loan products: development (long term, capital investment), school fees, emergency (fast, small), and normal or regular loans requiring guarantors.
- Worked example: a member with a given savings balance can borrow a multiple of it; compute the maximum loan = savings x multiple, then the repayment schedule and cost.

## 4. Using a SACCO in the plan's funding mix

| Need | Use |
|---|---|
| Working capital | Emergency or short-term loan for one to three months |
| Equipment | Development loan against savings multiple |
| Start-up equity | Accumulated savings as the owner's contribution, plus a loan |
| Premises | Some SACCOs run land or property schemes |
| Cheaper debt | Refinance expensive bank debt |

Planning guideline: where the plan includes a SACCO loan, show (1) accumulated savings, (2) the loan multiple, (3) the repayment schedule and interest convention, (4) how repayments fit projected cash flow, and (5) an explicit comparison with the commercial-bank alternative. Model at least one scenario in which cooperative finance supplements bank debt to lower the weighted cost of capital. A SACCO statement of savings also serves as evidence of financial discipline when approaching other lenders.

## 5. Chamas and informal groups
- Typically 5 to 30 members contributing a fixed amount on a schedule.
- Models: rotating credit (each member receives the full pool in turn, for example monthly contributions x members), investment groups (pooled funds into property, shares or business equity), and table banking (lend pooled funds to members at interest so the pool grows).
- Informal groups need no formal registration but may register voluntarily under society or group law; growing groups often convert to a SACCO.
- Group rules to plan for: constitution, contribution and fine schedule, signatories and bank or mobile-money account controls, loan approval limits, record-keeping, dispute and exit rules, and insurance against fraud.

## 6. Starting a SACCO (planning checklist)
- Establish common bond and membership pool, feasibility of deposit volume, by-laws, registration with the cooperative registrar, whether deposit-taking status and prudential regulation apply (with capital and governance requirements), management information system, loan policy, loan-loss provisioning, and audit.
- Consult the national regulator for licensing and capital requirements; this reference does not give operating financials for running one.
- Accounting and prudential reporting: verify with the Chwezi finance engine.

## 7. Regulatory and country notes
- Kenya: non-deposit-taking SACCOs under cooperative law; deposit-taking SACCOs licensed and supervised by SASRA. Many SACCOs have opened to community membership, so a business owner may join independent of employer.
- Uganda: cooperative registrar and UMRA for regulated microfinance; SACCO loan rates commonly higher than Kenya; mobile-money-integrated products growing; community groups and rounds are widespread; development-bank SME loans are a formal alternative.
- Tanzania: Tanzania Cooperative Development Commission; village community banks (VICOBA) and similar savings groups dominate rural finance; bank-run SACCO programmes exist.
- Rwanda: Umurenge SACCOs (at least one per administrative sector) under the cooperative agency; high financial inclusion; plans may treat the local SACCO as a working-capital or equity partner.
- Loan costs differ by country and by SACCO; do not import rates across borders.

## 8. Risks
- Mismanagement or fraud by officials, liquidity shortfall when loans exceed savings, loan default and guarantor disputes, over-dependence on one employer or sector, weak record-keeping, regulatory change, and members' savings locked in shares.

## 9. KPIs
- Loan-to-savings ratio, portfolio at risk, dividend rate, member growth, share of members borrowing, cost-to-income ratio, capital adequacy where regulated.

## 10. Plan-section prompts
- Funding: how much, from which SACCO or group, at what effective cost.
- Repayment: schedule and cash-flow coverage.
- Alternatives: bank comparison and refinancing path.

Sources consulted: Mirieri, T., SACCO and Chama Business Explained (self-published, c. 2015-2016); general cooperative-finance practice.
